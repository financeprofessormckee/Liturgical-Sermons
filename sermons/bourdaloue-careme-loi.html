Voici l'accomplissement de ce grand mystère qu'annonçoit l'Apôtre aux Hébreux, lorsqu'il leur disoit, que Dieu ayant autrefois parlé à nos Peres en plusieurs maniéres différentes par ses Prophétes, il nous a enfin parlé dans ces derniers tems par son Fils même: Multifariam2, multisque modis olim Deus loquens patribus in Prophetis, novissimè locutus est nobis in Filio. C’est dans la transfiguration de Jesus-Christ, qui fait aujourd’hui le sujet de notre Evangile, que cette parole de saint Paul s’est pleinement & sensiblement vérifiée. Dieu avoit donné aux hommes sur la montagne de Sinaï une loi, dont Moyse étoit le ministre, l’interpréte, & même, selon l’expression de l’Ecriture, le Législateur. Dans la suite des tems il avoit suscité des Prophétes pour expliquer aux hommes cette loi, pour leur en faire connoître les préceptes, pour leur en reprocher la transgression, pour les y soumettre, & pour les engager soit par des menaces, soit par des promesses, à l’accomplir. Mais du reste, ni Moyse, ni les Prophètes ne furent que les précurseurs de l’Homme-Dieu; & la loi qu’ils publioient, ne fut qu’une disposition à la sainte & nouvelle loi que Jesus-Christ devoit apporter au monde. C’est pour cela qu’il paroît entre Moyse & Elie, l’un Législateur, l’autre Prophète, & qu’il y paroît tout éclatant de lumière: c’est, dis-je, pour nous apprendre que toutes les ombres de l’ancienne loi étant dissipées, que toutes les prophéties ayant reçû un parfait éclaircissement, il n’y a plus désormais que lui qui mérite d’être écouté, ni qui nous doive servir de maître. Ecoutons le donc en effet , Chrétiens , ce nouveau Législateur, & obéissons à cette voix céleste qui nous dit: Ipsum audite. Pour vous inspirer ce sentiment si juste & si nécessaire , je veux vous entretenir de la loi Chrétienne; & pour traiter dignement un si grand sujet , j’ai besoin des graces du Saint-Esprit: je les demande , &c. Ave, Maria.

English translation

Here is the fulfillment of that great mystery which the Apostle announced to the Hebrews, when he told them that God, having in times past spoken to our Fathers in many different ways through his Prophets, has at last in these latter times spoken to us through his own Son: Multifariam, multisque modis olim Deus loquens patribus in Prophetis, novissimè locutus est nobis in Filio. It is in the transfiguration of Jesus Christ, which forms today the subject of our Gospel, that this word of Saint Paul was fully and perceptibly verified. God had given to men upon the mountain of Sinai a law, of which Moses was the minister, the interpreter, and even, according to the expression of Scripture, the Lawgiver. In the course of time he had raised up Prophets to explain this law to men, to make its precepts known to them, to reproach them for its transgression, to subject them to it, and to engage them, whether by threats or by promises, to fulfill it. But for the rest, neither Moses nor the Prophets were anything but the forerunners of the God-Man; and the law which they published was nothing but a preparation for the holy and new law that Jesus Christ was to bring into the world. This is why he appears between Moses and Elijah, the one a Lawgiver, the other a Prophet, and why he appears there all radiant with light: it is, I say, to teach us that, all the shadows of the old law having been dispelled, all the prophecies having received their perfect clarification, there is from now on only he who deserves to be heard, and who must serve as our master. Let us then indeed listen, Christians, to this new Lawgiver, and let us obey this heavenly voice which says to us: Ipsum audite. To inspire in you this sentiment so just and so necessary, I wish to speak to you of the Christian law; and in order to treat worthily so great a subject, I need the graces of the Holy Spirit: I ask for them, etc. Ave, Maria.2

Je ne m'en tiens pas là; mais pour vous y attacher encore plus fortement , j’ajoûte que cette loi si sage est en même-tems de toutes les loix la plus aimable & la plus douce. C’est ma seconde proposition. Deux rapports sous lesquels nous devons considérer la loi de Jesus-Christ: rapport à l’esprit, rapport au cœur. Par rapport à l’esprit, elle n’a rien qui ne soit digne de notre estime; par rapport au cœur, elle n’a rien qui ne soit digne de notre amour. C’est ainsi que je prétens combattre deux faux principes dont les ennemis de la religion Chrétienne se sont servis de tout tems pour nous la rendre également méprisable & odieuse: méprisable, en nous persuadant qu’elle choque le bon sens & les régles de la vraie prudence; odieuse, en nous la représentant comme une loi trop dure & sans onction. Or à ces deux erreurs, j’oppose deux caractères de la loi évangélique: caractère de raison, & caractère de douceur, Loi souverainement raisonnable, vous le verrez dans le premier point. Loi souverainement aimable, je vous le montrerai dans le second point. Deux vérités importantes, qui vont faire le sujet de votre attention.

English translation

When Saint Paul says that it has pleased God to save men by the foolishness of the Gospel, Placuit Deo per stultitiam praedicationis salvos facere credentes, we must not imagine that the Christian law has, for that reason, anything contrary to true wisdom and to reason. For, according to the observation of Saint Jerome, the same Apostle, after speaking in this way, nevertheless declares that his ministry is to preach wisdom to the spiritual and the perfect: Sapientiam loquimur inter perfectos. Since I today hold the same place as the Doctor of the Nations, unworthy as I may be of it, and since I preach to you the same law which he preached to the Gentiles, I have the right, Christians, to say to you as he did—and I say it to you from the very start of this discourse—that the evangelical law of which I am about to speak to you is, of all laws, the most reasonable and the wisest: this is my first proposition. I do not stop there; but to attach you to it still more strongly, I add that this law, so wise, is at the same time, of all laws, the most lovable and the gentlest. This is my second proposition. Two aspects under which we must consider the law of Jesus Christ: a relation to the mind, a relation to the heart. In relation to the mind, it has nothing that is not worthy of our esteem; in relation to the heart, it has nothing that is not worthy of our love. It is thus that I intend to combat two false principles which the enemies of the Christian religion have at all times used to make it seem to us both contemptible and odious: contemptible, by persuading us that it offends against good sense and the rules of true prudence; odious, by representing it to us as a law too harsh and without unction. Now against these two errors, I set two characteristics of the evangelical law: a character of reason, and a character of gentleness. A sovereignly reasonable Law—you will see it in the first point. A sovereignly lovable Law—I will show it to you in the second point. Two important truths, which will form the subject of your attention.3

A prendre4 les choses en elles-mêmes, & dans les termes de ce devoir légitime, qui assujettit la créature au créateur, il ne nous appartient pas de contrôler, ni même d’examiner la loi, que Jesus-Christ nous a apportée du ciel, & qu'il est venu publier au monde. English translation

Taking things in themselves, and within the terms of that legitimate duty which binds the creature to the creator, it is not for us to criticize, nor even to examine, the law which Jesus Christ brought us from heaven, and which he came to proclaim to the world. For since the sovereigns of the earth have the power to make laws without being obliged to say why; since their will and their good pleasure suffice to authorize the orders they issue, without their subjects being able to demand any other reason for them, it is only just that we should grant at least the same privilege, and that we should render the same homage to him who is not only our Lawgiver and our Master, but our Savior and our God. What concerns us, then, is to submit ourselves to his law, and not to submit it to our censure: it is to observe his law with perfect fidelity, and not to make it the subject of discussion through a presumptuous curiosity.4

Car comme le caractère de l’esprit de l’homme est de se laisser toujours emporter à l’une ou à l’autre, & que le caractère de l’esprit de Dieu, selon la maxime de saint Grégoire Pape, consiste dans une sage modération, il est d’une conséquence presque infaillible, qu’une loi que les hommes ont osé tout à la fois condamner & d’excès & de défaut, est justement celle où se trouve ce tempérament de sagesse & de raison, qui en fait, selon la pensée du Prophéte Royal, une loi sans tache: Lex Domini7 immaculata.

English translation

And yet, Christians, it so happens that no law in the world has ever been more criticized, and as a necessary consequence, more opposed, nor more condemned, than the law of Jesus Christ; and one may say of it what the Holy Spirit in Ecclesiastes said of the world in general, that God, by a particular design, has willed, it seems, to abandon it to the disputes and contentions of men: Tradidit mundum disputationi eorum. For this law, holy and venerable as it is, has been, if I may express myself thus, since its institution, the problem of every age. The pagans, and even among Christians the libertines, following the lights of carnal prudence, have rejected it as too sublime and too far above humanity—that is, as affecting an excessive perfection well beyond the bounds that right reason prescribes. And several, on the contrary, among the heretics, prejudiced by their own opinions, have attacked it as too natural and too human—that is, as still leaving man too much liberty, and as not carrying far enough the strict and rigorous obligation of the precepts it establishes. The former have accused it of indiscretion, and the latter of laxity. Some, according to the report of St. Augustine, have complained that it committed men to a detachment from the things of the world that was fanciful and senseless: Visi sunt iis Christiani res humanas stultè & suprà quàm oportet deserere; and the others, rash and self-styled reformers, have reproached it on this very point for showing too much indulgence, and for demanding still too little. Do you know, Christians, what I should like first to infer from this? Without going any further, my conclusion would be that the Christian law is therefore a just law, a reasonable law, a law conformed to the universal rule of the Spirit of God: why? because it holds the middle between these two extremes. For as the character of man's spirit is to let itself always be carried away to one extreme or the other, and as the character of God's Spirit, according to the maxim of St. Gregory the Pope, consists in a wise moderation, it follows almost infallibly that a law which men have dared to condemn at once for excess and for defect is precisely the one in which is found that tempering of wisdom and reason which makes it, according to the thought of the Royal Prophet, a law without stain: Lex Domini immaculata.5, 6, 7

English translation

And indeed, adds St. Augustine (this remark is important), if the law of Jesus Christ had been perfectly to the liking of the pagans, it would thereby have ceased, so to speak, to be reasonable; and if the libertines approved of it, it ought thereby to be suspect to us, since it would have pleased, and would still please, vicious and corrupt men. To be what it ought to be, to be an irreproachable law, it is necessary that it not be to their taste; and the very excess which they have imputed to it is its justification. I say the same, in proportion, of the heresiarchs, prejudiced by a false zeal and swollen with vain pride: they have wished to narrow this law, already so strict; they have undertaken to reform, as Vincent of Lérins puts it, that which ought to have reformed them; and it was necessary that the Christian law, in order not to go to an unmeasured severity, and in order to remain within the limits of that reasonable worship which constitutes its essential difference, and by which St. Paul distinguishes it, should not conform to their ideas, and that they should find defects in it; so that it might be true that it has none.

English translation

If it were only a question here of making a simple apology for the duties of Christianity, I could stop there; and without saying anything more, I would believe I had sufficiently fulfilled my design: but I go further, and as much as is possible for me, I must, Christians, put you in a state to render henceforth, without contradiction, without resistance, an entire obedience to this divine master, whom God commands us to hear: Hic est filius meus dilectus: ipsum audite. I must win your affection for his law and attach you to it, and for that purpose give you all the necessary knowledge of it. Attention, if you please. I grant that it is a moderate law, and as such, proportioned to the weakness of men: but I claim that in its moderation it has nothing lax, as the spirit of heresy has imagined. Now these two truths, well conceived, effectively engage me to practice this law; they destroy all the prejudices that libertinism or self-love might form in my mind against this law; they determine me to live as a Christian, because nothing seems to me more reasonable, nor more upright, than the conduct of this law. What an advantage, both for you and for me, if we were well filled with these sentiments!

Venons au détail, & reprenons.

English translation

No, my Brothers, says Saint Chrysostom treating the same subject, the law of Jesus Christ in its perfection has nothing that ought to wound the most delicate human prudence; and to reject it as an excessive law is to do it injury and not to know it. Whether we have regard to the general obligations it imposes on all states of life, or whether we consider the particular rules it traces out for each condition, everywhere it bears with it, if I may use this term, the seal of a sovereign reason that directs it; everywhere it shows that it has emanated from the counsel of God as from its source. For indeed, continues Saint Chrysostom, what is there so singular in the Christian law that the most exquisite good sense should not approve? It obliges man to renounce himself, to mortify his mind, to crucify his flesh: it wills that he stifle his passions, that he abandon his interests, that he bear an outrage without avenging it, that he let his goods be taken from him without reclaiming them: it commands him two things in appearance most contradictory, or at least most paradoxical, the one to hate his relatives and friends, the other to love his persecutors and enemies: it makes it a crime for him to seek riches and greatness, a virtue to be humble, a beatitude to be poor, a cause for joy to be persecuted and afflicted: it regulates even his desires, even his thoughts: it commands him, on such occasion as may present itself, to tear out his eye, to cut off his arm: finally it reduces him to the very necessity of shedding his blood, of giving his life, of suffering death and the cruelest death, as soon as the honor of his religion demands it and it is a question of proving his faith. Now all this, my dear Hearers, is reasonable; and so reasonable, that if the evangelical law did not require it, however interested I might be in the matter, and whatever the corruption of my heart, I would have difficulty not condemning it. Let us come to the particulars, and take it up again.

Thus it is that the law of God is at the same time a yoke and a relief, a burden and a support. If you call it in question, I appeal not to the testimony of your own heart; for, you cannot bear witness to that of which you have no idea: I appeal to the testimony of so many saints who have experienced it, and righteous persons who do still experience it every day. How then! Hath not this law of charity turned ignominious chains into honourable bands? Witness St. Peter and St. Paul. Hath it not given irresistible attraction even to the cross? Witness St. Andrew. Hath it not afforded refrigeration in the midst of flames? Witness St. Laurence. Doth it not work miracles without end before our eyes? Is it not this that makes so many virgins undergo cheerfully all the austerities of the cloister? Is it not this that engages so many edifying penitents in a holy war against themselves, and instructs them in the method of crucifying their flesh? Is it not this that makes evangelical poverty be preferred to riches; obedience, to liberty; celibacy, to matrimony; abstinence, fasts, hair-cloths, flagellations, to all the ease and convenience of life? Have I mentioned anything, of which the world hath not afforded frequent instances? And are not these instances so many instructive lessons for you? If, therefore, (says St. Jerom,) the law seems hard, you must lay the blame of it neither on the law, nor on the difficulties which attend it, but on yourself, and on your indifference to Almighty God. The law is hard for those who fear it, and wish to enlarge the indulgence of it; whom the Spirit of God, that Spirit of grace, that Spirit of love, awakens not, enlivens not, affects not, because they are unwilling to be affected by it. Let us, however, be of good heart, and, with a holy desire of pleasing God, let us enter the blessed way of his precepts: we shall walk in it steadily, shall advance speedily, and shall come to the joys of the kingdom of heaven.
