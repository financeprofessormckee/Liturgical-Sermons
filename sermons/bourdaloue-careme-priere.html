Introduction & Scripture references

Editorial introduction

Taking the Canaanite woman’s petition in Matthew 15 as its example, Bourdaloue asks why prayers so often appear fruitless. The first part examines what is requested: desires opposed to salvation, temporal advantages placed before spiritual goods, and imagined graces that would dispense with conversion and action. The second treats the manner of prayer: humility, confidence, perseverance, attention of mind and affection of heart. Its conclusion calls for the gift of prayer itself.

English translation

If the power of prayer ever appeared visibly and in a striking manner, Christians, was it not in the example set before us by today's Gospel? There we see, to speak with Saint Ambrose, God himself surprised and filled with admiration; God confounding the powers of hell, working miracles, and displaying all his power in favor of a foreign woman who turns to him. Although she is an idolater, she serves as our model and teaches us to pray. I say, God surprised and filled with admiration : O woman, great is your faith !1 O woman, great is your faith! This is how Jesus Christ himself expresses it; and does not the faith of this Canaanite woman, and the fervor of her prayer, seem to hold something surprising and new for him? I say, God confounding the powers of hell and working miracles : what does this woman ask of him? That he heal her daughter, cruelly tormented by the demon; and with one and the same word the Son of God not only delivers the daughter, but also sanctifies the mother : Let it be done for you as you will;2 let it be done for you as you wish.

English translation

There is therefore nothing more effective with God than prayer. Yet how does it happen, my dear hearers, that day after day God shows so little favor to our petitions; that we pray and he does not hear us; that we ask and do not obtain? This is what I wish to examine today, and what will form the substance of this discourse. It is a subject of the greatest consequence, deserving particularly careful reflection. For the task, Christians, is to teach you the most excellent of all sciences, to show you how to make good use of the most powerful means of salvation, and to make known to you the priceless secret and wholly divine art of touching God's heart and drawing down upon us the most precious treasures of his grace. To receive this gift of prayer, let us employ prayer itself, and implore heaven's help through Mary's intercession. Hail, Mary.

Rien n'est plus solidement établi dans la religion & la théologie Chrétienne, que l'infaillibilité de la prière. Elle a une telle force, dit saint Jean Chrysostome, qu'elle rend, à ce qu'il semble, la parole de l'homme aussi puissante & même plus puissante que la parole de Dieu. Aussi puissante : car comme Dieu d'une parole a fait toutes choses, Dixit & facta sunt3, 4; l'homme n'a qu'à parler & à demander, tout lui est accordé : Quodcumque volueritis petetis, & fiet vobis.5 Plus puissante même en quelque sorte, puisque si Dieu se fait obéir, ce n'est que des êtres créés ; au lieu que par la vertu de la prière, tout Dieu qu'il est, il obéit, selon l'expression de l'Ecriture, à la voix de l'homme : Obediente Domino voci hominis.6 Nous entendons tous les jours des Chrétiens qui se plaignent de l'inutilité de leurs prières & du peu de fruit qu'ils en retirent : je ne m'en étonne pas. Car en quel sens disons-nous que la prière est infaillible ? nous supposons pour cela une prière sainte, une prière faite avec toutes les conditions qui la doivent accompagner, & que Dieu attend de nous, lorsque de sa part il s'engage à nous accorder tout ce que nous demanderons. Or voilà souvent ce qui manque à nos prières : Ce sont des prières défectueuses, & quant au sujet, & quant à la forme : quant au sujet, qui en fait la matière ; & quant à la forme, qui en fait la qualité : L'Apôtre saint Jacques le disoit aux fidèles de son temps, & je vous le dis à vous-mêmes : vous demandez, mes Freres, & vous ne recevez pas, parce que vous ne demandez pas bien : Petitis & non accipitis, eò quòd malè petatis.7 En effet, nous ne demandons pas à Dieu ce que Dieu veut que nous lui demandions ; défaut par rapport au sujet de la prière. Rien de plus juste que la prière qu'elle fait à Jésus-Christ : elle lui demande qu'il délivre sa fille du démon dont elle est possédée. Rien de plus engageant : elle pratique dans sa prière toutes les vertus qui peuvent gagner & intéresser le Sauveur du monde. English translation

Nothing is more firmly established in Christian religion and theology than the infallibility of prayer. Its power is such, says Saint John Chrysostom, that it seems to make the word of man as powerful as, and even more powerful than, the word of God. As powerful : for just as God made all things by a word, He spoke, and they were made,3, 4 man has only to speak and ask, and everything is granted him : You will ask whatever you wish, and it will be done for you.5 In a certain respect even more powerful : for when God makes himself obeyed, it is only by created beings; whereas through the power of prayer, God himself, God though he is, obeys the voice of man, as Scripture puts it : With the Lord obeying the voice of man.6 Every day we hear Christians complain that their prayers are useless and bear little fruit; I am not surprised. For in what sense do we say that prayer is infallible? We presuppose a holy prayer, one made with all the conditions that must accompany it and that God expects of us when, for his part, he undertakes to grant everything we ask. Yet this is often what our prayers lack. They are defective both in their object and in their form : in their object, which constitutes their matter; and in their form, which constitutes their quality. The Apostle Saint James told the faithful of his time, and I say the same to you : you ask, my brothers, and do not receive, because you do not ask rightly : You ask and do not receive, because you ask wrongly.7 Indeed, we do not ask God for what God wants us to ask him : a defect concerning the object of prayer. We do not ask him in the manner in which he wants us to ask : a defect concerning its form or quality. But let us pray like the Canaanite woman. Nothing could be more just than her prayer to Jesus Christ : she asks him to deliver her daughter from the demon who possesses her. Nothing could be more persuasive : in her prayer she practices all the virtues that can win over the Savior of the world and enlist his concern. Let us pray, I say, like this woman. Otherwise our prayers are fruitless. Why? Either because we do not ask for what we ought, which will be the first part; or because we do not ask as we ought, which will be the second. These are two lessons I must set in their fullest light. Give them your attention, Christians, and try to profit from them.

En faut-il davantage pour vous faire comprendre, pourquoi le Sauveur du monde écoute cette étrangére & lui accorde un miracle de sa toute-puissance ; & pourquoi Dieu au contraire se rend sourd à nos vœux, & rejette communément nos prières ? Appliquez-vous, Chrétiens, aux grandes vérités que ce sujet renferme, & que je vais développer comme les secrets les plus importans de votre prédestination.

English translation

It is above all on the nature of the things we ask of God that the essence of prayer depends, and consequently its merit, its efficacy, its power. It is therefore here too, says Saint Chrysostom, that we must begin to judge ourselves fairly concerning the little worth and the little effect almost all our prayers have before God; and this is the admirable lesson the Gospel of the Canaanite woman gives us at the outset. For consider, please, and allow me to express myself in this way: whereas this woman, prostrate at the feet of Jesus Christ, asks him to deliver her daughter from the demon possessing her, we, in an entirely opposite spirit, ask God every day for what maintains the reign of the demon in our souls, and even of several demons by whom we wish to be possessed. Is anything more needed to make you understand why the Savior of the world hears this foreign woman and grants her a miracle of his almighty power, and why God, on the contrary, turns a deaf ear to our wishes and commonly rejects our prayers? Attend, Christians, to the great truths this subject contains, which I shall unfold as the most important secrets of your predestination.

English translation

We ask for things harmful to salvation: the first obstacle we set against the divine mercies, which stops their course. For let us not imagine, my dear listeners, that because we are Christians by profession we are any less subject in practice to the disorders of paganism. Now one of the disorders of the pagans, if we believe the pagans themselves, was to turn to their gods and ask them—for what? For what they would not have had the impudence to ask of an upright man, what they could not have asked openly in the temples and at the foot of the altars without blushing: the death of a relative whose estate they awaited, the death of a rival whose influence or merit gave them offense, the property of a ward which they sought to seize and on which they cast covetous eyes. Such was the subject of their prayers; and, to give them more weight, they accompanied them with all the ceremonies of a superstitious cult, joined offerings and sacrifices to them, and purified themselves. This seems monstrous and senseless to us; but, Christians, in condemning them, is it not ourselves whom we condemn? Comparing their prayers with ours, are we less guilty? What am I saying? Are we not still more guilty than they were?

English translation

For, after all, they were pagans; and these pagans worshiped not only empty and false divinities, but, according to their own belief, vicious and dissolute divinities. What, then, could they more naturally ask of such divinities than what favored their vices and the corruption of their morals? Was this not an almost necessary consequence of their unbelief? This is no longer merely a disorder; it is, I dare say, impiety; it is sacrilege.

Mais si nous nous trompons nous-mêmes, nous ne trompons pas Dieu qui nous entend, & qui sçait bien discerner la malignité de nos intentions de la simplicité de nos expressions. Cependant on n'oublie rien pour intéresser le ciel & pour le toucher ; on y employe jusqu'au sacrifice, & aux prières de l'Eglise : mais parce que cette affaire qu'on poursuit avec tant de chaleur, n'est qu'une cabale, qu'une intrigue qui ne peut réussir qu'aux dépens du prochain, Dieu tuteur de l'innocent & du pauvre, rejette alors jusques au plus adorable sacrifice, jusques aux plus saintes prières de son Eglise. Ce détail me conduiroit trop loin, si j'entreprenois de lui donner toute son étendue : mais si vous voulez, mes chers Auditeurs, aller plus avant & vous l'appliquer à vous-mêmes, vous aurez bientôt reconnu, que cent fois votre cœur vous a séduits de la sorte, & fait abuser de la prière, pour porter devant Dieu même les intérêts de vos passions.

English translation

It is true, and I admit it, that in Christianity we know better how to put a favorable color on our prayers and express them in less hateful terms: for we have found the secret of disguising everything. But though we deceive ourselves, we do not deceive God, who hears us and knows well how to distinguish the malice of our intentions from the simplicity of our expressions. In vain, then, does a man of the world ask God for the means to subsist in his station and maintain his rank: since his rank, or rather the idea he forms of his rank, rests only on the principles of either boundless ambition or insatiable avarice, God, whose discernment is infinite, knows his designs and takes pleasure in making them fail. In vain does a father ask God to establish his children: since he has only profane and worldly aims for them, neither governed by conscience nor subject to the divine calling, God, without stopping at the appearance of a humble prayer, discovers its purpose; and by a just judgment, far from raising up this family, ruins it from top to bottom and lets it fall into misfortune. In vain does a woman ask God for bodily health: since her health, in the use she wishes to make of it, is to serve only her idleness, her softness, and perhaps her libertinism and disorder, God, who sees this, instead of withdrawing his arm, strikes her still harder blows and makes her lose in habitual languor everything that might nourish her self-satisfaction and flatter her vanity. In vain does a dishonest litigant ask God to win a lawsuit in which his entire fortune is involved: since this lawsuit is at bottom only a concealed injustice sustained by legal trickery, God, who cannot be ignorant of it, takes the widow's and the orphan's case against him and makes him shamefully lose all his claims. Meanwhile nothing is left undone to interest heaven and move it; even the sacrifice and the prayers of the Church are employed. But because this affair, pursued with such ardor, is only a cabal, an intrigue that can succeed only at a neighbor's expense, God, guardian of the innocent and the poor, then rejects even the most adorable sacrifice and the holiest prayers of his Church. These details would take me too far if I undertook to give them their full extent; but if you wish, my dear listeners, to go further and apply them to yourselves, you will soon recognize that your heart has deceived you in this way a hundred times and made you abuse prayer to bring even before God the interests of your passions.

The second deduction. As prayer essentially includes attention, it follows that attention is, itself, of precept in those prayers which are strictly enjoined us. So that to pronounce the words is not then sufficient, but that a remarkable and voluntary evagation of mind should be looked upon as a grievous and deadly offence. Now this, my brethren, I say both on your account and my own, for that one of the principal engagements of your profession and mine consists therein, and for that vocal prayer is a kind of attribute which the church requires of us every day. And, indeed, it were strange that this action, in itself so holy—an action that naturally leads to holiness—should serve to condemn us, and that the origin of grace should be the source of reprobation. Nor let it be forgotten, that when we submitted to the obligation of reciting the Divine Office, we bound ourselves to perform an act of religion; that an act of religion is not a practice merely external; and that as the church, by enjoining confession enjoins contrition, so she enjoins attention by enjoining prayer. Whether this obligation arises from the precept of the church immediately, as some very able divines maintain, or from the precept naturally conjoined with that of the church, in virtue of which God commands us to perform holily and worthily the thing prescribed, as others imagine: however it be, the difference is only a scholastic question; and in either opinion we equally violate the laws of religion. Ah! my brethren, let us not draw down that curse upon us with which the prophet, in the excess of his zeal, threatened the sinner, when he said: "Let his prayer become sin." And is it not probable that this is applicable to numbers of those who attend at the altar? With far greater reason ought we to accuse ourselves than the great St. Augustin, who condemned himself for negligence in this behalf.
