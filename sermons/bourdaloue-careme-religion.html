Curiosité présomptueuse, puisqu’au lieu d’engager le Fils de Dieu, par une humble priére, à leur accorder comme une grace ce qu’ils demandoient, ils parurent l’exiger, comme s’ils n’eussent eu qu’à le vouloir, pour être en droit de l’obtenir: Magister, volumus. Curiosité maligne, puisqu’en cela même ils n’avoient point d’autre dessein que de le perdre, déterminés qu’ils étoient à tourner contre lui ses miracles mêmes, dont ils lui faisoient autant de crimes, & dont enfin ils se servirent pour le calomnier & pour l’opprimer. Car de là vint que le Fils de Dieu ne leur répondit qu'avec un zéle plein de sagesse d’une part, mais de l’autre plein d’indignation; qu’il ne satisfit à leur curiosité, que pour leur reprocher au même tems leur incrédulité; qu’il les traita de nation méchante & infidéle; Generatio mala & adultera; enfin qu’il les cita devant le tribunal de Dieu, parce qu’il prévoyoit bien que le prodige qu’il alloit leur marquer, mais auquel ils ne se rendoient pas, ne serviroit qu’à les confondre: Viri Ninivitæ3 surgent in judicio adversus generationem istam.

English translation

IT was a curiosity, but a presumptuous curiosity, a captious and malicious curiosity, that led the Pharisees to make this request of the Savior of the world. A presumptuous curiosity, since instead of inducing the Son of God, by a humble prayer, to grant them as a grace what they asked for, they seemed to demand it, as if they had only to will it in order to have the right to obtain it: Magister, volumus. A captious curiosity, since, according to the account of another Evangelist, they made this proposal to him only to tempt him, and to set a trap for him: Tentantes eum, signum de cœlo quærebant. A malicious curiosity, since in this very thing they had no other design than to destroy him, determined as they were to turn even his miracles against him, which they made so many crimes of, and which they finally used to slander and oppress him. Hence it was that the Son of God answered them with a zeal full of wisdom on the one hand, but on the other full of indignation; that he satisfied their curiosity only to reproach them at the same time for their unbelief; that he called them a wicked and faithless nation; Generatio mala & adultera; and finally that he cited them before the tribunal of God, because he well foresaw that the prodigy he was about to point out to them, but to which they would not yield, would serve only to confound them: Viri Ninivitæ surgent in judicio adversus generationem istam.2, 3

Voilà, mes chers Auditeurs, le précis de notre Evangile; & dans l’exemple des Pharisiens, ce qui se passe encore tous les jours entre Dieu & nous. Je m'explique. Nous voudrions que Dieu nous fît voir des miracles, pour nous confirmer dans la foi: & Dieu nous en fait voir actuellement dont nous ne profitons pas, à quoi nous sommes insensibles, & qui par l'abus que nous en faisons, rendent notre endurcissement d'autant plus criminel qu'il est volontaire, puisqu'il ne procede aussi-bien que celui des Pharisiens, que de notre perversité & de la corruption de nos cœurs. Or c'est ce que notre divin Maître condamne aujourd'hui dans ces prétendus esprits forts du Judaïsme, & ce qui doit, si nous tombons dans leur infidélité, nous condamner nous mêmes. Tertullien a dit un beau mot, & qui exprime parfaitement le caractére de la profession chrétienne; sçavoir, qu'après Jesus-Christ la curiosité n'est plus pour nous de nul usage, & que désormais elle ne nous peut plus être utile; beaucoup moins, nécessaire: parce que depuis la prédication de l'Evangile le seul parti qui nous reste, est celui de croire, & de soumettre notre raison, en la captivant sous le joug de la foi. Nobis curiositate4 opus non est post Christum, nec inquisitione post Evangelium. C'est ainsi qu'il s'en expliquoit. Mais pour moi j'ose enchérir sur sa pensée, & j'ajoute que quand il nous seroit permis dans le Christianisme de faire de nouvelles recherches, quand nous aurions droit de raisonner sur notre foi & sur les mystères qu’elle nous révèle, nous trouvons dans Jesus-Christ & dans son Evangile, non-seulement de quoi convaincre nos esprits, mais de quoi contenter pleinement notre curiosité: pourquoi? parce que Jesus-Christ nous a fait voir dans sa personne des prodiges si éclatans & d’une telle évidence, que nul esprit raisonnable n’y peut résister; & que si nous n’en sommes pas touchés, ce ne peut être que l’effet d’une mauvaise disposition, dont nous serons responsables à Dieu, & qui ne suffira que trop pour attirer sur nous toutes les rigueurs de son jugement.

English translation

Such, my dear Hearers, is the summary of our Gospel; and in the example of the Pharisees, what still happens every day between God and us. Let me explain. We would like God to show us miracles, to confirm us in the faith: and God does actually show us some, of which we take no advantage, to which we are insensible, and which, through the abuse we make of them, render our hardening of heart all the more criminal because it is voluntary, since it proceeds, just as did that of the Pharisees, only from our perversity and the corruption of our hearts. Now this is what our divine Master condemns today in those so-called freethinkers of Judaism, and what must, if we fall into their unbelief, condemn us as well. Tertullian uttered a fine saying, which perfectly expresses the character of the Christian profession; namely, that after Jesus Christ curiosity is of no further use to us, and that henceforth it can no longer be useful to us, much less necessary: because since the preaching of the Gospel the only course left to us is to believe, and to submit our reason, holding it captive under the yoke of faith. Nobis curiositate opus non est post Christum, nec inquisitione post Evangelium. Thus did he express himself. But for my part, I dare to go further than his thought, and I add that even if it were permitted us in Christianity to make new inquiries, even if we had the right to reason about our faith and about the mysteries it reveals to us, we find in Jesus Christ and in his Gospel not only enough to convince our minds, but enough to fully satisfy our curiosity: why? because Jesus Christ has shown us in his own person prodigies so brilliant and of such evidence, that no reasonable mind can resist them; and that if we are not moved by them, it can only be the effect of a bad disposition, for which we shall be answerable to God, and which will be more than enough to draw down upon us all the severities of his judgment.4

English translation

This is the important matter that I have undertaken to treat in this discourse. And can I do it with greater advantage, Madam, than in the presence of Your Majesty, whose sentiments and examples must be, for this whole assembly, so many tangible and convincing proofs of what I wish today to persuade them of? For what more marvelous effect can the Christian Religion have, than to sanctify, in the midst of the Court and even upon the Throne, the greatest Queen in the world; and should not this alone already lead us to conclude that this Religion is necessarily the work of God and not of men? Ce n’est pas sans raison, que les Pharisiens de notre Evangile dans le dessein, quoique peu sincére, de connoître Jesus-Christ, & de sçavoir s'il étoit Fils de Dieu, lui demandèrent un prodige qui vînt de lui, & dont il fût l'auteur: Magister, volumus à te signum videre. Car il faut convenir, dit S. Faites-nous voir un prodige qui vienne de vous, disent les Pharisiens à Jesus-Christ. Que fait ce Sauveur adorable? Ecoutez-moi: en ceci consiste tout le fonds de cette instruction. De ces deux genres de prodiges ainsi distingués, il leur en fait voir un, qui n'avoit pû venir que de Dieu, & qui fut un miracle évident & incontestable; je veux dire, la foi des Ninivites convertis par la prédication de Jonas. Mais au même tems il leur en découvre un autre bien opposé, & qui ne pouvoit venir que d'eux-mêmes, sçavoir, le prodige ou le désordre de leur infidélité. Or nous n'avons, mes chers Auditeurs, qu'à nous appliquer ces deux sortes de prodiges, pour nous reconnoître aujourd'hui dans la personne de ces Pharisiens; & pour être obligés par la comparaison que nous ferons de leur état & du nôtre, d'avouer que le reproche du Fils de Dieu ne nous convient peut-être pas moins qu'à ces faux Docteurs de la loi; que dans le sens qu'il l'entendoit, peut-être ne sommes-nous pas moins qu'eux une nation corrompue & adultére; & qu'il pourroit avec autant de raison nous appeller à ce jugement redoutable, où il les cita en leur adressant ces paroles: Viri Ninivitæ surgent in judicio cum generatione ista.

English translation

It was not without reason that the Pharisees of our Gospel, in a design, though scarcely sincere, to know Jesus Christ and to learn whether he was the Son of God, asked him for a sign that should come from him, and of which he would be the author: Magister, volumus à te signum videre. For it must be granted, says St. Augustine, that there are prodigies of two different kinds; the first which come from God, and the second which come from man: the one kind which excite admiration, because they are the visible testimonies of the absolute power of the Creator; and the other which cause only horror, because they are the sorry effects of the disorder of the creature: those which we revere, and which we call miracles; and these which we regard as monsters in the order of grace. Show us a sign that comes from you, say the Pharisees to Jesus Christ. What does this adorable Savior do? Listen to me: in this consists the whole substance of this instruction. Of these two kinds of prodigies thus distinguished, he shows them one which could only have come from God, and which was an evident and incontestable miracle; I mean, the faith of the Ninevites converted by the preaching of Jonah. But at the same time he reveals to them another, quite opposite, which could only come from themselves, namely, the prodigy or the disorder of their unbelief. Now, my dear Hearers, we have only to apply these two kinds of prodigies to ourselves, in order to recognize ourselves today in the person of these Pharisees; and to be obliged, by the comparison we shall make of their state and ours, to admit that the reproach of the Son of God applies to us perhaps no less than to these false Doctors of the law; that, in the sense in which he meant it, we are perhaps no less than they a corrupt and adulterous nation; and that he might with as much reason summon us to that fearful judgment to which he summoned them, addressing to them these words: Viri Ninivitæ surgent in judicio cum generatione ista.

English translation

For I maintain—and here, in two propositions, is the division of this discourse; understand them well—I maintain that Jesus Christ, in establishing his Religion, showed us a miracle more authentic and more convincing than that of the converted Ninevites, and this is the great miracle of the conversion of the world and the spread of the Gospel, which I call the miracle of faith: this shall be the first point. I maintain that we daily set against this miracle a prodigy of unbelief, but an unbelief far more monstrous and more condemnable than that of the Pharisees themselves: this shall be the second point. Two prodigies, let me say again, one supernatural and divine—the world sanctified by the preaching of the Gospel—the other all too natural and all too human, yet a prodigy nonetheless—the disorder of our unbelief. Two grounds of condemnation that God will bring against us at his judgment, if we do not take care to forestall him by judging ourselves even now. Miracle of faith: prodigy of unbelief. Miracle of faith, which God has made perceptible to us, and which we have continually before our eyes. Prodigy of unbelief, which we take no care to guard against, and which we keep hidden in our hearts. Miracle of faith, which will fill you with a salutary shame, by making known to you the excellence and grandeur of your Religion. Prodigy of unbelief, which perhaps, if you do not take heed, after having been the source of your corruption, will become the ground of your eternal reprobation. Both the one and the other demand particular attention.

English translation

It is fitting then, Christians, in order first to enter into the thought of Jesus Christ, and into the essential point which I must now unfold, to form a proper conception of this great miracle of the conversion of the world, and of the establishment of Christianity, which I regard, following Saint Jerome, as the miracle of faith. And because it is beyond doubt that this miracle must be one of the most invincible proofs that God will employ against us, if ever he condemns us, we must today, you and I, form of it an idea capable of awakening in our hearts the liveliest sentiments of Religion. Here it is.7

Jonas fugitif, mais malgré sa fuite ne pouvant se dérober au pouvoir du Dieu qui l'envoie, confus, & touché de repentir, reçoit de la part du Seigneur un nouvel ordre d'aller à Ninive. Voilà, disoit le Fils de Dieu aux Juifs incrédules, le miracle qui vous condamnera & qui confondra votre impénitence: & je dis à tout ce qu’il y a de Chrétiens endurcis dans leur libertinage, Voilà le miracle que le Saint-Esprit vous propose comme la figure d’un autre miracle encore plus étonnant, encore plus au-dessus de l’homme, encore plus capable de vous convaincre & de vous élever à Dieu. Ecoutez-le sans prévention, & vous en conviendrez.

English translation

Jonah, a fugitive, yet unable, despite his flight, to escape the power of the God who sends him, ashamed and moved by repentance, receives from the Lord a new command to go to Nineveh. He goes there: though a stranger, though unknown, he preaches there, and declares himself sent by God. He threatens that great city and all its inhabitants with utter and imminent destruction. No other term than forty days, no other proof of his prediction than the prediction itself which he makes: etc. At his word, this people given over to every vice, this people for whom, it seems, there was no longer either God or Law, this people unresponsive to the warnings and lessons of all the other Prophets, by a change wrought by the hand of the Most High, listens to this one and listens with respect, comes back to itself, and sets about to appease the wrath of God, performs the most austere and most exemplary penance; neither state, nor age, nor sex is exempted; even the King, says Scripture, to weep and to humble himself, comes down from his Throne; children are included in the law of fasting ordained by the Prince; each one clothed in sackcloth and covered with ashes gives every sign of an effective and prompt sorrow. In the end the reformation of morals is so general that the prophecy is fulfilled to the letter, Et Ninive subvertetur [And Nineveh shall be overthrown], since, according to Saint Chrysostom's fine reflection, it is no longer that dissolute Nineveh which God held in abomination, but an entirely new and entirely holy Nineveh, built upon the ruins of the first—and by whom? by the ministry of a single man who spoke, and who, full of the spirit of God, sanctified thousands of men whose hearts he broke. Behold, said the Son of God to the unbelieving Jews, the miracle that will condemn you and confound your impenitence: and I say to all those Christians hardened in their licentiousness, Behold the miracle that the Holy Spirit sets before you as the figure of another miracle still more astonishing, still more beyond the power of man, still more capable of convincing you and raising you up to God. Hear it without prejudice, and you will agree.8

Some raise admiration, being visible testimonies of the absolute power and will of the Creator; others cause horror, being the melancholy effects of the viciousness of the creature. Those we revere, and denominate miracles; these we deem monsters, in the order of grace. Let us see a prodigy (say the Pharisees to Jesus) which comes from yourself. But what doth this adorable Saviour do? Be attentive, I pray you; for herein is placed the ground-work on which this instruction is raised. 3rdly. But not to insist any longer on this, let us conclude, Christians, with the last prodigy, which concerns ourselves, and which is neither a renouncing to faith, nor a corruption of faith, but a dreadful contrariety between our life and our faith. My meaning is this: we are Christians by profession, and live like pagans; we have faith in theory, and in practice our conduct is nothing but infidelity; we have one mode of belief, and another of action. In every other respect, our actions and affections are conformable to our opinions and knowledge of things; for we love and hate, we shun and seek, we undergo and undertake, according to the insight we have into them. In nothing but what concerns the salvation of our souls do we act perversely. We shun what we judge is our sovereign good, and we seek what we judge is our sovereign evil. We profane the thing which we know to be adorable, and idolize that which we heartily despise. We abhor what would save us, and cherish what destroys us. Were we real, and not merely nominal Christians, and lived agreeably to the faith we profess, our life, it is true, would be a continual miracle, but yet in that there would be nothing prodigious. Were we pagans by profession, and destitute of faith, and lived in conformity to the flesh and the senses, however lost we might be to all hope, in such irregularity there would be nothing unnatural. But the prodigy is, that we want no faith, yet live like infidels; a prodigy which the faithless will not admit, ridiculously pretending that life and creed have an interchange of influence, and that we live as we believe, and believe as we live, in order to attribute their irregular demeanour to their want of conviction, and not to the perverse disposition of their hearts. But to remove this error would be no hard matter, as to possess faith, and to act against it, is not more difficult than to possess reason, and to act against it. Now, is not this, by their own confession, what they do every day?

Ah! Christians, let us stop the progress of these prodigies. Let us be consistent with ourselves. Let our morals and our faith keep equal pace. Otherwise what have we not to fear on account of this faith so profaned, this faith so scandalized, this faith so dishonoured? Let us make it instrumental to a sincere repentance, if we have forsaken its ways. Let us make it instrumental to our perseverance, if we are already penitent, or have never swerved from our pious resolutions. Let us go on with the help of these divine lights, and let us not put them out by delivering ourselves up to our blind passions, and carnal appetites; for, nothing more exposes us to lose the faith, than the indulging in a sensual and voluptuous life. By these means it was, that thousands of reprobates lost it; and by these means it is, that they still persist in their libertine ways. Ah! my Lord, you have many chastisements in the treasures of your justice, by which you may inflict due punishment for our trespasses. Strike, my God! and if in your wisdom you should find it necessary to afflict us with all temporal calamities, pour your vengeance on our heads; but preserve our faith. Nor is this enough. Enliven and rouse this drooping faith, this expiring faith, nay, this faith dead without good works. In the same degree that it shall live in us, we shall live by it, and in the end it will lead us to everlasting life.
