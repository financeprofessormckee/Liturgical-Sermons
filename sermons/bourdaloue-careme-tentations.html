French original

N'est-il pas étonnant que le Fils de Dieu, qui n'est descendu sur la terre, comme dit saint Jean, que pour détruire les œuvres du démon, ait voulu les éprouver lui-même, & se voir exposé aux attaques de cet esprit tentateur? Mais quatre grandes raisons, remarque saint Augustin, l'y ont engagé, & toutes sont prises de notre intérêt. Nous étions trop fragiles & trop foibles pour soutenir la tentation, & il a voulu nous fortifier. Nous étions trop timides & trop lâches, & il a voulu nous encourager. Nous étions trop imprudens & trop téméraires, & il a voulu nous apprendre à nous précautionner. Nous étions sans expérience & trop peu versés dans l’art de combattre notre commun ennemi, & il a voulu nous l'enseigner.

English translation

Is it not astonishing that the Son of God, who came down to earth, as Saint John says, only to destroy the works of the devil, should have wished to experience them himself, and to see himself exposed to the attacks of this tempting spirit? But four great reasons, Saint Augustine observes, led him to do this, and all are taken from our own interest. We were too fragile and too weak to withstand temptation, and he wished to strengthen us. We were too timid and too cowardly, and he wished to encourage us. We were too imprudent and too rash, and he wished to teach us to take precautions. We were without experience and too little versed in the art of fighting our common enemy, and he wished to teach it to us.

English translation

Now this is what he does admirably today. For according to the thought and expression of Saint Gregory, he has made us stronger, by overcoming our temptations through his own temptations, just as by his death he overcame our death: Justum quippe erat, ut tentatus nostras tentationes suis vinceret, quemadmodum mortem nostram venerat suâ morte superare. He has made us more courageous and more bold, by animating us with his example, since indeed nothing ought to animate us more than the example of a God-Man, our sovereign High Priest, tried as we are in every way, according to the word of Saint Paul: Tentatum autem per omnia. He has made us more circumspect and more watchful, by making us know that no one ought to hold himself secure, when he himself, the Holy of Holies, is not sheltered from temptation. Finally he has made us more skilled and more intelligent, by showing us what weapons we must use to defend ourselves, and by tracing out for us the rules of this spiritual warfare.2, 3

English translation

In this he is like a great King, who, to repel the enemies of his State, and to break up their alliances, is not content merely to raise troops and give orders; but is the first to appear at the head of his armies, sustains them by his presence, leads them by his wisdom, animates them by his valor, and always, despite obstacles and perils, secures victory for them. Now if the example of a king has so much force and so much power, as you know, Christians, and as you yourselves have so often acknowledged, what must the example of a God do? Here, without doubt, is one of the most important subjects I could treat from the pulpit, and one that demands more reflection. Among so many excellent lessons that Jesus Christ gives us in today's Gospel, concerning the manner in which we ought to conduct ourselves in temptation, I choose two on which I will dwell, and which the words of my text furnish me. The first is, that this divine Master goes into the desert, where he is tempted, only by the inspiration of the Spirit of God: Ductus est in desertum à spiritu, ut tentaretur. The second, that he is not tempted there until he had armed himself beforehand with fasting and the mortification of the senses: Et cùm jejunasset quadraginta diebus & quadraginta noctibus, accessit tentator. From this I will draw two conclusions, both very useful and very necessary. Let us ask, etc. Ave, Maria.

English translation

In whatever manner God may have disposed, in the counsel of his wisdom, concerning what pertains to this preparation of graces, which Saint Augustine calls predestination, three things are evident and incontestable in the principles of the faith; namely, that to conquer temptation, the help of grace is necessary; that there is no temptation that cannot be overcome by grace; and that God, finally, by a bond of faithfulness, never fails to strengthen us with his grace in temptation.

Car, dit-il, en parlant aux fidéles, celui qui est en vous par sa grace, est bien plus fort que celui qui est dans le monde, & qui y regne en qualité de Prince du monde: Vicistis eum6, quoniam major est qui in vobis est, quàm qui in mundo. C’est dans la pensée de S. Bernard, une parole d’infidélité encore plus que de foiblesse: pourquoi? parce qu’en parlant ainsi, ou nous n’avons égard qu’à nos propres forces, & en ce sens la proposition est vraie, mais nous sommes infidéles de séparer nos forces de celles de Dieu; ou nous supposons la grace & le secours de Dieu, & en ce sens la proposition non-seulement est fausse, mais hérétique, parce qu’il est de la foi qu’avec le secours de Dieu nous pouvons tout: Omnia possum7 in eo qui me confortat.

English translation

There is no temptation that cannot be overcome by grace: another essential maxim of religion; and the beloved disciple Saint John gives an excellent reason for it. For, speaking to the faithful, he says that he who is in you by his grace is far stronger than he who is in the world, and who reigns there as Prince of the world: Vicistis eum, quoniam major est qui in vobis est, quàm qui in mundo. It is therefore an insult to God to believe that temptation is insurmountable, and to say what we nevertheless say so often: I cannot resist such a passion; I cannot hold out against such a habit, such an inclination. This is, in the thought of Saint Bernard, a word of unbelief even more than of weakness. Why? Because in speaking thus, either we have regard only to our own strength—and in that sense the statement is true, but we are unfaithful in separating our strength from God's—or else we presuppose grace and God's help, and in that sense the statement is not only false but heretical, because it is a matter of faith that with God's help we can do all things: Omnia possum in eo qui me confortat.6, 7

French original

Mais avons-nous toujours ce secours de Dieu dans la tentation? c’est ce qui me reste à vous expliquer & ce qui doit faire le fonds de ce discours, où j’ose dire que sans embarrasser vos esprits, & sans rien avancer dont vous ne soyez édifiés, je vais vous donner l’éclaircissement de ce qu’il y a de plus important & de plus solide dans la matiére de la grace. Oui, Chrétiens, il est encore de la foi, que Dieu ne permet jamais que nous soyons tentés au-delà de ce que nous pouvons. Fidelis Deus8, qui non patietur vos tentari supra id quod potestis. Or nous n’avons ce pouvoir que par la grace. Elle ne nous manque donc point du côté de Dieu, non-seulement pour vaincre la tentation, mais pour en profiter: Sed faciet cum tentatione9 proventum. Voilà comment parle saint Paul, & de quoi nous ne pouvons douter, si nous ne sommes pas assez aveugles pour nous figurer un Dieu sans miséricorde & sans providence. Mais quoique cela soit ainsi, il y a pourtant une erreur qui n’est aujourd’hui que trop commune, & qui se découvre dans la conduite de la plupart des hommes: c’est de croire que ces graces nous sont toujours données telles que nous les voulons, & au moment que nous les voulons. Erreur dont les conséquences sont très-pernicieuses, & dont j’ai crû qu’il étoit important de vous détromper. Pour vous faire entendre mon dessein, je distingue deux sortes de tentations; les unes volontaires, & les autres involontaires. Les unes où nous nous engageons de nous-mêmes contre l'ordre de Dieu; & les autres où nous nous trouvons engagés par une espéce de nécessité attachée à notre condition. Dans les premiéres, je dis que nous ne devons point espérer d'être secourus de Dieu, si nous ne sortons de l'occasion; & que pour cela nous ne devons point alors nous promettre une grace de combat, mais une grace de fuite; ce sera la premiére partie. Dans les autres, je prétens qu'en vain nous aurons une grace de combat, si nous ne sommes en effet résolus à combattre nous-mêmes, & sur-tout comme Jesus-Christ par la mortification de la chair: ce sera la seconde partie. Toutes deux renferment de solides instructions.

English translation

But do we always have this help of God in temptation? That is what remains for me to explain to you, and what must form the substance of this discourse, in which I dare say that, without confusing your minds, and without putting forward anything that will not edify you, I am going to give you clarification on what is most important and most solid in the matter of grace. Yes, Christians, it is likewise a matter of faith that God never permits us to be tempted beyond what we are able to bear. Fidelis Deus, qui non patietur vos tentari supra id quod potestis. Now we have this power only through grace. Grace, therefore, is never lacking on God's part, not only to overcome temptation, but to profit from it: Sed faciet cum tentatione proventum. This is how Saint Paul speaks, and of this we cannot doubt, unless we are blind enough to imagine a God without mercy and without providence. But although this is so, there is nevertheless an error which today is only too common, and which is revealed in the conduct of most men: it is to believe that these graces are always given to us exactly as we want them, and at the moment we want them. This is an error whose consequences are very pernicious, and which I thought it important to correct in your minds. To make my purpose clear, I distinguish two kinds of temptation: some voluntary, others involuntary. Some in which we engage ourselves of our own accord against God's order; and others in which we find ourselves engaged by a kind of necessity attached to our condition. In the first case, I say that we must not hope to be helped by God if we do not withdraw from the occasion; and that for this reason we must not then promise ourselves a grace of combat, but a grace of flight; this will be the first part. In the second case, I maintain that it will be in vain for us to have a grace of combat, if we are not in fact resolved to fight ourselves, and above all, as Jesus Christ did, through the mortification of the flesh: this will be the second part. Both parts contain solid instruction.8, 9

English translation

Whatever obligation we may be under—and indeed sometimes are—to risk our life, it is an incontestable truth, founded on the first law of the charity we owe to ourselves, that it is never permitted to us to risk our salvation. Now it is evident that we do risk it, and consequently that we sin, as often as we rashly engage ourselves in temptation. Let me explain. There is no one who does not have, both within himself and outside himself, sources of temptation peculiar to him: within himself, passions and habits; outside himself, objects and occasions against which he must personally guard, and which are, in relation to him, principles of sin. For one may very well say of temptation what Saint Paul said of grace: that just as there is a diversity of graces and inspirations, all proceeding from the same spirit of holiness, which God, working in us, employs—though differently—to convert and save us; so too there is a diversity of temptations which the same spirit of iniquity raises up against us, in order to corrupt and ruin us. We know well enough what is the weak point by which he most commonly attacks us; and with but a little attention to our own conduct, we can easily distinguish not only the temptation that predominates in us, but also the circumstances that make it more dangerous for us. For, as Saint Chrysostom observes, what is a temptation for one man is not a temptation for another; what is an occasion of falling for this man may pose no danger at all to that man; and one person will not be troubled or shaken by the greatest scandals in the world, while a mere trifle, if I may call it so, will unhappily cause him to fall, because of the particular disposition in which he finds himself. To know this, and not to flee the danger, is what I call exposing oneself to temptation against the order of God. Now I maintain that a Christian who does this ought not to expect from God the helps of grace prepared for fighting temptation and overcoming it. I maintain that he has no right to ask God for them, nor even to hope for them. I go further, and do not fear to add that even were he to ask for them, God, according to the ordinary course of his providence, is expressly determined to refuse them to him. What stronger thing can I say, to show these presumptuous souls the disorder of their conduct, and to bring them back into the holy paths of the prudence of the just?10

English translation

No, Christians, any man who rashly and against the order of God engages himself in temptation must not count on those graces of protection and defense, those graces of resistance and combat, so necessary to sustain us. By what title could he claim them, or ask them of God? By title of justice? They would no longer be graces, no longer gifts of God, if God owed them to him. By title of fidelity? God has never promised them to him. By title of mercy? By his own presumption he sets up a voluntary obstacle to it; he renders himself absolutely unworthy of divine mercies. There he stands, then, so long as he remains in this state, and wills to remain in it, without resource from God, and deprived of all his rights to grace—I mean to that grace of which Saint Augustine speaks, and which he calls victorious, because it is by this grace that we triumph over temptation.

English translation

I say more: not only can man not presume, in that state, that God will give him this victorious grace; he must even be assured that God will not give it to him. Why? Because God himself has declared this, and there is no truth more clearly marked in Scripture than this one, namely, that to punish the rashness of the sinner, God abandons him and delivers him over to the corruption of his desires. And do not tell me that God is faithful, and that the faithfulness of God, according to Saint Paul, consists in not permitting that we should ever be tempted beyond our strength. God is faithful: I grant it. But these are two very different things: not permitting that we be tempted beyond our strength, and giving us whatever strength we please when we ourselves engage in temptation. The one is not a consequence of the other; and without prejudice to his faithfulness, God can indeed refuse us what we have no reason to hope for. Whatever obligation we may sometimes be under, as in fact we are, of risking our lives, it is an incontestable truth, founded in that primary law of charity which we owe to ourselves, that we are never permitted to venture our salvation. Now, it is evident that we do so, and therefore, that we sin as often as we rashly engage in temptation. For with great propriety we may say of temptation, what St. Paul said of grace: that as there is in us a diversity of graces and inspirations, all which proceed from one and the same spirit of sanctity, and which Almighty God, who operates in us, makes use of, though differently, in order to convert us and bring us to salvation; so also there is in us a diversity of temptations, which one and the same spirit of iniquity raises up against us, in order to corrupt us, and lead us to perdition. We are sufficiently apprised what that foible is, through which he mostly, and most vehemently attacks us; and if we give but ever so little attention to our own conduct, we easily discern, not only the temptation which predominates in us, but the particular circumstances which enhance the danger of it. For (as St. Chrysostom judiciously remarks) what is temptation for one man, is not so for another; what may be the occasion of one man’s fall, may be no way dangerous with regard to another; and many there are, who, though firm and unshaken amidst the most enormous scandals in the world, yet, on account of the particular disposition they are in, unhappily miscarry for (if I may so call it) the veriest trifle. To know the danger, and not to fly from it, is what I call exposing one’s self, against the order of God, to temptation. Now I maintain, that a Christian should not then expect the assistance of those graces, which are given him to resist and overcome temptation. I maintain that he hath no right to petition, or even to hope for them. I proceed still farther, nor am I afraid to add, that although he should petition for them, God, in the ordinary course of his Providence, is expressly determined to give him a denial. How can I address myself in stronger terms to those presumptuous souls, in order to let them see the irregularity of their conduct, and induce them to come back into the prudential and holy ways of righteousness?

A. Carroll · 1843

Thus reasoned the fathers, who were given us as teachers by the divine Providence, and ought to be our guides in the way of salvation. Be not, therefore, surprised, that worldlings who walk (as the apostle speaks) according to the flesh, and are enemies to the cross and mortification of Jesus, are so easily overcome in the hour of temptation. Ask me not, whence comes it that they resist so seldom, that they yield so easily, that they rise with such difficulty? These are the consequences of their delicacy and sensuality; and if souls like these, who idolize their bodies, were not swayed by concupiscence, it would be the greatest of miracles in the order of grace. No, no; (says Tertullian, speaking to Christians who were persecuted in the primitive times of the Christian era;) I shall never believe that those whose flesh is nourished in pleasure, have the necessary qualifications to enter the lists with torments and death. Whatever ardour a Christian may show for the cause of God, and in defence of his faith, I shall always diffide in, or rather despair of, his willingness to exchange the delicacy of banquets, of sumptuous apparel, of gay equipages, of a numerous train, for the rigour of dungeons, of racks, of wheels, of scorpion-whips. A champion, in order to get strength for the fight, must previously abstain from sensual pleasures, and be constantly inured to toilsome occupations; for these are the means of acquiring strength. In like manner, the man who prepares for the engagement to which his religion calls him, must make trial of himself by mortification, which will put him in a condition to undergo everything, and be dismayed at nothing. Now what Tertullian said with regard to persecutions, which were the public and external temptations of Christianity, I, with equal reason, apply to the internal and particular temptations of all the faithful. It is God's grace that must overcome them. But in vain do we presume that grace, though all powerful, will put them to flight, if the flesh which they proceed from be not curbed and kept under. And whoever thinks otherwise is grossly mistaken, and goes astray.

But in what doth this mortification of the flesh consist? And to what is this exercise (according to the practice of the world) reduced? The third and last consequence. Ah! beloved hearers, give me leave to wave mentioning what this virtue is, according to the general practice of the world; a virtue scarce known in it, exposed to derision, and held in abhorrence. But whatever notions the world may have of it, the apostolic oracle still subsists, that to serve Jesus Christ, and preserve in his service an inviolable fidelity, we must crucify our flesh, and die to our passions and irregular desires, because “ they that are Christ’s, have crucified their flesh with their vices and lusts.” Gal. v. It will always be true, (whatever may be the opinion of the world,) that among all mankind, there is no condition in which this crucifying of the flesh is not necessary, absolutely necessary, because all mankind are exposed to temptation. Whatever difficulty the world may have to join issue with me, the sole experience of its own irregularities sufficiently demonstrates, that the more exalted men's stations are, the more frequent ought to be the mortification of their senses, as temptations in exalted stations are most general and most violent. Whatever thoughts the world may conceive of it, at least it must grant, that the more a sinner lies open to temptation, the more strictly and rigorously it is incumbent on him to observe this law of bodily chastisement. Were we, indeed, such Christians as we ought to be, these gospel rules, however general, would be more than sufficient to make us know our duty. But being overruled by self-love, and our self-indulgence being so excessive, scarce can we take up a settled resolution to inflict any voluntary pains on ourselves, though ever so slight. What then doth the church do? This general command she hath determined and reduced to a particular obligation, the Lenten fast.
