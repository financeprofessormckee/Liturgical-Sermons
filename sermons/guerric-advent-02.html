Editorial introduction

This second Advent sermon belongs to Guerric of Igny's medieval collection of sermons for the liturgical year, here read from the 1879 edition of Patrologia Latina. Its individual date is unknown; the collection is associated approximately with his abbacy in 1138–1157. Guerric urges his hearers to run out with joy to meet the coming Christ, as the prophets and the just longed to see him. He then describes a hidden, spiritual coming between the first and the last, in which the soul is transformed into Christ's image. Until that is granted, faith and a pure conscience console the patient. The source names Advent without assigning a particular Sunday.

Quomodo Christo advenienti occurrendum

English translation

How to Go Out to Meet the Coming Christ

¶cols. 13-14; cols. 15-16
Latin original

1. Ecce venit Rex, occurramus obviam Salvatori nostro. Salomon pulchre ait: Aqua frigida animae sitienti, et nuntius bonus de terra longinqua (Prov. XXV, 25). Bonus itaque nuntius, qui adventum Salvatoris nuntiat, reconciliationem mundi, bona superventuri saeculi. Quam pulchri pedes annuntiantium pacem, annuntiantium bona! (Isa, LII, 7.) Multi siquidem, non unus. Multi inquam, sed in uno spiritu longa serie ab initio saeculi nobis supervenere nuntii, et omnium vox una, simul una sententia: Venit ecce venit (Ezech. XXXIX, 8). Et unde, inquis, isti venere nuntii? Ut scriptum est, de terra longinqua (Isa. XXXIX, 3); quia de terra viventium, quae magno interstitio separata est ab hac terra morientium: nam inter nos et illos adhuc chaos magnum firmatum est. Inde tamen prophetae nobis, sicut et angeli missi sunt; quia si corpore hic versabantur, spiritu illuc assumebantur, quando mittendi erant; ut illic audirent et viderent, quod hic nuntiarent. Hujusmodi nuntii aqua refrigerii, potusque sapientiae sunt salutaris animae Deum sitienti: cui nimirum qui nuntiat adventum vel alia mysteria Salvatoris, haurit ei, et propinat aquas in gaudio de fontibus Salvatoris, ut etiam nuntianti sibi, sive Isaiae, sive cuilibet ex prophetis, eadem anima respondere videatur verbis Elizabeth, quia eodem potata spiritu quo Elizabeth: Et unde mihi hoc, ut veniat Dominus meus ad me? (Luc. I, 34, 43.) Ecce enim, ut facta est vox annuntiationis tuae in auribus meis, exsultavit in gaudio spiritus in corde meo, gestiens occurrere Deo Salvatori suo.

English translation

1. Behold, the King is coming; let us go out to meet our Savior. Solomon says beautifully: “Cold water to a thirsty soul, and good news from a distant land” (Prov. XXV, 25). Good, then, is the messenger who announces the Savior’s coming, the reconciliation of the world, and the blessings of the age that is to come. “How beautiful are the feet of those who announce peace, who announce good things!” (Isa, LII, 7.) There are many of them, not just one. Many, I say, but in one spirit, in a long succession from the beginning of the world, messengers have come to us, and the voice of them all is one, their judgment together one: “Behold, he comes, yes, he comes” (Ezech. XXXIX, 8). And where, you ask, did these messengers come from? As it is written, “from a distant land” (Isa. XXXIX, 3); for they came from the land of the living, which is separated by a great interval from this land of the dying—for between us and them a great chasm is still fixed. Yet from there the prophets, like the angels, were sent to us; for although they were living here in body, in spirit they were taken there whenever they were to be sent, so that there they might hear and see what they were to announce here. Such messengers are water of refreshment and a drink of saving wisdom for the soul thirsting for God. Whoever announces to it the Savior’s coming or any of his other mysteries draws and offers it water with joy from the fountains of the Savior, so that the same soul seems to answer the one announcing it—whether Isaiah or any other of the prophets—with Elizabeth’s words, because it has been given to drink of the same Spirit as Elizabeth: “And how has this happened to me, that my Lord should come to me?” (Luc. I, 34, 43.) For behold, as the voice of your announcement sounded in my ears, the spirit in my heart leaped for joy, eager to go out to meet God its Savior.

2. Et revera, fratres, exsultatione spiritus Christo venienti occurrendum est. Et jam nunc a longe salutandus, vel certe resalutandus, qui mandat salutes Jacob. Amicum salutare non confundar (Eccli. XXII, 31), inquit Sapiens; quanto magis resalutare? O salutare vultus mei, et Deus meus! quanta dignatio fuit quod servos salutasti? sed quanto major, quod salvasti? Nec enim integra nobis salus esset, si salutes mandares, et non dares. Dedisti autem, non modo, quos salutaveras verbis pacificis, postmodum salutando in osculo pacis, videlicet per unionem carnis; sed etiam salutem operando per mortem crucis. Exsurgat igitur spiritus noster alacri gaudio, curratque obviam Salvatori suo, et jam a longe venientem adoret et salutet, acclamans ei et dicens: O Domine, salvum me fac; o Domine, bene prosperare! Benedictus qui venturus es in nomine Domini (Psal. CXVII, 25, 26). Salve, qui venis salvare nos; benedictus, qui venis benedicere nos. Igitur, o Domine, bene prosperare, qui tam prosper et salutaris advenis generi humano. Intende, prospere procede et regna. Prosperum iter faciet tibi Pater, Deus salutarium nostrorum. Prosperabitur, inquit Pater, in his ad quae misi illum (Isa. LV, 11): non pro votis carnalium, non pro voluntate Petri, qui abhorrebat illum pati. Et omnia quaecunque faciet prosperabuntur (Psal. I, 3): non ad praeproperam hominum voluntatem, sed ad veram eorum salutem. Vana quidem salus hominis (Psal. CVII, 13): sed Domini est salus (Psal. III, 9), qui operatus est salutem de sanguine suo, fundens illum in pretium, et propinans in potum. Veni ergo, o Domine, salvum me fac, et salvus ero (Jerem. XVII, 14). Veni et ostende faciem tuam, et salvi erimus (Psal. LXXIX. 4, 8, 20). Te enim exspectavimus: esto brachium nostrum, salus nostra in tempore tribulationis (Isai. XXXIII, 2). Sic Prophetae et justi desiderio et affectu tanto ante Christo venturo occurrebant, desiderantes, si fieri posset, oculis videre quod spiritu praevidebant. Unde Dominus discipulis dicebat: Beati oculi qui vident quae vos videtis. Dico enim vobis, quod multi prophetae, et reges voluerunt videre quae vos videtis, et non viderunt (Luc. X, 23, 24). Abraham quoque pater noster exsultavit, ut videret diem Christi: vidit, sed apud inferos, et gavisus est. In quo utique tepor et duritia cordis nostri suggillatur, si non videt eum gaudio spirituali.

English translation

2. And truly, brothers, we must go out to meet the coming Christ with the rejoicing of the spirit. Even now, from afar, he must be greeted—or certainly greeted in return—he who sends greetings of salvation to Jacob. “I will not be ashamed to greet a friend,” says the Wise Man (Eccli. XXII, 31); how much more to greet him in return? O salvation of my face, and my God! How great was your condescension, that you greeted your servants; but how much greater was it that you saved them! For our salvation would not have been complete if you had sent greetings of salvation but had not given salvation. Yet you did give it—not only by greeting those whom you had greeted with words of peace, later greeting them with the kiss of peace, that is, through the union of flesh, but also by accomplishing salvation through the death of the cross. Let our spirit, then, rise up in joyful gladness, and run out to meet its Savior; let it already adore and greet him as he comes from afar, acclaiming him and saying: “O Lord, save me; O Lord, prosper!” “Blessed are you who are to come in the name of the Lord” (Psal. CXVII, 25, 26). Hail, you who come to save us; blessed are you who come to bless us. Therefore, O Lord, prosper, since you come to the human race so prosperously and as its Savior. Attend, proceed prosperously, and reign. The Father, the God of our salvation, will make your journey prosperous. “It shall prosper,” says the Father, “in the things for which I sent him” (Isa. LV, 11)—not according to the desires of the carnal, nor according to the will of Peter, who shrank from his suffering. “And all that he does shall prosper” (Psal. I, 3)—not according to the hasty will of human beings, but for their true salvation. “Vain indeed is the salvation of man” (Psal. CVII, 13); “but salvation belongs to the Lord” (Psal. III, 9), who accomplished salvation by his blood, pouring it out as the price and offering it as a drink. Come, then, O Lord, “save me, and I shall be saved” (Jerem. XVII, 14). “Come and show your face, and we shall be saved” (Psal. LXXIX. 4, 8, 20). For we have waited for you: “be our arm, our salvation in the time of tribulation” (Isai. XXXIII, 2). In this way the prophets and the righteous, with such great desire and longing, went out so long beforehand to meet the Christ who was to come, wishing, if it were possible, to see with their eyes what they foresaw in spirit. Hence the Lord said to his disciples: “Blessed are the eyes that see what you see. For I tell you that many prophets and kings wished to see what you see, and did not see it” (Luc. X, 23, 24). Our father Abraham, too, rejoiced that he might see Christ’s day; he saw it, but in the netherworld, and rejoiced. In this, surely, the lukewarmness and hardness of our heart are reproved, if it does not see him with spiritual joy.

Latin original

3. Christi nascentis diem anniversarium exspectamus; qui nobis in proximo videndus, Domino annuente, promittitur. Hoc sane gaudium nostrum tale videtur exigere Scriptura, ut spiritus noster levans se super se, Christo venienti quodammodo occurrere gestiat: et desiderio se extendens in anteriora, impatiensque morarum, jam videre contendat futura. Ego namque non solum ad secundum adventum, sed etiam ad primum arbitror pertinere, quod tot locis Scripturarum ei monemur occurrere. Quomodo, inquis? Quia videlicet sicut secundo adventui occurremus motu et exsultatione corporis: sic et primo occurrendum est affectu et exsultatione cordis. Scitis enim quia resumptis in resurrectione corporibus novis, secundum doctrinam Apostoli, rapiemur in nubibus obviam Christo in aera, et sic semper cum Domino erimus (I Thess. IV, 16). Sic nec modo desunt nubes, quae spiritus nostros, si nimis non fuerint pigri terraeque affixi, sublevabunt ad altiora; et sic cum Domino erimus, vel hora dimidia. Agnoscit, ni fallor, experientia vestra quod loquor, cum aliquando vocem dederunt nubes, id est, sonuerunt in Ecclesia prophetarum, vel apostolorum voces, ad quam sublimia sensus vestri, quasi vehiculo nubis, fuerint subvecti; et eo usque nonnunquam excesserint, ut gloriam Domini quantulumcunque mererentur speculari. Tunc, ni fallor, vobis innotuit veritas illius sermonis, quem Dominus de illa nube pluit, quam quotidie ponit ascensum vobis: Sacrificium laudis honorificabit me: et illic iter, quo ostendam illi salutare Dei (Psal. XLIX, 23). Ita ergo sit, ut ante adventum suum Dominus veniat ad vos, et antequam mundo generaliter adveniat, familiariter invisat vos. Non vos, inquit, relinquam orphanos: vado, et veniam ad vos (Joan. XIV, 18). Et quidem pro merito cujusque, vel studio, creber est ad unumquemque iste Domini adventus, hoc tempore medio inter adventum primum et novissimum, conformans nos adventui primo, et praeparans novissimo. Ad hoc nempe venit modo in nos, ne primo adventu frustra venerit ad nos, vel ne in novissimo veniat iratus adversus nos. Hoc siquidem adventu satagit reformare sensum superbiae nostrae, configuratum sensui humilitatis suae, quam primo veniens exhibuit; ut perinde reformet corpus humilitatis nostrae, configuratum corpori claritatis suae, quam denuo rediens exhibebit. Prorsus optandus omnibus votis, et expetendus studiis adventus iste familiaris, qui nobis gratiam impertiat adventus primi, et gloriam promittat novissimi. Quia enim misericordiam et veritatem diligit Deus, gratiam et gloriam dabit Dominus (Psal. LXXXIII, 12); per misericordiam praerogans gratiam, per veritatem reddens gloriam.

English translation

3. We await the anniversary of Christ’s birth, which, with the Lord’s favor, is promised to us as something we shall soon see. Scripture certainly seems to require that our joy be such that our spirit, lifting itself above itself, eagerly desire in some way to go out to meet the coming Christ; stretching forward in its longing and impatient of delay, it strives already to see what is still to come. For I believe that it applies not only to the second coming but also to the first, that in so many places of Scripture we are urged to go out to meet him. “How so?” you ask. Because, just as we shall go out to meet his second coming through the movement and exultation of the body, so we must go out to meet his first through the affection and exultation of the heart. You know, in fact, that when our bodies have been taken up again in the resurrection, renewed, then, according to the Apostle’s teaching, “we shall be caught up in the clouds to meet Christ in the air, and so we shall always be with the Lord” (I Thess. IV, 16). Even now, then, clouds are not lacking—clouds that will lift our spirits to higher things, if they are not too sluggish or fixed to the earth—and so we shall be with the Lord, even if only for half an hour. Your own experience, unless I am mistaken, recognizes what I am saying: sometimes the clouds have given voice—that is, the voices of prophets or apostles have sounded in the Church—and your understanding has been carried up to sublime heights, as though in a cloud’s chariot; sometimes it has risen so far that it merited, however slightly, to contemplate the glory of the Lord. Then, unless I am mistaken, you came to know the truth of that saying which the Lord rained down upon you from that cloud, which he sets before you every day as a means of ascent: “The sacrifice of praise will honor me, and there is the way by which I will show him the salvation of God” (Psal. XLIX, 23). Let it be so, then: before his coming, may the Lord come to you, and before he comes to the world as a whole, may he visit you personally. “I will not leave you orphans,” he says; “I am going away, and I will come to you” (Joan. XIV, 18). And indeed, according to each person’s merit or devotion, this coming of the Lord to each one is frequent during this time between his first and final coming: it conforms us to the first coming and prepares us for the final one. For this is why he comes into us now: so that he may not have come to us in vain in his first coming, and so that at the last he may not come against us in anger. By this coming he earnestly strives to reform our proud mind, conforming it to the mind of his humility, which he displayed when he first came; and thereby to reform our body of humility, conforming it to the body of his glory, which he will display when he returns. This personal coming is wholly to be desired with every prayer and sought with every effort: it grants us the grace of his first coming and promises us the glory of the last. For because God loves mercy and truth, “the Lord will give grace and glory” (Psal. LXXXIII, 12), granting grace beforehand through mercy and repaying glory through truth.

4. Porro sicut dispositione temporis, sic etiam proportione similitudinis medius est adventus iste spiritualis inter adventum utrumque corporalem, et velut quidam mediator, participat cum utroque. Primus siquidem adventus, occultus et humilis; ultimus, manifestus et admirabilis: iste autem occultus quidem, sed mirabilis. Occultum sane dixerim, non quod ignoretur ab eo cui venit, sed quia secretus ei advenit. Unde glorians illa gloriosa anima secum cum dixit: Secretum meum mihi, secretum meum mihi (Isa. XXIV, 16). Sed nec ab ipso cui advenit, potest ante videri, quam teneri, juxta illud beati Job de se confitentis: Si venerit ad me, non videbo eum; si abierit, non intelligam (Job IX, 11). Nec veniens quippe videtur, nec recedens intelligitur, qui solummodo, dum praesens est, lumen est animae et intellectus, quo invisibilis videtur, et incogitabilis intelligitur. Caeterum quam admirabilis sit, licet occultus sit iste adventus Domini, quam suavi et felici stupore in admirationem sui suspendat et expendat animam contemplantis, quomodo omnia ossa interioris hominis acclament ei, Domine, quis similis tui (Psal. XXXIV, 10)? norunt quidem experti; sed utinam satis desiderent experiri non experti: ita duntaxat, ut non temeraria curiositas faciat scrutari majestatem opprimendos a gloria, sed pia charitas suspirare dilectum suscipiendos a gratia. Suscipiens enim mansuetos Dominus, humilians autem peccatores usque ad terram (Psal. CXLVI, 6), superbis resistit, humilibus autem dat gratiam (Jac. IV, 6). Cum igitur primus adventus sit gratiae; novissimus, gloriae: iste nimirum, gratiae pariter et gloriae; in quo videlicet per consolantem gratiam futuram nobis utcunque datur praelibare gloriam. Cum igitur in primo Deus majestatis visus sit contemptibilis, in novissimo autem videndus sit terribilis, in isto medio videtur mirabilis et amabilis: ut nec propter dignationem gratiae, qua se praebet amabilem, possit esse contemptui, sed admirationi; nec propter magnificentiam gloriae, qua mirabilis apparet, sit terrori, sed potius consolationi. De primo dicebat Videns: Vidimus eum, et non erat ei species, neque decor: unde nec reputavimus eum (Isa. LIII, 2, 3). De ultimo expavescit etiam justus, dicens: Et quis stabit ad videndum eum? (Malach. III, 2.) De isto autem dicit Apostolus: Gloriam Domini speculantes, in eamdem imaginem transformamur a gloria in gloriam, tanquam a Domini Spiritu (II Cor. III, 18). Mirabile prorsus, et amabile, cum Deus amor amantis sensibus illabitur: cum sponsam Sponsus in unitate Spiritus amplectitur, et in eamdem cum illa imaginem transformatur; per quam, velut per speculum, Domini gloriam speculatur. Quam beati, quorum ardens charitas hanc praerogativam jam meruit obtinere! sed et illi beati, quorum sancta simplicitas idem quandoque potest sperare! Et illi quidem jam de fructu amoris, sui capiunt solatium laboris: isti autem tanto fortasse majore merito, quanto interim minore solatio, portantes pondus diei et aestus, exspectant adventum remunerationis. Nos igitur, fratres, quos nondum consolatur tam sublimis experientia, ut patientes simus usque ad adventum Domini, consoletur interim certa fides et pura conscientia, quae tam feliciter quam fideliter cum Paulo dicat: Scio cui credidi, et certus sum quia potens est depositum meum servare in illum diem (II Tim. I, 12): in adventum scilicet gloriae magni Dei, et Salvatoris nostri Jesu Christi, cui est gloria in saecula saeculorum. Amen.

English translation

4. Moreover, just as it is intermediate in the order of time, so also in the likeness it bears, this spiritual advent stands between the two bodily advents and, as a kind of mediator, shares in both. The first advent was hidden and humble; the last will be manifest and admirable; this one, however, is hidden yet wondrous. I call it hidden—not because it is unknown to the one to whom he comes, but because he comes to that person in secret. Hence that glorious soul, glorying within itself, said: “My secret is for me, my secret is for me” (Isa. XXIV, 16). Yet even by the one to whom he comes, he cannot be seen before he is possessed, in accordance with the blessed Job’s confession about himself: “If he comes to me, I shall not see him; if he goes away, I shall not understand” (Job IX, 11). For he is neither seen as he comes nor understood as he departs; only while he is present is he the light of the soul and of the intellect, by which the invisible is seen and the unthinkable is understood. But how wondrous this advent of the Lord is, even though hidden; how, with what sweet and joyful astonishment, it suspends and absorbs the soul that contemplates him in wonder at himself, so that all the bones of the inner person cry out to him, “Lord, who is like you?” (Psal. XXXIV, 10)—those who have experienced it know; but would that those who have not experienced it desired enough to experience it. Let it be only in such a way that reckless curiosity does not lead one to scrutinize the majesty and be overwhelmed by the glory, but devout love makes one sigh for the Beloved and be received by grace. For the Lord receives the meek, but humbles sinners to the ground (Psal. CXLVI, 6); he resists the proud, but gives grace to the humble (Jac. IV, 6). Since, then, the first advent is one of grace and the last one of glory, this advent is plainly of both grace and glory: in it, through the grace that consoles us, we are in some measure given a foretaste of the glory that is to come. Since, then, in the first advent the God of majesty appeared contemptible, while in the last he will be seen as terrifying, in this middle advent he is seen as wondrous and lovable: so that, because of the condescension of grace by which he makes himself lovable, he cannot be despised but rather admired; and because of the magnificence of the glory in which he appears wondrous, he is not a cause of terror but rather of consolation. Of the first advent the Seer said: “We saw him, and there was in him neither appearance nor beauty; therefore we did not regard him” (Isa. LIII, 2, 3). Of the last even the just man is afraid, saying: “And who will stand to see him?” (Malach. III, 2.) But of this advent the Apostle says: “Beholding the glory of the Lord, we are transformed into the same image, from glory to glory, as by the Spirit of the Lord” (II Cor. III, 18). Truly wondrous and lovable it is when God, who is love, glides into the senses of the one who loves; when the Bridegroom embraces the bride in the unity of the Spirit and is transformed with her into the same image, through which, as through a mirror, the glory of the Lord is beheld. How blessed are those whose ardent love has already merited this privilege! Yet blessed also are those whose holy simplicity may one day hope for the same. The former already draw comfort from the fruit of love for their labor; the latter, perhaps with all the greater merit because meanwhile they have less consolation, bear the burden of the day and the heat while awaiting the advent of their reward. Therefore, brothers, we whom so sublime an experience does not yet console: that we may be patient until the coming of the Lord, may certain faith and a pure conscience console us meanwhile, saying with Paul, as happily as faithfully: “I know whom I have believed, and I am certain that he is able to guard my deposit until that day” (II Tim. I, 12)—that is, until the advent of the glory of the great God and our Savior Jesus Christ, to whom be glory forever and ever. Amen.
