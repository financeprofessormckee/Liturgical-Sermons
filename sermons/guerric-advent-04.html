Editorial introduction

This fourth Advent sermon belongs to Guerric of Igny's medieval collection of sermons for the liturgical year, here read from the 1879 edition of Patrologia Latina. Its individual date is unknown; the collection is associated approximately with his abbacy in 1138–1157. Addressed to monks in the solitude of the desert, it reads John the Baptist's cry to prepare the way for the Lord as a call to correct one's life. Guerric commends the peaceful ways of wisdom, the spotless way of chastity, the baptism of penance and knowledge of God's law. The source names Advent without assigning a particular Sunday.

SERMON IV.

¶col. 21
Latin original

De vita solitaria, et de via Domino paranda.

English translation

On the solitary life, and on preparing the way for the Lord.

¶cols. 21-22
Latin original

1. Vox clamantis in deserto, Parate viam Domini (Isa. XL, 3). Primo omnium considerandam arbitror gratiam deserti, beatitudinem eremi; quae ab initio gratiae, quieti sanctorum meruit consecrari. Consecravit nobis plane habitationem deserti vox clamantis in deserto, Joannes praedicans et tribuens baptismum poenitentiae in deserto; quanquam et ante ipsum quibusque sanctissimis prophetarum, velut auditorium Spiritus, amica semper fuerit solitudo. Longe tamen excellentior atque divinior ipsi loco gratia sanctificationis accessit, cum Jesus Joanni successit: qui etiam, antequam praedicare poenitentibus inciperet, locum poenitentibus praeparandum putavit; atque per dies quadraginta, dum versatur in deserto, velut purificans locum, novumque novae vitae dedicans locum, tyrannum qui incubabat, omnemque malitiam et subtilitatem ejus, non tam sibi, quam illis qui accolae eremi futuri erant, superavit. Si ergo elongasti fugiens et manens in solitudine, ibi permane, ibi exspecta eum qui te salvum faciet a pusillanimitate spiritus, et tempestate. Quantalibet ingruat tibi tempestas bellorum, quantacunque patiaris in deserto, penuriam etiam victualium, noli pusillanimitate spiritus redire mente in Aegyptum. Felicius te pascet eremus manna, id est pane angelorum, quam Aegyptus ollis carnium. Ipse Jesus quidem in eremo jejunavit; sed multitudinem sequentium se in deserto, saepe et mirabiliter pavit. Saepius autem et mirabilius te satiabit, qui tanto gratiore merito, quanto sanctiore proposito secutus es eum in deserto. Cum enim diutius eum tui oblitum putaveris, ipse suae non immemor bonitatis, consolabitur te, et dicet tibi: Recordatus sum tui, miserans adolescentiam tuam, et charitatem desponsationis tuae, quando secuta es me in deserto (Jerem. II, 2). Tunc plane ponet desertum tuum quasi delicias paradisi: et ipse confiteberis quia gloria Libani data est ei, decor Carmeli et Saron (Isa. XXXV, 2). Sicut enim in multis hodie locis, etiam juxta litteram illud propheticum videmus impleri, Pinguescent speciosa deserti (Psal. LXIV, 13); et, Deserta in ubertatem versa advenae comedent (Isa. V, 17): sic quilibet Scripturae locus, qui ante tibi sterilis videbatur et aridus, repente ad benedictionem Dei mira replebitur ubertate, ac pinguedine spiritus; ut de saturitate mentis eructes hymnum laudis, Confiteantur, inquiens, Domino, misericordiae ejus, et mirabilia ejus filiis hominum: quia satiavit animam inanem, et animam esurientem satiavit bonis (Psal. CVI, 8, 9).

English translation

1. The voice of one crying in the desert: Prepare the way of the Lord (Isa. XL, 3). First of all, I think we must consider the grace of the desert, the blessedness of the wilderness; which from the very beginning of grace deserved to be consecrated to the rest of the saints. The voice crying in the desert clearly consecrated for us the dwelling-place of the desert—John, preaching and bestowing the baptism of repentance in the desert; although even before him solitude had always been a friend to the holiest of the prophets, as a hearing-chamber of the Spirit. Yet a far more excellent and more divine grace of sanctification came upon that very place when Jesus succeeded John, who, even before he began to preach to penitents, thought it right that the place be prepared for penitents; and for forty days, while he dwelt in the desert, as though purifying the place and dedicating this new place to a new life, he overcame the tyrant who lay brooding there, and all his malice and cunning—not so much for his own sake as for the sake of those who were to be future dwellers of the wilderness. If, then, you have fled far off and remain in solitude, stay there; wait there for him who will save you from faintness of spirit and from the storm. However great a storm of battles may rush upon you, whatever you may suffer in the desert, even a lack of provisions, do not, through faintness of spirit, turn back in your mind to Egypt. The wilderness will feed you more happily with manna, that is, with the bread of angels, than Egypt with its pots of meat. Jesus himself indeed fasted in the wilderness; but the multitude that followed him in the desert he often and wonderfully fed. But he will satisfy you still more often and still more wonderfully, since you have followed him into the desert with a purpose all the holier, and thus with a merit all the more pleasing. For when you have thought for a long time that he has forgotten you, he himself, not forgetful of his own goodness, will console you and say to you: I have remembered you, having pity on your youth and on the love of your betrothal, when you followed me into the desert (Jerem. II, 2). Then he will surely make your desert like the delights of paradise, and you yourself will confess that the glory of Lebanon has been given to it, the beauty of Carmel and Sharon (Isa. XXXV, 2). For just as today, in many places, we see that prophetic word fulfilled even according to the letter—The beautiful places of the desert shall grow fat (Psal. LXIV, 13); and, The deserts turned into abundance shall strangers eat (Isa. V, 17)—so too any passage of Scripture which before seemed to you barren and dry will suddenly, by God's blessing, be filled with wondrous abundance and with fatness of spirit, so that from the fullness of your mind you will belch forth a hymn of praise: Let them give praise to the Lord, he says, for his mercies, and his wonderful works to the children of men: for he has satisfied the empty soul, and filled the hungry soul with good things (Psal. CVI, 8, 9).

¶cols. 21-22; cols. 23-24
Latin original

2. Illud sane mirabili gratia provisum est divinae dispensationis, ut in his desertis nostris quietem habeamus solitudinis, nec tamen consolatione careamus gratiae et sanctae societatis. Licet cuique sedere solitarium, et tacere, eo quod neminem patiatur interpellantem: nec tamen vae soli, eo quod non habeat confoventem: aut, si ceciderit, sublevantem (Eccle. IV, 10). In frequentia hominum sumus, et sine turba sumus; velut in urbe versamur, nullum tamen tumultum patimur, quominus vox clamantis in deserto a nobis audiatur: si modo interius, sicut exterius silentium habeamus. Verba namque sapientium, ait Salomon, audiuntur in silentio, plus quam clamor principis inter stultos (Eccle. IX, 17). Et nunc quoque si medium silentium teneant omnia interiora tua, omnipotens sermo tibi secretus illabetur de sede paterna. Felix igitur, qui sic elongavit fugiens tumultum mundi: qui tam procul recessit in secretum et solitudinem quietae mentis, ut non modo vocem Verbi, sed et Verbum ipsum; non Joannem, sed Jesum mereatur audire! Interim tamen quid vox Verbi nobis clamet audiamus, ut quandoque de voce ad Verbum proficiamus. Parate, inquit, viam Domino: rectas facite semitas ejus (Isa. XL, 3). Parat viam, qui corrigit vitam: rectam facit semitam, qui per arctiorem se dirigit viam. Plane vita correcta, via est recta, per quam Dominus veniat ad nos, qui in hoc ipsum praevenit nos. A Domino siquidem gressus hominis dirigentur, et viam sic ejus volet, ut gratanter per eam veniens ad hominem, cum eo jugiter ambulet. Nisi enim suum ad nos adventum ipse praeveniat, qui est via, veritas et vita (Joan. XIV, 6); non potest via nostra corrigi secundum regulam veritatis, ac per hoc nec dirigi ad vitam aeternitatis. In quo vero corrigit adolescentior viam suam, nisi in custodiendo sermones ejus, nisi in sequendo vestigia ejus, qui viam fecit se ipsum, qua veniamus ad ipsum? Utinam dirigantur viae meae, ad custodiendas, o Domine, vias tuas (Psal. CXVIII, 5); ut propter verba labiorum tuorum custodiam etiam vias duras: quae etsi durae videantur carni, quia infirma est, suaves et pulchrae videbuntur spiritui, si promptus est! Viae, inquit, ejus viae pulchrae, et omnes semitae ejus pacificae (Prov. III, 17). Non solum pacatae, sed et pacificae sunt viae sapientiae: quia, cum placuerint Domino viae hominis, etiam inimicos ejus convertet ad pacem (Id. XVI, 7). Israel, si in viis meis, inquit, ambulasset, pro nihilo forsitan inimicos eorum humiliassem, et super tribulantes eos misissem manum meam (Psal. LXXX, 14, 15). Quare enim contritio et infelicitas in viis eorum, nisi quia viam pacis non cognoverunt (Psal. XIII, 3)? Eapropter, si quando vidi hominem inquietum, contumacem, litigiosum, gravissima tentatione vexari, vel tribulatione flagellari, recordabar illius proverbii: Semper jurgia quaerit malus: angelus autem crudelis mittetur contra eum (Prov. XVII, 11), cui traditur forsitan in afflictionem carnis, ut spiritus salvus sit in die Domini. Si tamen aliquando prosperatur via impiorum, noli aemulari in eo qui prosperatur in via sua, in homine faciente injustitias (Psal. XXXVI, 7): quia haec via eorum scandalum ipsis; ut tanto liberius in mortem currant, quanto minus sepitur eis via qua ambulant. De istis Sapiens loquitur: Via peccantium complanata lapidibus: sed in fine illorum, inferi, tenebrae et poenae (Eccli. XXI, 11): et, Ducunt in bonis dies suos, et in puncto descendunt ad inferos (Job XXI, 13). Beati igitur immaculati in via, qui ambulant in via Domini! quia, etsi de torrente in via bibunt, propterea exaltabunt caput; quia caput suum, quod sequuntur per viam passionis, consequentur in fine mansionis.

English translation

2. It was surely provided by a wonderful grace of divine dispensation that in these deserts of ours we should have the quiet of solitude, and yet not lack the consolation of grace and holy fellowship. Each one may sit solitary and be silent, in that he suffers no one interrupting him; and yet it is not "woe to him alone," in that he has no one to comfort him, or, if he should fall, to lift him up (Eccle. IV, 10). We are amid a throng of men, and yet we are without a crowd; we dwell as it were in a city, yet we suffer no tumult that would keep us from hearing the voice of one crying in the desert—provided only that we keep interior silence as we keep exterior silence. For, as Solomon says, the words of the wise are heard in silence more than the shouting of a prince among fools (Eccle. IX, 17). And even now, if all your inward parts keep deep silence, the omnipotent Word will steal into you secretly from the Father's throne. Happy, then, is he who has so withdrawn, fleeing the tumult of the world, who has retreated so far into the secrecy and solitude of a quiet mind, that he deserves to hear not only the voice of the Word, but the Word itself—not John, but Jesus! Meanwhile, however, let us listen to what the voice of the Word cries to us, so that one day we may advance from the voice to the Word. "Prepare," he says, "the way for the Lord; make straight his paths" (Isa. XL, 3). He prepares the way who corrects his life; he makes the path straight who directs himself along the narrower way. Clearly a corrected life is a straight way, by which the Lord may come to us—he who has gone before us for this very purpose. For indeed the steps of man are directed by the Lord, and he so wills his way that, gladly coming by it to man, he may walk continually with him. For unless he himself, who is the way, the truth, and the life (Joan. XIV, 6), should go before us in his own coming to us, our way cannot be corrected according to the rule of truth, and therefore cannot be directed toward the life of eternity. But in what does the young man correct his way, except in keeping his words, except in following the footsteps of him who made himself the way by which we come to him? "Would that my ways were directed, O Lord, to the keeping of your ways" (Psal. CXVIII, 5); that for the sake of the words of your lips I might keep even the hard ways—which, though they seem hard to the flesh, because it is weak, will seem sweet and beautiful to the spirit, if it is willing! "Her ways," it says, "are beautiful ways, and all her paths are peaceful" (Prov. III, 17). The ways of wisdom are not only peaceable but peace-making: because, when the ways of a man please the Lord, he will turn even his enemies to peace (Id. XVI, 7). "If Israel," he says, "had walked in my ways, I might perhaps have humbled their enemies with ease, and would have set my hand against those who troubled them" (Psal. LXXX, 14, 15). For why is there destruction and unhappiness in their ways, except that they have not known the way of peace (Psal. XIII, 3)? For this reason, whenever I have seen a restless, stubborn, quarrelsome man vexed by some very grave temptation or scourged by tribulation, I would recall that proverb: "The wicked man always seeks quarrels; but a cruel angel will be sent against him" (Prov. XVII, 11)—to whom perhaps he is handed over for the affliction of the flesh, that his spirit may be saved in the day of the Lord. Yet if sometimes the way of the impious prospers, do not envy the man who prospers in his way, the man who does unjust things (Psal. XXXVI, 7): for this very way of theirs is a snare to them; so that they run the more freely into death, the less their way is hedged in as they walk it. Of such the Wise Man speaks: "The way of sinners is made smooth with stones; but at their end are hell, darkness, and torments" (Eccli. XXI, 11); and, "They spend their days in good things, and in a moment they go down to hell" (Job XXI, 13). Blessed, then, are the undefiled in the way, who walk in the way of the Lord! For even if they drink of the torrent in the way, they shall therefore lift up the head; because the Head whom they follow through the way of suffering, they shall attain at the end of their dwelling.

Latin original

3. Quidquid igitur occurrerit vobis in via Domini, laeto ac dilatato corde currite viam mandatorum Dei: quia etsi pusillanimis arcta videtur via, tamen recta; et si dura videtur, immaculata est. Plane beati immaculati in via! qui sic immaculato calle transeunt viam mundi, ut non inquinent vestimenta sua: aut certe, si inquinaverint, secundum beatitudinis non amittunt locum; sed lavant vestimenta sua baptismo poenitentiae, quo Joannes baptizat in eremo, viam parans Domino. Via utique immaculata, castitas; via grata, per quam Dominus gratiae dignanter veniat: cui propheta cantat, Deus meus, impolluta via ejus. Impolluta castitas Virginis, qua venit in uterum illius: impolluta necesse est sit castitas hominis, qua veniat in animam illius. Felix conscientia, cui convenit vox illa: Deus praecinxit me virtute continentiae, et posuit immaculatam viam meam (Psal. XVII, 31, 33)! Verumtamen, ne de castitate sine virtutibus aliis glorieris, quasi jam viam immaculatam Domino paraveris: scito quia nihilominus necesse est ut via haec etiam recta sit, plana sit, ut etiam tenebrosa vel lubrica non sit. Nam via impiorum tenebrae et lubricum; et angelus Domini persequens eos (Psal. XXXIV, 6). Et via impiorum tenebrosa: nesciunt ubi corruant (Prov. IV, 19). Putamusne, fratres, in aliquo nostrum inveniatur adhuc pravum aliquid in voluntatibus, asperum in moribus, tenebrosum per ignorantiam sensus, lubricum per inconstantiam actus? Quomodo autem scripturam de via paranda implebimus, nisi, sicut scriptum est, faciamus: Ut sint prava, in directa; et aspera, in vias planas (Isa. XL, 4, et Luc. III, 5)?

English translation

3. Therefore, whatever may meet you on the way of the Lord, run the way of God's commandments with a joyful and enlarged heart: for even if the way seems narrow to the faint-hearted, it is nevertheless straight; and if it seems hard, it is spotless. Truly blessed are the spotless in the way! who so pass through the way of the world by a spotless path that they do not stain their garments: or if indeed they have stained them, they do not lose the second place of blessedness; but they wash their garments in the baptism of penance, with which John baptizes in the desert, preparing the way for the Lord. Surely the spotless way is chastity; the pleasing way, by which the Lord of grace may deign to come: to whom the prophet sings, 'My God, his way is undefiled.' Undefiled was the chastity of the Virgin, by which he came into her womb; undefiled must necessarily be the chastity of man, by which he comes into his soul. Happy is the conscience to which that voice applies: 'God has girded me with the strength of continence, and has made my way spotless' (Psal. XVII, 31, 33)! Yet, lest you boast of chastity apart from the other virtues, as though you had already prepared a spotless way for the Lord, know that it is likewise necessary that this way be also straight, be level, and also not be dark or slippery. For the way of the wicked is darkness and slipperiness; and the angel of the Lord pursues them (Psal. XXXIV, 6). And the way of the wicked is dark: they do not know where they will fall (Prov. IV, 19). Do we suppose, brothers, that there may still be found in any of us something crooked in our wills, something rough in our conduct, something darkened through ignorance of understanding, something slippery through inconstancy of action? But how shall we fulfill the Scripture about preparing the way, unless, as it is written, we do this: that the crooked places be made straight, and the rough ways be made smooth (Isa. XL, 4, et Luc. III, 5)?

Latin original

4. Primo itaque necesse est ut, si qua est in nobis prava et distorta voluntas, corrigatur, et ad regulam divinae voluntatis dirigatur. Aliter videbimur dicere, sicut impii dicunt: Non est recta via Domini. Sed statim refellit nos irrefragabilis veritas, et increpat terribilis severitas: Nunquid non viae meae rectae sunt, et non magis viae vestrae pravae sunt (Ezech. XVIII, 29)? Qui igitur viam Domino parare festinas primo omnium bona sit voluntas; quoniam in malevolam animam non introibit sapientia (Sap. I, 4). Cum autem hoc modo posueris prava in directa, scito quia non minus necesse est ut aspera sint in vias planas: ut scilicet omnem morum complanes asperitatem, et in quamdam vitae socialis sternas aequalitatem, ne mitis et suavis ille viator offensus asperitate viae resiliat. Quidni resiliret ille mitis et humilis corde, qui non nisi super mansuetum requiescit et humilem; cum etiam humanus omnis sensus refugiat et horreat hominem iracundum, et intractabilem? Sed, et si eatenus profecisti in via Domini, ut et propositum rectitudinis, et disciplinam mansuetudinis assecutus sis; profecisti quidem, sed non perfecisti, nisi lucerna sit pedibus tuis verbum Dei, et lumen semitis tuis. In via enim qua ambulabam, ait, absconderunt laqueum mihi (Psal. CXLI, 4). Et sunt viae quae videntur hominibus rectae, novissimum autem earum ducit in profundum inferni. Via enim impiorum tenebrosa, ut Salomon ait: nesciunt ubi corruant (Prov. IV, 19). Hoc et Dominus ait: Qui ambulat in tenebris, nescit quo vadat (Joan. XII, 35). At vero mandatum lucerna est, et lex lux, et via vitae increpatio disciplinae (Prov. VI, 23). Qui enim increpationes relinquit, errat. Si igitur sapis, in via qua nunquam ambulasti, non ipse tibi doctor aut ductor eris, sed aurem magistris inclinabis, et increpationibus eorum acquiesces, et consiliis; et operam scientiae et lectioni dabis, ne sero poenitens dicas: Cur detestatus sum disciplinam, et increpationibus non acquievit cor meum; nec audivi vocem docentium me, et magistris non inclinavi aurem meam? Pene fui in omni malo, in medio ecclesiae, et synagogae (Prov. V, 12-14). Quod enim scientia legis liberet a laqueis, audi confitentem David: Posuerunt peccatores laqueum mihi, sed de mandatis tuis non erravi. Quomodo id potuisti? Quia haereditate acquisivi testimonia tua. Et in via testimoniorum tuorum delectatus sum, sicut in omnibus divitiis. Me exspectaverunt peccatores ut perderent me: sed per hoc evasi, quia testimonia tua intellexi (Psal. CXVIII, 110, 111, 94, 95). Lex Dei, inquit, in corde justi, et per hoc non supplantabuntur gressus ejus (Psal. XXXVI, 31). Hinc et filius ejus Salomon ait: Simulator ore decipit amicum suum, justi autem liberabuntur scientia (Prov. XI, 9).

English translation

4. First of all, then, it is necessary that, if there is in us any crooked and twisted will, it be corrected and directed to the rule of the divine will. Otherwise we shall seem to say, as the wicked say: 'The way of the Lord is not straight.' But immediately unanswerable truth refutes us, and terrible severity rebukes us: 'Are not my ways straight, and are not your ways rather crooked?' (Ezech. XVIII, 29). Therefore you who hasten to prepare the way for the Lord, let your will be good above all else; for wisdom will not enter into a malevolent soul (Sap. I, 4). But when you have in this way made the crooked straight, know that it is no less necessary that the rough places be made into smooth ways: namely, that you smooth away every roughness of conduct, and spread a certain evenness in your life together with others, lest that gentle and sweet traveler, offended by the roughness of the way, draw back. And why would he not draw back—he who is gentle and humble of heart, who rests only upon the meek and the humble—since even every human sense flees and shudders at an angry and intractable man? But even if you have progressed thus far on the way of the Lord, so as to have attained both the purpose of rectitude and the discipline of gentleness, you have indeed made progress, but you have not reached perfection, unless the word of God is a lamp to your feet and a light to your paths. For 'in the way in which I walked,' he says, 'they hid a snare for me' (Psal. CXLI, 4). And there are ways that seem right to men, but their end leads into the depths of hell. For the way of the wicked is dark, as Solomon says: they do not know where they will fall (Prov. IV, 19). The Lord also says this: 'He who walks in darkness does not know where he goes' (Joan. XII, 35). But indeed the commandment is a lamp, and the law is light, and the way of life is the rebuke of discipline (Prov. VI, 23). For whoever abandons rebukes goes astray. If, then, you are wise, on a way you have never walked, you will not be your own teacher or guide, but you will incline your ear to teachers, and you will acquiesce to their rebukes and counsels; and you will give diligence to knowledge and to reading, lest, repenting too late, you say: 'Why have I hated discipline, and my heart did not consent to rebukes; and I did not hear the voice of those who taught me, nor did I incline my ear to my teachers? I was almost in every evil, in the midst of the church and the assembly' (Prov. V, 12-14). That knowledge of the law frees from snares, hear David confessing: 'Sinners have laid a snare for me: but I have not strayed from your commandments.' How were you able to do that? 'Because I have acquired your testimonies as an inheritance. And in the way of your testimonies I have delighted, as in all riches. Sinners waited for me, to destroy me: but through this I escaped, because I understood your testimonies' (Psal. CXVIII, 110, 111, 94, 95). 'The law of God,' he says, 'is in the heart of the just, and therefore his steps shall not be overthrown' (Psal. XXXVI, 31). English translation

5. But would that this way, by which we must receive salvation or the Savior, just as it is now no longer dark through ignorance of the truth, so also might not be slippery through inconstancy of action. But, just as Balaam, when falling, had his eyes open (Num. XXII, 27, 31), so also do we: we see with open eyes through knowledge, and yet we fall through negligence. We sin willingly and knowingly, and we slip gladly: nor can we blame the slipperiness of the way so much as the disposition of the mind, that is, the foot on which we lean. For who does not walk on slippery ground, while living in the world, while living in a body of clay? It is not, then, so much the way that is at fault as the foot, which is less firm on the way of God. But shall he who falls not add to his falling a rising up again? The just man falls seven times in a day, and seven times rises again (Prov. XXIV, 16). For the Lord raises up those who are cast down, the Lord sets the just upright (Psal. CXLV, 8). For, if I said, 'My foot is moved'; your mercy, O Lord, helped me (Psal. XCIII, 18). Therefore, when you cannot do otherwise, walk the way falling and rising, yet always crying out to him whom you desire to follow and to attain: 'Perfect my steps in your paths, that my footsteps be not moved' (Psal. XVI, 5); and, 'if the way of iniquity be in me' (as is human frailty), 'lead me in the way everlasting' (Psal. CXXXVIII, 24); so that through you, who are the way and the truth, I may come to you, who are the truth and life everlasting: to you be glory through everlasting ages. Amen.
