Editorial introduction

Guerric reads the bride's words, "tell my beloved that I languish with love," as Mary's on her deathbed. Using a licence he credits to Jerome, he imagines the angels visiting her: she has languished with fear and with sorrow, and now with love. She declines Gabriel's attendance, content with the disciple Jesus loved, and asks for the kiss of her Son's mouth, trusting that his glory has not changed his gentleness. The sermon appears under the heading for the Assumption of Blessed Mary in the 1879 edition of Patrologia Latina. The collection is associated approximately with Guerric's abbacy in 1138–1157; the individual sermon is undated.

English translation

On the mutual love of Jesus and Mary.

¶cols. 189-190; cols. 191-192
Latin original

1. Filiae Jerusalem, nuntiate dilecto quia amore langueo (Cantic. V, 8). Verba ista quae a nobis hac nocte cantata sunt, qualiter ad Assumptionem beatae Mariae possint videri pertinere, volumus, si placet, cum vestra charitate tractare. Id autem eo genere sermonis faciendum videtur, quo non modo saecularium, sed etiam ecclesiasticarum auctores litterarum nonnunquam usi sunt, maxime in tractandis Canticis, unde haec sunt sumpta. Hoc namque genus sermonis, salva veritate, quadam ampliori utitur libertate, cum videlicet sumpto themate causae, ut beatus Hieronymus ait, non tam loquitur quae revera facta vel dicta sint, quam ejusmodi fuisse negotium ostendit, ut ea quae dicuntur, etsi facta vel dicta non fuerint, fieri tamen et dici potuisse, aut etiam in affectu facientis, vel dicentis fuisse non absurde credi possint. Maria igitur migratura corpore decumbebat, ut est humana infirmitas. Porro filiae Jerusalem quae sursum est, id est angelicae Virtutes, scientes nimirum quia obsequiis Matris captanda est gratia Filii, visitabant satis officiose Dominam suam, Matrem Domini sui. Et fortasse aliquid hujusmodi primo post salutationis officia locuti sunt ei Angeli; sicut speciem humano vultui, ita sermonem humano nimirum conformantes affectui vel usui:

English translation

1. "Daughters of Jerusalem, tell my beloved that I languish with love" (Cantic. V, 8). We wish, if it please you, to treat with your charity how these words, which have been sung by us this night, may be seen to pertain to the Assumption of blessed Mary. This must be done, it seems, in that kind of discourse which not only the authors of secular letters but also of ecclesiastical letters have sometimes employed, especially in treating the Canticle, from which these words are taken. For this kind of discourse, while preserving the truth, makes use of a certain ampler liberty, since, having taken up the theme of the matter, as blessed Jerome says, it does not so much speak of what was actually done or said, as show that the business was of such a kind that the things which are said, even if they were not done or said, could nevertheless have been done and said, or could even, not absurdly, be believed to have been in the affection of the one doing or speaking. Mary, therefore, about to depart in body, lay abed, as is human weakness. Moreover the daughters of the Jerusalem that is above, that is, the angelic Virtues, knowing well that favor with the Son is to be sought through attentions paid to the Mother, were visiting their Lady, the Mother of their Lord, with due dutifulness. And perhaps the Angels said something of this sort to her first, after the courtesies of greeting; conforming their appearance to a human countenance, and so their speech to human affection or custom:

2. Quid est, obsecro, mi Domina, quod sic aegra videris et languida? quid est quod praeter solitum tristis et pigra, ab heri et nudiustertius, sicut solebas, non sancta revisis loca, quorum contemplatione amorem tuum pascebas? Jam per aliquot dies non vidimus te, aut rupem Calvariae ascendentem, ut ibi locum crucis impleres lacrymis; aut ad sepulcrum Filii adorantem gloriam resurrectionis, aut in monte Oliveti deosculantem extrema vestigia ascendentis. --Ob hoc ipsum siquidem creditur in valle Josaphat commorata (ubi et sepulchrum ejus monstratur, ut ait Beatus Hieronymus, in ecclesia miro lapideo fabricata tabulatu), ne scilicet a sanctis locis longius recederet, sed saepius ea invisens, licet omnia memoria teneret, formam tamen gestorum, velut corporaliter in ipsis effigiatam locis, dulcius amplecteretur, ut vel sic suum aliquatenus amorem solaretur.

English translation

2. "What is it, I beg, my Lady, that you seem thus sick and languishing? What is it that, contrary to your custom, you have been sad and sluggish since yesterday and the day before, and have not, as you were wont, revisited the holy places, by contemplation of which you used to nourish your love? For some days now we have not seen you either ascending the rock of Calvary, there to fill the place of the cross with your tears; or adoring the glory of the resurrection at the Son's tomb; or on the Mount of Olives kissing the last footprints of him who ascended." --For this very reason indeed she is believed to have dwelt in the valley of Josaphat (where also her tomb is shown, as blessed Jerome says, in a church wondrously built with a stone roof), namely so that she might not withdraw too far from the holy places, but by visiting them more often, although she held everything in memory, might the more sweetly embrace the form of the deeds, as it were bodily figured in those very places, so that even thus she might in some measure console her love.

3. Causam itaque percunctantibus illis, cur ab his modo vacaret, et ex toto decumberet: Langueo, inquit. Quare langues? Languor enim quem locum in corpore tuo reperit, in quo salus mundi tam diu habitavit? De corpore Filii tui virtus exibat et sanabat omnes, adeo ut etiam fimbria vestimenti haemorrhoissam sanaret (Matth. IX, 20-22; Marc V, 25-34 et Luc. VIII.43-48): et tu quae tamdiu ipsum in utero, in sinu, in gremio continuisti, ulli infirmitati, aut languori vel accessibilis esse potuisti? Non est, inquit, quod id miremini, si illud ipsum corpus Filii mei, quale aliquando fuerit recordemini. Et ipsum enim quam infirmum, quantisve defectibus subditum fuerit, voluntate tamen, ego novi quae ipsum in utero alui, uberibus lactavi, sinu fovi: nec solius infantiae, sed et sequentium defectus aetatum vidi, et eis, ut potui, ministravi: ad extremum non sine passione mea, ludibria et supplicia passionis et crucis aspexi, per singula discens quam vere de eo noster Isaias dixerit, Vere languores nostros ipse tulit, et dolores nostros ipse portavit (Isai. LIII, 4; Matth. VIII, 17). Cur ergo doleam non eum meo dedisse corpori, quod suo non dedit? Non sum ita delicata aut superba, ut quod ille dignatus est pati, ego quantulacunque ex parte pati non possim aut nolim. Et ille quidem misericordi voluntate, ego naturali necessitate. Sane aliud est sanitas, aliud est sanctitas. Sanctitatem dedit corpori meo, concepti sui corporis sacramento: sanitatem se daturum promisit, suscitati corporis exemplo. Denique, ut minus miremini languorem meum, amore langueo. Langueo plus impatientia amoris, quam passione doloris: plus vulnerata charitate, quam gravata infirmitate.

English translation

3. When, therefore, they inquired the cause of why she was now free from these things, and lying abed altogether, she said: "I languish." "Why do you languish? For what place has languor found in your body, in which the salvation of the world dwelt for so long? Power went forth from the body of your Son and healed all, so much so that even the hem of his garment healed the woman with the flow of blood (Matth. IX, 20-22; Marc V, 25-34 et Luc. VIII.43-48): and you, who for so long held him in your womb, in your bosom, in your lap, could you be accessible to any infirmity or languor?" "There is no need," she said, "for you to marvel at this, if you remember what that body of my Son was once like. For how weak it was, and to how many defects it was subject, by his own will, I know, who nourished him in the womb, suckled him at my breasts, cherished him in my lap: and I saw the defects not of infancy alone but of the ages that followed, and I ministered to him as I was able: and at the last, not without my own suffering, I beheld the mockeries and torments of the passion and the cross, learning from each thing how truly our Isaiah said of him, 'Surely he has borne our languors, and carried our sorrows' (Isai. LIII, 4; Matth. VIII, 17). Why then should I grieve that he did not give to my body what he did not give to his own? I am not so delicate or proud that I cannot or will not suffer, to whatever small degree, what he deigned to suffer. He indeed suffered by merciful will, I by natural necessity. Yet health is one thing, holiness another. He gave holiness to my body, by the sacrament of his own body conceived; health he promised he would give, by the example of his body raised up. In short, that you may marvel less at my languor, I languish with love. I languish more from the impatience of love than from the suffering of pain: more wounded by charity than weighed down by infirmity."

4. Heu! inquiunt illi, quam frequentes, imo continuae causae languoris! Bone Jesu, quomodo haec Mater tua postquam te genuit, nunquam fere nisi in languore fuit? Primo languit timore; postea, dolore; nunc, amore. Timore, a nativitate usque ad passionem, cum semper vitam Filii insidiis appeti videret: dolore, toto tempore passionis, donec redivivum reciperet: nunc amore et desiderio felicius, sed mirabilius cruciatur, quia sedentem in coelo non tenet. Quomodo, Jesu bone, qui summi fructus es gaudii, tam longi factus es illi causa martyrii, ut charissimam tibi ipsius animam, tot et tam acuti sine cessatione pertranseant gladii? Sed obsecramus te, Domina, quid vis faciamus tibi? Vis saltem ut resideat hic iste Gabriel symmystes tuus, ut assideat et ministret tibi, qui ab initio conscius et minister mysterii tui, custos quoque tui cubiculi meruit deputari? Non opus est, inquit Virgo. Sufficit mihi meus novus in carne angelus, discipulum dico quem diligebat Jesus, cujus me dilectionis haeredem reliquit, cum illum mihi, et me illi in cruce commendavit: cujus obsequio nihil mihi gratius, quia conversatione et affectu nihil castius, moribus nihil suavius, nihil sincerius fide, nihil sanctius sermone. Nos ergo, aiunt illi, in quo tibi esse poterimus officiosi? Filiae, inquit, Jerusalem, nuntiate dilecto quia amore langueo. Ipse novit quomodo meo medendum sit languori.

English translation

4. "Alas," they say, "how frequent — nay, how continual — are the causes of your languishing! Good Jesus, how is it that this Mother of yours, after she bore you, has scarcely ever been free from languor? First she languished with fear; afterward, with sorrow; now, with love. With fear, from the nativity even to the passion, since she always saw the life of her Son beset by snares; with sorrow, throughout the whole time of the passion, until she received him alive again; now she is tormented more happily, but more wondrously, by love and longing, since she does not hold him as he sits in heaven. How is it, good Jesus, that you, who are the fruit of highest joy, became for her the cause of so long a martyrdom, that so many and so sharp swords should pass, without ceasing, through that soul of hers so dear to you?" "But we beseech you, Lady, what do you wish us to do for you? Do you wish at least that this Gabriel, your companion in the mystery, remain here, to sit by you and minister to you — he who from the beginning was made privy to, and minister of, your mystery, and deserved also to be appointed guardian of your chamber?" "There is no need," says the Virgin. "My new angel in the flesh suffices me — I mean the disciple whom Jesus loved, whose heir in love he left me to be, when on the cross he commended him to me, and me to him: nothing is more pleasing to me than his service, since in conversation and affection nothing is more chaste, in manner nothing more gentle, in faith nothing more sincere, in speech nothing more holy." "What, then," they say, "can we do to be of service to you?" "Daughters of Jerusalem," she says, "tell the beloved that I languish with love. He himself knows how my languor must be healed."

5. At nosti, inquiunt, quia cum sciat omnia, de multis quasi nescius interrogat. Si ergo quaesierit, quid illud sit quod tuo vulneri velis adhiberi, quid respondebimus ei? Sodales, inquit, estis sponsi, Gabriel iste meus paranymphus: non puto vobis esse celandum amoris mysterium. Dicam igitur: tantum ne judicer temeraria, quod majora me quaerere videar. Osculetur me osculo oris sui. Si quid mihi conscia essem, contenta essem cum Maria Magdalene osculo pedum, ubi indulgentia sumitur delictorum. Sed quia in omni vita mea non me reprehendit cor meum, non puto me arroganter flagitare de osculo oris, gratiam gaudiorum. Cur autem arrogans videar, si illud os mihi reposco, quod de me sibi formavit ipse conditus et conditor? Cum parvulum tenebam inter brachia, quoties osculari speciosum forma prae filiis hominum mihi libebat, satis licebat: nunquam faciem avertebat, nunquam matrem repellebat. Et si fortassis impatientia desiderii eram nimia; ipse tamen more suo, morem matri gerebat. Gaudebat eam replere gratia, quae diffusa erat in labiis ejus, et dulcedine qua totus erat plenus, totus concupiscentia et desiderium, castarum scilicet animarum. Unde sicut ipse de se confitetur, Qui edunt me adhuc esurient, et qui bibunt me adhuc sitient (Eccli. XXIV, 29, et Joan. VI, 35): quanto gratiam oris illius gustavi suavius, tanto nimirum repeto nunc ardentius. Crevit quidem gloria et majestate: sed nihil ab illa ingenita mutatus est mansuetudine et bonitate. Nihil ad illum illud superbiae saecularis elogium: “Honores mutant mores.” Sublimior est, sed non superbior; gloriosior, sed non dedignantior. Non fastidiet matrem quam elegit, nec aeternam electionem suam novo judicio reprobabit.

English translation

5. "But you know," they say, "that although he knows all things, he asks about many things as though ignorant. If, then, he should ask what it is you wish applied to your wound, what shall we answer him?" "You are companions of the bridegroom," she says, "and this Gabriel of mine is his paranymph: I do not think the mystery of love should be hidden from you. I will tell it, then — only let me not be judged presumptuous for seeming to seek things greater than myself. Let him kiss me with the kiss of his mouth. If I were conscious of any guilt in myself, I would be content, like Mary Magdalene, with the kiss of the feet, where pardon of sins is received. But because, throughout my whole life, my heart does not reproach me, I do not think that in demanding the kiss of the mouth I am arrogantly asking for the grace of joys. But why should I seem arrogant, if I ask back for myself that mouth which he, both the one fashioned and the fashioner, formed for himself out of me? When I held him as a little one between my arms, as often as it pleased me to kiss him who was beautiful in form above the sons of men, it was granted me freely enough: he never turned away his face, never repelled his mother. And if perhaps I was too impatient in my longing, he nevertheless, after his own manner, indulged his mother's way. He rejoiced to fill her with the grace that was poured out upon his lips, and with the sweetness of which he was wholly full — wholly the desire and longing, namely, of chaste souls. Whence, as he himself confesses of himself, 'They who eat me shall still hunger, and they who drink me shall still thirst' (Eccli. XXIV, 29, et Joan. VI, 35): the more sweetly I have tasted the grace of his mouth, the more ardently, surely, do I now seek it again. He has indeed grown in glory and majesty; but he has in no way been changed from that innate gentleness and goodness. To him applies nothing of that maxim of worldly pride: 'Honors change manners.' He is more exalted, but not more proud; more glorious, but not more disdainful. He will not disdain the mother whom he chose, nor by some new judgment reject his eternal election."

6. Ne timeas, Maria, ait Gabriel more suo: invenisti enim gratiam apud Deum. Etiam in caeteris qui longe jacent tuis sub vestigiis, probare consuevit improbitatem hujus affectus et precis; qui nihil nimium, aut injuriosum sibi deputat, quod in eum vera duntaxat charitas praesumat. Et conversus ad sociam multitudinem: Eamus, inquit, eamus ne ipsi Filio facere videamur injuriam, si gloriam Matris differamus. Cum ergo reversi nuntiassent haec Domino suo, quid putamus Jesum nisi tale aliquid locutum? Ego sum qui patrem et matrem filiis honorandos commendavi; ego ut facerem quod docui, et exemplo essem aliis, ut Patrem honorarem, in terram descendi: nihilominus, ut Matrem honorarem, in coelum reascendi. Ascendi et praeparavi ei locum, thronum gloriae; ut a dextris Regis Regina coronata consedeat in vestitu deaurato, circumdata varietate. Neque hoc dico, quod in parte thronus ei collocetur: quin potius ipsa erit thronus meus. Veni igitur, electa mea, et ponam in te thronum meum. In te mihi quamdam regni sedem constituam, de te judicia decernam, per te preces exaudiam. Nullus mihi plus ministravit in humilitate mea. Communicasti mihi praeter alia quod homo sum: communicabo tibi quod Deus sum. Flagitabas osculum oris, quinimo tota de toto osculaberis. Non imprimam labia labiis, sed spiritum spiritui osculo perpetuo et indissolubili: quia concupivi speciem tuam, etiam desiderantius quam tu meam; nec satis glorificatus videbor mihi, donec tu conglorificeris. Gloria tibi, Domine Jesu, subintulit chorus Angelorum. Gloria tibi, Domine Jesu, ingeminet coetus fidelium. Glorificatio Matris tibi proficiat ad gloriam, nobis ad veniam; te praestante cui est honor, et gloria, per omnia saecula saeculorum. Amen.

English translation

6. "Do not fear, Mary," says Gabriel, in his accustomed way, "for you have found grace with God." Even in others who lie far beneath your footsteps, he is accustomed to show that this boldness of affection and petition is no fault; for he counts nothing excessive or injurious to himself which true charity alone presumes to ask of him. And turning to the company gathered with him, he said, "Let us go, let us go, lest we ourselves seem to do injury to the Son, if we delay the Mother's glory." When, then, they had returned and reported these things to their Lord, what do we suppose Jesus said but something like this? "I am he who commended fathers and mothers to be honored by their children; I, in order to do what I taught, and to be an example to others, that I might honor my Father, descended to earth; nonetheless, that I might honor my Mother, I ascended again to heaven. I have ascended and prepared a place for her, a throne of glory, that at the right hand of the King the Queen, crowned, may sit in gilded raiment, surrounded with manifold adornment. Nor do I say this as though a throne were set apart for her in some portion: rather, she herself shall be my throne. Come, then, my chosen one, and I will set my throne in you. In you I will establish for myself a certain seat of my kingdom; from you I will decree judgments; through you I will grant hearing to prayers. No one has ministered more to me in my humility. You shared with me, besides other things, that I am man: I will share with you that I am God. You were begging for the kiss of the mouth; nay rather, you shall be kissed wholly, by the whole of me. I will not press lip to lip, but spirit to spirit, in a perpetual and indissoluble kiss; for I have desired your beauty, even more longingly than you have desired mine; nor shall I seem to myself sufficiently glorified, until you are glorified together with me." "Glory to you, Lord Jesus," the choir of Angels added. "Glory to you, Lord Jesus," let the assembly of the faithful repeat again and again. May the glorification of the Mother avail for your glory, and for our pardon; through you granting it, to whom is honor and glory, for all ages of ages. Amen.
