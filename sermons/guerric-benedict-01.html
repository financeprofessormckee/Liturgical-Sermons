Introduction & Scripture references

Editorial introduction

Guerric considers perseverance in wisdom through monastic stability, prayer, Scripture, work and silence. He connects obedience under difficult teaching with sacramental nourishment and the fear of God. The sermon appears in the 1879 edition of Patrologia Latina under the feast of Saint Benedict. The collection is associated approximately with Guerric's abbacy in 1138–1157; the individual sermon is undated.

De mansione in sapientia.

English translation

On abiding in wisdom.

¶cols. 99-100
Latin original

1. Beatus vir qui in sapientia morabitur, et qui in justitia meditabitur, et in sensu cogitabit circumspectionem Dei! (Eccli. XIV, 22.) Quam congrue verba haec in laudem beati Benedicti cantata sint, facile quivis vestrum intelligit, cui tamen vita et doctrina ipsius non ignota sit. Quam commode vero ad vitae nostrae doctrinam trahi possint, ipsa per se verborum facies ostendit, quae scilicet illa quibus nihil est utilius in vita, sapientiam, justitiam, timorem Dei, cum praemio etiam beatitudinis commendat. Beatus, inquit, vir qui in sapientia morabitur. Prorsus haec est beatitudo, haec sapientia, si moreris in sapientia, ut perseveranter eam teneas: non enim statim qui invenerit, sed qui tenuerit eam, beatus. Dicit quidem Scriptura: Beatus qui invenit sapientiam. Sed non ibi quievit, imo addidit, et qui affluit sapientia (Prov. III, 13); ne videlicet solum invenisse statim putaretur ad beatitudinem sufficere, nisi, cum inveneris, cum ea et in ea demoreris, et contubernio atque convictu ipsius delecteris; nec discedas a magisterio ejus, donec meditando justitiam, cogitando circumspectionem Dei, affluere prudentia possit. Nam et Salomon sapientiam invenit; sed, quia prudentia minus affluxit, nec satis prudenter sibi circumspexit, dum videlicet non caute sibi providit a mulieribus alienis, non modo sapientiam perdidit, sed usque ad ultimam idololatriae insipientiam proruit (III Reg. XI). Sapientes quoque hujus mundi, qui invisibilia Dei, a creatura mundi per ea quae facta sunt, intellecta conspexerunt, sapientiam invenisse visi sunt; sed, quia nec ipsi prudentia affluxerunt, et, cum cognovissent Deum, non sicut Deum glorificaverunt, stulti et ipsi facti sunt, et obscuratum est insipiens cor eorum usque ad reprobum sensum, et ignominiam turpitudinum (Rom. I, 21-32).

English translation

1. "Blessed is the man who shall abide in wisdom, and who shall meditate in justice, and in understanding shall think upon the circumspection of God!" (Eccli. XIV, 22.) How fittingly these words are sung in praise of blessed Benedict, anyone of you to whom his life and teaching are not unknown easily understands. But how conveniently they can be drawn into instruction for our own life, the very face of the words themselves shows, since they commend those things than which nothing is more useful in life—wisdom, justice, the fear of God—together with the reward of blessedness as well. Blessed, he says, is the man who shall abide in wisdom. This indeed is blessedness, this is wisdom: if you abide in wisdom, so that you hold it perseveringly; for blessed is not at once the one who has found it, but the one who has held fast to it. Scripture indeed says: "Blessed is he who finds wisdom." But it did not stop there; rather it added, "and who overflows with wisdom" (Prov. III, 13); lest, namely, one should think that merely having found it suffices at once for blessedness, unless, when you have found it, you remain with it and in it, and delight in its company and converse; nor do you depart from its teaching, until, by meditating on justice, by thinking on the circumspection of God, you can overflow with prudence. For Solomon too found wisdom; but because he overflowed less with prudence, and did not look about himself prudently enough—since, namely, he did not warily provide for himself against strange women—he not only lost wisdom, but rushed forward even to the final folly of idolatry (III Reg. XI). The wise men of this world too, who gazed upon the invisible things of God, understood from the creation of the world through the things that were made, seemed to have found wisdom; but because they too did not overflow with prudence, and, when they had known God, did not glorify Him as God, they themselves also became fools, and their foolish heart was darkened even unto a reprobate sense, and the disgrace of base things (Rom. I, 21-32).

2. Alios itaque, sicut istorum exemplo liquet, repellit a sapientia quam invenerant, superbia cordis; alios, sicut in Salomone patet, abstrahit illecebra carnis; alii autem levitate et inconstantia mentis, levi perturbatione offensi eam relinquunt, qui scilicet ad tempus credunt, et in tempore tentationis recedunt. Quare autem recedunt? Quia radices non habent, quibus teneantur? (Luc. VIII, 13, 14.) Quomodo autem radicari poterunt, nisi morentur? Quae unquam planta radicatur, nisi loco cui affixa fuerit immoretur? Sic justus plantatus in domo Domini, nec radicari nec fundari in charitate poterit, nisi mora et stabilitate loci. Si autem radicatus non fuerit, nec florere, nec fructum qui maneat, afferre poterit; et, si florere visus fuerit alicujus spei initio, dicetur de illo: Ante messem totus floruit, et immatura perfectio germinavit (Isai. XVIII, 5); et secundum alium prophetam: Si fecerit fructum, alieni comedent eum (Osee, VIII, 7). Vis autem scire quam sit necessaria stabilitas loci ut in sapientia moreris, ut radicari et fructificare possis mora temporis? Interroga Patrem tuum Benedictum, et annuntiabit tibi quia claustrum monasterii, et stabilitas in congregatione, locus idoneus sit ad ferendum omnium fere fructus virtutum, quarum longum ibidem texit catalogum (Reg. S. Bened. cap. 4). Quid enim de instabili Salomon ait? Sicut avis transmigrans de nido suo, sic vir qui relinquit locum suum (Prov. XXVII, 8). Invenerat turtur nidum ubi poneret pullos suos, coeperat eum calefacere et ab eo calefieri, venerant usque ad partum pulli, et ecce avolat, opusque coeptum imperfectum relinquit. Quare, unde, vel quo avolet, ipse viderit, vel quando tot dispendia, quae interim patitur, compensabit, vel qua ratione primam fidem irritam factam excusabit. Ego enim non putarem esse consilii, pro spe incerta, certa subire dispendia; etsi profectus quorumdam me jubeat interim a praecipitatione cohibere sententiam.

English translation

2. Therefore, as is clear from the example of these men, pride of heart drives some away from the wisdom they had found; others, as is plain in the case of Solomon, the allurement of the flesh drags away; while others still, by lightness and inconstancy of mind, offended by some slight disturbance, abandon it—namely, those who believe for a time, and in the time of temptation fall away. But why do they fall away? Is it not because they have no roots by which they might be held fast? (Luc. VIII, 13, 14.) But how will they be able to be rooted, unless they abide? What plant was ever rooted, unless it remained in the place to which it had been fixed? So the just man, planted in the house of the Lord, will not be able to be rooted or founded in charity, except through continued residence and stability of place. But if he has not been rooted, he will not be able either to flower, or to bring forth fruit that remains; and if he has seemed to flower with some beginning of hope, it will be said of him: "Before the harvest it flowered entirely, and an unripe perfection sprouted" (Isai. XVIII, 5); and according to another prophet: "If it bears fruit, strangers shall eat it" (Osee, VIII, 7). But do you wish to know how necessary stability of place is, so that you may abide in wisdom, so that you may be able to be rooted and to bear fruit through length of time? Ask your Father Benedict, and he will declare to you that the cloister of the monastery, and stability in the community, is a place fit for bearing almost all the fruits of the virtues, of which he weaves a long catalogue in that very place (Rule of St. Benedict, ch. 4). For what does Solomon say of the unstable man? "As a bird that wanders from her nest, so is the man who abandons his place" (Prov. XXVII, 8). The turtledove had found a nest where she might lay her young; she had begun to warm it and to be warmed by it; the young had come even to the point of birth, and behold, she flies away, and leaves the work she had begun unfinished. Why, whence, or whither she flies away, let her see to it herself; or when she will make up for the many losses which she meanwhile suffers; or by what reasoning she will excuse her broken first fidelity. For I would not think it a matter of good counsel, for an uncertain hope, to undergo certain losses; although the progress of certain persons bids me meanwhile restrain my judgment from precipitate haste.

3. Enimvero multum dissimilis est causa eorum qui amore sapientiae inquieti fiunt, et eorum de quibus loqui coeperam, qui levi et frivola permoti causa a sapientia resiliunt. Sicut enim per patientiam disciplinis sapientiae quis immoratur, ut discat sapientiam; sic qui facile perdunt sustinentiam, non demorabuntur, ut scriptum est, projicere illam. Quid autem eos offendat, praemisit Scriptura, cum ait: Quasi lapidis virtus, probatio erit in illis (Eccli. VI, 22). Offenderunt in lapidem offensionis et petram scandali, cujus probata virtus corripiebat et erudiebat insipientes, probabatque mentes; cum ipsi virtutem sapientiae duritiam lapidis interpretarentur, duraque omnia, disciplinam, vultum, sermonem causarentur. Durus est, inquiunt, hic sermo (Joan. VI, 61). Esto, sermo durus est; numquid non verus est? Lapis durus est; nunquid non pretiosus est? Quare autem veritas dura est tibi, nisi propter duritiam cordis tui? si cor tibi pietate mollesceret, plus tibi soliditas veritatis quam vanitas mendacii vel oleum adulationis placeret. Durus est, aiebant, hic sermo, quia quasi lapidis virtus erat illis probatio sapientiae; ideoque non sunt demorati eam projicere, et retro abire. Nec ob aliud pretiosum et electum a Deo lapidem reprobaverunt, nisi quia durum putaverunt. Et erat quidem petra Christus, sed virtute, non duritia. Erat petra, sed quae converti posset, imo et conversa est in stagna vel fontes aquarum, ubi mollia et humilia, quibus influeret, invenit corda fidelium. Nam et isti, qui tam cito offensi quadam specie duritiae retro abierunt, si permansissent cum Apostolis, bibissent forsitan et ipsi cum eis de consequenti eos Petra, bibissent flumina aquae vivae de petra percussa in cruce fluentis largissime, ita ut hodieque bibat populus et jumenta; imo suxissent etiam mel de petra, oleumque de saxo durissimo.

English translation

3. Indeed, the case of those who become restless out of love of wisdom is very different from that of those I had begun to speak of, who, moved by some light and frivolous cause, spring back from wisdom. For just as a man dwells patiently under the discipline of wisdom in order to learn wisdom, so those who quickly lose their endurance will not delay, as it is written, to cast it away. And Scripture has already told us what offends them, saying: "As the virtue of a stone, so shall the trial be in them" (Eccli. VI, 22). They stumbled against the stone of stumbling and the rock of scandal, whose proven virtue was correcting and instructing the foolish, and testing minds; while they themselves interpreted the virtue of wisdom as the hardness of a stone, and complained that everything—the discipline, the countenance, the word—was hard. "This is a hard saying," they said (Joan. VI, 61). Granted, the word is hard; but is it not true? A stone is hard; but is it not precious? Why then is the truth hard to you, unless because of the hardness of your heart? If your heart were softened by devotion, the solidity of truth would please you more than the vanity of falsehood or the oil of flattery. "This is a hard saying," they said, because the trial of wisdom was to them as it were the virtue of a stone; and therefore they did not delay to cast it away and turn back. Nor for any other reason did they reject the precious and chosen stone of God, except that they thought it hard. And indeed Christ was the Rock—but by virtue, not by hardness. He was a Rock, but one that could be turned—indeed, was turned—into pools or fountains of water, where it found the soft and humble hearts of the faithful into which it could flow. For these men too, who so quickly took offense at a certain appearance of hardness and turned back, if they had remained with the Apostles, would perhaps have drunk themselves, with them, of the Rock that followed them; they would have drunk most abundantly of the rivers of living water flowing from the Rock struck upon the Cross, so that even today the people and the cattle drink of it; indeed, they would even have sucked honey from the rock, and oil from the hardest stone.

4. Prorsus tu beatus es, Simon Bar-Jona, tibi jam revelabat Pater pietatem mysterii, latentem, ut videbatur, sub duritia sermonis, qui, interrogatis duodecim, an et ipsi vellent abire, constanter respondisti: Domine, verba vitae aeternae habes; ad quem ibimus? (Joan. VI, 69.) Plane beatus, qui cum sapientia morari, et cum domesticis ejus ad mensam ipsius pane interim Sacramenti nutriri delegisti; donec de fide proficientem ad intellectum pane vitae et intellectus te cibaret, et aqua sapientiae salutaris potaret. Beati et vos, fratres mei, qui in disciplinam sapientiae, et christianae scholam philosophiae nomina dedistis, sed, si perseveranter in sapientia moremini; ut cum durior sermo ipsius visus fuerit, duriora scilicet praecipientis aut durius corripientis, non sit in aliquo vestrum cor malum incredulitatis discedendi a Deo vivo, sed constanter dicatis cum Apostolo: Verba vitae aeternae habes; ad quem ibimus? Fingis quidem laborem in praecepto (Psal. XCIII, 20), duritiam in sermone; sed scimus quia magna est multitudo dulcedinis tuae, Domine, quam abscondisti timentibus te (Psal. XXX, 20), perficies sperantibus in te. Ego autem semper sperabo, etiamsi occideris me. Imo tunc amplius sperabo, cum flagellaveris, secueris, usseris, occideris quidquid vivebat in me; ut non ego, sed Christus vivat in me. Omnino non discedimus a te, quia vivificabis nos etiam occidens, sanabis percutiens. Vere beatus qui cum sapientia moratur, ac constantia et fide longanimiter patiens et fideliter obediens usque ad mortem, nec deserens locum suum, quotiescunque spiritus potestatem habentis ascenderit super eum, sciens quia curatio disciplinae faciet cessare maxima peccata (Eccle. X, 4).

English translation

4. Truly you are blessed, Simon Bar-Jona; to you already the Father was revealing the graciousness of the mystery hidden, as it seemed, beneath the hardness of the word—you who, when the twelve were asked whether they too wished to go away, answered steadfastly: "Lord, thou hast the words of eternal life; to whom shall we go?" (Joan. VI, 69.). Truly blessed are you, who chose to dwell with wisdom, and to be nourished for a time at her table among her household with the bread of the Sacrament; until, as you advanced from faith to understanding, she fed you with the bread of life and understanding, and gave you to drink the water of saving wisdom. Blessed also are you, my brothers, who have given your names to the discipline of wisdom and to the Christian school of philosophy—but only if you dwell perseveringly in wisdom; so that when her word seems harder—that is, when her precepts are harder, or her correction more severe—there may not be in any of you an evil heart of unbelief, departing from the living God, but that you may say steadfastly with the Apostle: "Thou hast the words of eternal life; to whom shall we go?" You indeed fashion labor in your precept (Psal. XCIII, 20), hardness in your word; but we know that great is the multitude of your sweetness, O Lord, which you have hidden for those who fear you (Psal. XXX, 20), and which you will perfect for those who hope in you. And I will always hope, even if you should slay me. Indeed, I will hope the more when you have scourged, cut, burned, and slain whatever was living in me; so that it is not I, but Christ who lives in me. We will in no way depart from you, for you will give us life even by killing us, and heal us even by striking us. Truly blessed is the one who dwells with wisdom, and who, with constancy and faith, patiently enduring and faithfully obeying even unto death, does not desert his place, as often as the spirit of the one having power rises up against him, knowing that "the remedy of discipline shall make the greatest sins to cease" (Eccle. X, 4).

5. Sane ad hanc sapientiam morandi in sapientia, illud praecipue pertinere arbitror, ut inquietudo vel quaelibet levis molestia non facile quodcunque opus sapientiae nobis excutiat, verbi gratia, solemnem psalmodiam, orationem, lectionem divinam, pensum operis diurni, aut silentii disciplinam. Quia enim sapientia in exitu canitur: Gaudebunt, ait sanctus, labia mea, cum cantavero tibi Psal LXX, 231), dum etiam ad litteram, exitus matutini et vespere delectabis (Psal. LXIV, 9.) De oratione quoque certum et ipsi quotidie capitis experimentum, quod melior est finis orationis quam principium; ut fidele probetis dominicum illud toties inculcatum, tot exemplis commendatum de orationis perseverantia consilium. Jam legere et negligere, vel, antequam ceperis librum, de manibus projicere, quem fructum, putas, tibi poterit parere? Si Scripturae immoratus non fueris, ut assiduitate studii ei fias familiaris, quando, putas, se tibi revelabit? Qui habet, inquit, amorem verbi, dabitur ei intellectus, et abundabit; qui vero non habet, et quod habet per naturam, auferetur ab eo (Matth. XIII, 12; XXV, 29) propter negligentiam. De opere autem manuum, nonne et de isto satis comperistis quia sicut denarius operariis, sic consolatio saepius servatur in fine operis? De silentio quippe nota est promissio prophetica, quia in silentio et spe erit, inquit, fortitudo vestra (Isai. XXX, 15). Si enim colas justitiam in silentio, et, sicut Jeremias bonum esse ait, praestoleris cum silentio salutare Domini; in medio silentio secretus tibi illabetur a regalibus sedibus omnipotens sermo, et aquae Siloe, quae vadunt cum silentio, convallem quieti ac placidi pectoris grato rigabunt profluvio. Hoc non semel, sed saepius experieris, si modo silentium tuum cultus justitiae sit (Isai. XXXII, 17), id est, si in justitia mediteris, ut perseveres in Scriptura quam proposui, et in sensu cogites circumspectionem Dei.

English translation

5. Indeed, I think this belongs especially to that wisdom of abiding in wisdom: that restlessness, or any slight annoyance, should not easily shake us loose from whatever work of wisdom we are engaged in—for example, the solemn chanting of the psalms, prayer, divine reading, the task of the day's work, or the discipline of silence. For wisdom is sung at its conclusion: 'My lips shall rejoice,' says the saint, 'when I shall sing to thee' Psal LXX, 231); as also, according to the letter, 'thou shalt make the outgoings of the morning and the evening to be joyful' (Psal. LXIV, 9.). Concerning prayer also, you yourselves receive daily a sure proof that the end of prayer is better than its beginning; so that you may prove faithful that counsel of the Lord, so often urged, commended by so many examples, concerning perseverance in prayer. Now what fruit, do you think, can it bear you, to read and then neglect what you have read, or to throw the book out of your hands almost before you have taken it up? If you do not linger over Scripture, so that by diligent study you become familiar with it, when, do you suppose, will it reveal itself to you? 'He who has,' it says, 'love of the word, to him shall understanding be given, and he shall have abundance; but he who has not, even what he has by nature shall be taken away from him' (Matth. XIII, 12; XXV, 29), on account of negligence. As for the work of the hands, have you not also learned well enough from this, that just as the wage is kept for the laborers, so consolation is more often reserved for the end of the work? Concerning silence, indeed, the prophetic promise is well known: 'In silence and in hope shall your strength be,' he says (Isai. XXX, 15). For if you cultivate justice in silence, and, as Jeremiah says it is good, wait with silence for the salvation of the Lord, then in the midst of silence the almighty Word will come down secretly to you from the royal throne, and the waters of Siloam, which flow with silence, will water the valley of a quiet and peaceful breast with their pleasant stream. This you will experience not once but often, provided only that your silence be the cultivation of justice (Isai. XXXII, 17); that is, if you meditate in justice, so that you persevere in the Scripture I have set before you, and in your understanding think upon the circumspection of God.

6. Haec meditare, in his esto, ut profectus tuus manifestus fiat. Si enim iniquitatem mediteris in cubili tuo, malitias scilicet quas malignus immittit, aut phantasias quas cor tuum parturit, aut inanes philosophias, seu fallaces theorias, quae sunt velut aegri somnia; nonne magis injustitiae quam justitiae cultus erit silentium tuum? Si ergo vis in sapientia morari, stude in justitia meditari. Concupisti, inquit, sapientiam? conserva justitiam, et Dominus praebebit illam tibi (Eccli. I, 33). Si autem quasi violente2 irruunt cogitationes noxiae, pone tibi virilem et fidelem custodiam, quae omni custodia servet cor tuum. Dico autem timorem Dei, qui nihil negligit, qui nullum indiscussum intrare permittit, saepius interrogans etiam angelum lucis: Noster es, an adversariorum? (Josue V, 13.) Undique enim sibi circumspicit, tanquam omni hora cogitans circumspectionem Dei, quem sine intermissione intueri ac judicare corda filiorum hominum attendit. Pulchre autem dictum est: In sensu cogitabit (Eccli. XIV, 22); quia prorsus sine sensu est et a corde excessit qui timorem Dei cogitare negligit, qui pondus tantae majestatis ac judicii sibi incumbentis non sentit. Signanter quoque circumspicere Deus dicitur, quia omnia tam praeterita quam futura habet praesentia, ut nec ista respiciat, nec illa prospiciat, sed similiter, quia simpliciter, cuncta circumspiciat; sitque illa aeternitas velut punctum, omnium media temporalium, cujus immobili simplicitati semper aequaliter praesens est circuitus et rota temporum. Hunc oculum aeternum, sine intermissione videntem et judicantem omnia, semper cogitat, suaque se cogitatione sollicitat timor Domini, qui revocat a malis non solum operibus, sed et cogitationibus; erudiens nos, ut justitiam potius meditemur; continens, ut cum sapientia moremur. Ita enim sensim fit ut qui primo coercetur timore judicii et poenae, postea pascatur amore ac meditatione justitiae, demumque requiescat ac delectetur in convictu et amplexu sapientiae. Haec non modo foras mittit timorem, diffundendo charitatem; sed etiam pellit ab animo taedium et langorem, infundendo suavitatem, sicut quidam, contubernium habens ipsius, de ea loquebatur: Intrans in domum meam conquiescam cum illa; non enim habet amaritudinem conversatio illius, nec taedium convictus illius, sed laetitiam et gaudium (Sap. VIII, 16). Horum participes nos faciat, qui particeps nostri fieri dignatus est Dei sapientia Christus Jesus, qui vivit et regnat per omnia saecula saeculorum. Amen.

English translation

6. Meditate on these things, be occupied in them, that your progress may be manifest. For if you meditate on iniquity in your bed—that is, the malicious thoughts which the evil one injects, or the fancies which your own heart brings forth, or empty philosophies, or deceptive speculations, which are like the dreams of a sick man—will not your silence then be the cultivation of injustice rather than of justice? If, therefore, you wish to abide in wisdom, strive to meditate in justice. 'Have you desired wisdom?' it says. 'Keep justice, and the Lord will give it to you' (Eccli. I, 33). But if harmful thoughts rush upon you as though by force2, set for yourself a manly and faithful guard, which may keep your heart with all watchfulness. I mean the fear of God, which neglects nothing, which allows nothing to enter unexamined, often questioning even an angel of light: 'Art thou one of ours, or of the adversaries?' (Josue V, 13.). For it looks about on every side, as though at every hour thinking upon the circumspection of God, whom it attends as beholding without ceasing and judging the hearts of the children of men. It is beautifully said: 'He shall think with understanding' (Eccli. XIV, 22); for he is utterly without sense, and has gone out of his heart, who neglects to think upon the fear of God, who does not feel the weight of so great a majesty and of the judgment pressing upon him. God is also fittingly said to 'look around,' because he holds all things, past as well as future, as present to him, so that he neither looks back upon the one nor looks forward to the other, but looks around upon all things equally, because simply; and that eternity of his is, as it were, a point in the midst of all temporal things, to whose unmoving simplicity the circling wheel of the ages is ever equally present. Upon this eternal eye, seeing and judging all things without ceasing, the fear of the Lord continually thinks, and by its own thought disquiets itself—that fear which calls us back from evils not only in deeds but also in thoughts; teaching us rather to meditate on justice, restraining us, that we may abide with wisdom. For thus it comes about by degrees that he who is first held in check by fear of judgment and punishment is afterward fed by love and meditation of justice, and at last rests and delights in the companionship and embrace of wisdom. This wisdom not only casts out fear, by pouring forth charity, but also drives weariness and listlessness from the soul, by infusing sweetness—just as a certain man, who had companionship with her, spoke of her: 'Entering into my house, I will take my repose with her; for her company has no bitterness, nor her companionship any tediousness, but joy and gladness' (Sap. VIII, 16). May he make us partakers of these things, who deigned to become a partaker of us—the Wisdom of God, Christ Jesus, who lives and reigns for ever and ever. Amen.
