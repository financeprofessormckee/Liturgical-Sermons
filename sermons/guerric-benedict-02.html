Introduction & Scripture references

Editorial introduction

Trust in God gives steadiness amid tribulation and the loss of spiritual consolation. Guerric develops the image of a tree rooted in living water through Scripture, compunction and the sacraments. The sermon appears in the 1879 edition of Patrologia Latina under the feast of Saint Benedict. The collection is associated approximately with Guerric's abbacy in 1138–1157; the individual sermon is undated.

English translation

On Trust in God.

¶cols. 103-104
Latin original

1. Benedictus vir qui confidit in Domino (Jerem. XVII, 7). Sanctus Pater noster gratia Benedictus et nomine, cujus memoria in benedictione est, ipse profecto fuit benedictus vir qui confisus est in Domino. Quem enim Dominus praevenit in benedictionibus dulcedinis, ut in Domino confideret, jam benedixit omni benedictione spirituali in coelestibus in Christo, quia in Domino confisus est. Nec solum in coelestibus benedictionem omnium angelorum, sed etiam in terrestribus benedictionem omnium gentium dedit illi Dominus. Ubi enim gentium non benedicetur hodie benedictus Domini Benedictus? Plane benedictio Domini super caput justi (Prov. X, 6), quem tot benedictionibus coeli et terrae gratia Dei cumulavit. Non tamen, sicut Esau, in pinguedine terrae et in rore coeli benedictio ejus (Gen. XXVII, 28); sed in pinguedine spiritus et in rore coeli, qui per prophetam loquitur: Ero quasi ros (Osee XIV, 6), et cui dicitur: Ros lucis, ros tuus (Isai. XXVI, 19). In Christo utique benedictio Patris, cui Pater imprecatus est: Qui benedixerit tibi, benedictionibus repleatur (Gen. XXVII, 29). Merito proinde benedictus in Domino vir qui confidit in Domino, quia qui in Domino fidit, in eo se ipsum figit. Ubi autem arbor radicem figit, inde succum vitae atque humorem pinguedinis bibit. Plane ad humorem radices misit, qui, ut verbis utar Benedicti magistri, spem suam Deo commisit, ac de ipso fonte summi boni aquas vitae totius benedictionis et gratiae bibit (Reg. S. Bened. cap. 4).

English translation

1. Blessed is the man who trusts in the Lord (Jerem. XVII, 7). Our holy Father, Benedict both in grace and in name, whose memory is in benediction, was indeed himself a blessed man who trusted in the Lord. For him whom the Lord went before with the blessings of sweetness, so that he might trust in the Lord, the Lord had already blessed with every spiritual blessing in the heavenly places in Christ, because he trusted in the Lord. And the Lord gave him not only, in heavenly things, the blessing of all the angels, but also, in earthly things, the blessing of all nations. For in what nation today is Benedict, the Lord's blessed one, not blessed? Truly the blessing of the Lord is upon the head of the just man (Prov. X, 6), whom the grace of God has heaped with so many blessings of heaven and earth. Yet his blessing was not, like Esau's, in the fatness of the earth and the dew of heaven (Gen. XXVII, 28); but in the fatness of the spirit and the dew of heaven, who speaks through the prophet: I will be as the dew (Osee XIV, 6), and to whom it is said: The dew of light is your dew (Isai. XXVI, 19). In Christ indeed is the blessing of the Father, for whom the Father invoked this blessing: May he who blesses you be filled with blessings (Gen. XXVII, 29). Rightly, then, is the man blessed in the Lord who trusts in the Lord, because he who trusts in the Lord fixes himself in him. Now where a tree fixes its root, from there it drinks the sap of life and the moisture of richness. Clearly he sent his roots toward that moisture who, to use the words of our master Benedict, 'committed his hope to God,' and drank from that very fount of the highest good the waters of life of all blessing and grace (Rule of St. Benedict, ch. 4).

2. Per hanc nempe piam et fidelem fiduciam remittuntur peccata; sanitatum corporalium, sed magis spiritualium, obtinentur remedia; propulsantur pericula; terrores contemnuntur; vincitur mundus; omnia denique possibilia credenti redduntur. Illi quippe qui in peccatis erat: Confide, ait, fili, remittuntur tibi peccata tua. Illis quibus donabatur sanitas corporum vel animarum: Secundum fidem tuam fiat tibi; et: Fides tua te salvum fecit (Matth. IX, 2, 29, 22). Illis, qui periculo terrebantur naufragandi: Habete, inquit, fidem Dei (Marc. XI, 22); et: Quid timidi estis, modicae fidei? (Matth. VIII, 26.) Illis quos armabat adversus saevitiam mundi furoresque diaboli: Confidite, ait; ego vici mundum (Joan. XVI, 33). Et utique haec est victoria quae vincit mundum, fides nostra (I Joan. V, 4), si modo non sit tepida, non trepida; sed sit fiducia, id est fides non ficta, seu spes non infirma. Non solum autem mundus per eam vincitur, sed et coelum possidetur, super aeternitatem homo stabilitur, et in Deo ipso per charitatem radicatur et fundatur. Qui enim confidit in Domino, est sicut mons Sion; non commovebitur in aeternum, qui fundatus est super Aeternum (Psal. CXXIV, 1). Tam enim perire non potest, quam nec ille cui adhaerens unus cum ipso spiritus est. Quis enim speravit in Domino, et confusus est, permansit in mandatis illius, et derelictus est? (Eccli. II, 11, 12.) Si dixerit infidelis, Ille derelictus erat qui in cruce clamabat: Deus, Deus meus, utquid me dereliquisti? (Matth. XXVII, 46.) puto quia non usquequaque dereliquit, in quo erat mundum reconcilians sibi. O quanta consolatio, sic desolari; quanta dilectio, sic derelinqui, ut Unico Patris unice dilecto merearis vel in passionibus consociari!

English translation

2. By this devout and faithful trust, indeed, sins are forgiven; remedies are obtained for bodily health, but even more for spiritual health; dangers are driven off; terrors are held in contempt; the world is overcome; and finally, all things become possible to the one who believes. For to the one who was in sins, he said: Have confidence, son, your sins are forgiven you. To those to whom health of body or soul was granted: According to your faith be it done to you; and: Your faith has made you whole (Matth. IX, 2, 29, 22). To those who were terrified by the danger of shipwreck, he said: Have the faith of God (Marc. XI, 22); and: Why are you fearful, O you of little faith? (Matth. VIII, 26.) To those whom he armed against the savagery of the world and the ragings of the devil, he said: Have confidence; I have overcome the world (Joan. XVI, 33). And indeed this is the victory that overcomes the world, our faith (I Joan. V, 4), provided only that it be not lukewarm, not fearful; but be trust—that is, faith not feigned, or hope not weak. Not only is the world overcome through it, but heaven is also possessed, man is made stable upon eternity, and he is rooted and grounded in God himself through charity. For he who trusts in the Lord is like Mount Sion; he shall not be moved forever, who is founded upon the Eternal One (Psal. CXXIV, 1). For he can no more perish than can the one to whom, clinging to him, he is made one spirit with him. For who has hoped in the Lord, and been confounded? Who has remained in his commandments, and been forsaken? (Eccli. II, 11, 12.) Should the unbeliever say: He was forsaken who cried out on the cross: My God, my God, why have you forsaken me? (Matth. XXVII, 46.) — I think that he did not altogether forsake him, in whom he was reconciling the world to himself. O how great a consolation, thus to be desolated; how great a love, thus to be forsaken, that you might merit to be associated even in his sufferings with the Only-begotten of the Father, the uniquely beloved!

3. Aperi, Domine, oculos hujus pueri, rudem loquor et novitium, cui, cum tribulatur, putat se derelictum. Novum hoc omnino et insolitum, si justum quempiam viderem derelictum, cum Ecclesia dicat: Junior fui, etenim senui, et non vidi justum derelictum (Psal. XXXVI, 25). Aperi, inquam, o Domine, oculos pueri tui, ut videat quia plures nobiscum sunt quam cum adversariis. Siquidem Dominus virtutum nobiscum, et cum eo omnis virtus et militia coeli, imo favor omnis creaturae, servientis nutui et arbitrio Creatoris sui. Si enim Deus pro nobis, quis contra nos? (Rom. VIII, 31.) et quis est qui nobis noceat, si boni aemulatores fuerimus? (I Petr. III, 13.) Poterit invidus ille saevire; sed hoc erit servire. Poterit urere; tundere, dissecare; sed hoc erit coronas fabricare. Diligam te, Domine, fortitudo mea, per quem mihi vermiculo succumbit omnis fortitudo contraria, et illuditur ab angelis tuis, in ministerium nostrum missis, antiqui draconis astutia, ut, volens nocere, prosit. Diligam te, Domine, propugnator potens, gubernator prudens, consolator clemens, remunerator affluens. Securus in eum projicio omnem sollicitudinem meam, cujus nec potentia vinci, nec sapientia falli, nec benevolentia potest fatigari quominus expleat omnem indigentiam meam. Quam satius, quam securius est ipsum esse sollicitum, quam me ipsum pro me! quam bonum est confidere in Domino, quam in homine! quam jure maledictus qui confidit in homine, carnem ponit brachium suum, et recedit a Domino cor ejus! (Jerem. XVII, 5.) et quidem egenus et pauper ego sum; sed si Dominus sollicitus est mei, dives sum, beatus sum; cui nimirum omnia cooperantur in bonum. Sperent igitur in te qui noverunt nomen tuum, quoniam non derelinquis sperantes in te, Domine (Psal. IX, 11); sedeatque populus tuus, sicut scriptum est, in pulchritudine pacis, in tabernaculis fiduciae, in requie opulenta, in securitate sempiterna (Isai. XXXII, 18, 17). Omnino pulchra pax et securitas sempiterna, habitare in adjutorio Altissimi, in protectione Dei coeli commorari. Prorsus requies opulenta, sedere feriatum sub vite vera, ficu et oliva; et post saturitatem variorum fructuum, se ipsum mulcere carmine amatorio, ac dicere: Sub umbra illius quam desiderabam [Vulg. quem desideraveram] sedi, et fructus illius dulcis gutturi meo? (Cant. II, 51.) Plane dulcis gutturi fructus de cujus gustu tam dulcis erumpit ructus.

English translation

3. Open, Lord, the eyes of this child—I speak of one unformed and a novice—who, when he is afflicted, thinks himself abandoned. This would be altogether new and unheard of, were I to see any just man abandoned, since the Church says: "I was young, and now I am old, and I have not seen the just man abandoned" (Psal. XXXVI, 25). Open, I say, O Lord, the eyes of your servant, that he may see that there are more with us than with our adversaries. For the Lord of hosts is with us, and with him every power and the army of heaven, indeed the favor of every creature, serving the nod and will of its Creator. For if God is for us, who is against us? (Rom. VIII, 31.) And who is there to harm us, if we are zealous for what is good? (I Petr. III, 13.) That envious one may rage; but this will be to serve us. He may burn, beat, cut apart; but this will be to fashion crowns. I will love you, Lord, my strength, through whom every opposing strength yields to me, a mere worm, and the cunning of the ancient dragon is mocked by your angels, sent for our service, so that, wishing to do harm, it profits us. I will love you, Lord, mighty defender, prudent governor, gentle comforter, abundant rewarder. Secure, I cast all my anxiety upon him, whose power cannot be conquered, whose wisdom cannot be deceived, whose benevolence cannot be wearied from supplying all my need. How much better, how much more secure it is that he himself be anxious for me, than that I should be anxious for myself! How good it is to trust in the Lord, rather than in man! How justly cursed is he who trusts in man, and makes flesh his arm, and whose heart withdraws from the Lord! (Jerem. XVII, 5.) And indeed I am needy and poor; but if the Lord is anxious for me, I am rich, I am blessed; for to such a one, surely, all things work together for good. Let those, then, hope in you who know your name, for you do not abandon those who hope in you, O Lord (Psal. IX, 11); and let your people sit, as it is written, in the beauty of peace, in the tabernacles of confidence, in opulent rest, in everlasting security (Isai. XXXII, 18, 17). Truly it is a beautiful peace and an everlasting security, to dwell in the help of the Most High, to abide under the protection of the God of heaven. Truly it is an opulent rest, to sit at leisure beneath the true vine, the fig tree, and the olive; and, after being filled with manifold fruits, to soothe oneself with a love-song, and to say: "Under his shadow, which I desired [Vulg.: whom I had desired], I sat, and his fruit was sweet to my throat" (Cant. II, 51.). Truly sweet to the throat is that fruit, from the tasting of which so sweet an eructation breaks forth.

4. Haec sunt ergo tabernacula fiduciae, in quibus verus Israel habitat confidenter, pascit et cubat, et non est qui exterreat; et, sicut Sapientia promittit, Requiescet absque terrore, abundantia perfruetur, malorum timore sublato (Prov. I, 33). Et bene timore malorum sublato, non timore Domini; ne fiducia ista et securitate, quam commendamus, negligentiam putes generari, cum non in alio quam in timore Domini sit fiducia fortitudinis. Timor quippe, dum cavet offensam, custodit gratiam; locumque servat fiduciae, quandiu tibi conscius non es Dominum offendisse. Inde fit ut nonnisi caste timeas eum; praeter eum nullatenus timeas alienum. Sola ergo conscientia bona fiduciam istam praesumit, suadet corda nostra esse in conspectu Judicis aeterni. Quis enim bene sibi de illo promittat, a quo se minime diligi intelligat? Qui autem diligit, nunquam diffidit, sciens quia is quem corda non fallunt, diligentes se diligit, etiam cum arguit et corripit. Siquidem eruditis corde in sapientia non aufert, sed auget fiduciam Patris disciplina, scientes scriptum, Beatus qui corripitur a Domino (Job V, 17), et: Ego quos amo arguo et castigo (Apoc. III, 19; Prov. III, 12). Cum igitur exarserit in brevi ira ejus, beati omnes qui confidunt in eo (Psal. II, 13), quos scilicet amoris proprii conscientia consolatur, quia, cum iratus fuerit, misericordiae recordabitur; et, cum dixerint: Confitebor tibi, Domine, quoniam iratus es mihi, statim subsequentur: Conversus est furor tuus, et consolatus es me (Isa. XII, 1), eo quod nonnisi in brevi exarserit ira ejus, quam tam cito placaverit confessio reatus.

English translation

4. These, then, are the tabernacles of confidence, in which the true Israel dwells securely, feeds and lies down, and there is none to terrify him; and, as Wisdom promises, "He shall rest without terror, and shall enjoy abundance, the fear of evils being removed" (Prov. I, 33). And rightly is it "the fear of evils" that is removed, not the fear of the Lord; lest you should think that this confidence and security which we commend breeds negligence, since the confidence of strength lies in nothing other than the fear of the Lord. For fear, while it guards against giving offense, preserves grace, and keeps a place open for confidence, so long as you are not conscious of having offended the Lord. Hence it follows that you should fear him alone chastely; and besides him you should in no way fear any other. It is a good conscience alone, then, that lays claim to this confidence, persuading our hearts that they stand in the sight of the eternal Judge. For who could rightly promise himself good from one by whom he understands himself to be in no way loved? But he who loves never distrusts, knowing that he whom hearts cannot deceive loves those who love him, even when he rebukes and corrects them. Indeed, for those whose hearts are instructed in wisdom, the Father's discipline does not take away but increases confidence, since they know it is written: "Blessed is the man whom the Lord corrects" (Job V, 17), and: "Those whom I love, I rebuke and chastise" (Apoc. III, 19; Prov. III, 12). When, therefore, his anger has been kindled but for a short while, blessed are all they who trust in him (Psal. II, 13), namely those whom the awareness of their own love consoles, because, when he has been angry, he will remember mercy; and when they shall have said: "I will give thanks to you, O Lord, for you were angry with me," there shall at once follow: "Your wrath is turned away, and you have comforted me" (Isa. XII, 1), because his anger was kindled only for a short while, which the confession of guilt so quickly appeased.

5. Merito igitur benedictum virum qui confidit in Domino ligno comparat Jeremias quod transplantatur super aquas: quod quia ad humorem amoris mittit radices cordis, non timebit cum venerit aestus irae et tribulationis; sed et in tempore siccitatis, cum multo tempore coelum clausum fuerit, nec ros nec pluvia gratiae super eum descenderit, non erit tamen sollicitum, quasi Deus eum projecerit (Jerem. XVII, 7, 8). Sentit quippe se plantatum fide, radicatum charitate super aquas vitae, quae, secundum Ezechielem, de sanctuario egredientes vivificant omnia, et habent in utraque ripa omne lignum pomiferum, cujus folium non defluit, nec fructus deficit (Ezech. XLVII, 3-12). Cur igitur lignum illud benedictum aut timeret aestum, aut de siccitate esset sollicitum, cui aqua viva, id est Spiritus sancti gratia, vitalem in occulto succum spei et charitatis subministrare non desinit; unde et folium sit viride, id est Verbum plenum gratiae et veritatis; et ipsum non desinat aliquando facere fructum quodcunque opus pietatis? Et grata quidem illa verna temperies, pacis et laetitiae; desiderabilis illa pluvia voluntaria, quam segregavit Deus haereditati suae; sed, si ita necesse est, adurat omnia aestus tribulationis, veniant iterum quaecunque prophetavit Jeremias de sermonibus siccitatis spiritualis; non tamen timebit, quem fiducia Domini radicavit super aquam refectionis (Jerem. XVII, 6, 8), id est gratiam Spiritus sancti; quae etsi manifeste non pluat, ut sensibiliter influat, occulte tamen interius vivificat et fecundat; quandiu videlicet fidem propositi conservat, ad perseverandum confortat, verbumque irreprehensible et opus stabile subministrat.

English translation

5. Rightly, then, does Jeremiah compare the blessed man who trusts in the Lord to a tree that is transplanted beside the waters: because it sends the roots of its heart into the moisture of love, it will not fear when the heat of wrath and tribulation comes; but even in time of drought, when the sky has long been shut up, and neither dew nor rain of grace has descended upon it, it will nevertheless not be anxious, as though God had cast it off (Jerem. XVII, 7, 8). For it feels itself planted by faith, rooted by charity, upon the waters of life, which, according to Ezekiel, going forth from the sanctuary, give life to all things, and have on either bank every fruit-bearing tree, whose leaf does not fall nor its fruit fail (Ezech. XLVII, 3-12). Why, then, should that blessed tree either fear the heat, or be anxious about drought, since the living water—that is, the grace of the Holy Spirit—does not cease to supply it, in secret, with the vital sap of hope and charity; whence its leaf also is green, that is, the Word full of grace and truth; and it itself never at any time ceases to bear fruit, whatever work of piety it may be? Pleasant indeed is that springtime mildness of peace and joy; desirable is that bountiful rain which God has set apart for his inheritance; but if it must needs be so, let the heat of tribulation scorch everything, let there come again whatever Jeremiah prophesied concerning words of spiritual drought; still he will not fear, whom trust in the Lord has rooted upon the water of refreshment (Jerem. XVII, 6, 8), that is, the grace of the Holy Spirit; which, even if it does not rain down openly so as to flow in perceptibly, nevertheless secretly gives life and fruitfulness within—so long, that is, as it preserves the faith of one's purpose, strengthens him to persevere, and supplies a blameless word and a stable work.

6. In hujusmodi vestra, fratres, sit consolatio, quoties alias consolationes non modo carnales, sed et spirituales subtraxerit vobis non negligentia vestra, sed dispensatio divina. Et fortasse quidem illa, quam diximus, occulta gratia Spiritus irriguum est inferius, quo nimirum Axa non vult esse contenta, nisi adjiciatur ei et irriguum superius (Josue XV, 19); ut scilicet de excelso effundatur Spiritus, rorent coeli desuper, et nubes angelicae pluant justum, justificans Dei verbum, loquens ad cor Jerusalem. Et bene illud irriguum inferius, quod quasi ad radices fluens nutrit humilitatem; hoc autem superius, quod sese desuper effundens, spe et exsultatione attollit mentem. Si ergo et vos, fratres, ad illud suspiratis superius irriguum, laudabile quidem desiderium; si tamen nondum attingitis, mittite radices interim ad humorem, salubre remedium. Qui igitur non praevalet capere laetitiam contemplativae, meditetur justitiam vitae activae. Inde radices affectionum impinguet, dulcoret mores, omnemque vitae statum informet, quatenus in ipso nec marcescat aut defluat folium, id est verbum leviter aut inutiliter prolatum, nec aliquando vita desinat facere fructum. Benedictum lignum, cujus folium ad medicinam, fructus ad vitam; id est, cujus sermo gratiam parit audienti, et opus vitam facienti.

English translation

6. Let your consolation, brothers, be of this kind, whenever other consolations—not only carnal but also spiritual—are withdrawn from you, not through your own negligence, but by divine dispensation. And perhaps indeed that hidden grace of the Spirit of which we spoke is the lower watering, with which Axa surely does not wish to be content, unless the upper watering also be added to her (Josue XV, 19); so that, namely, the Spirit may be poured out from on high, the heavens may drop dew from above, and the clouds of angels may rain down the Just One, the justifying Word of God, speaking to the heart of Jerusalem. And rightly is that called the lower watering, which, flowing as it were to the roots, nourishes humility; but this the upper watering, which, pouring itself out from above, lifts up the mind with hope and exultation. If, then, you too, brothers, sigh after that upper watering, it is indeed a praiseworthy desire; yet if you do not yet attain it, in the meantime send your roots down to the moisture—a wholesome remedy. Let him, then, who cannot grasp the joy of the contemplative life, meditate upon the righteousness of the active life. From this let him fatten the roots of his affections, sweeten his habits, and shape his whole manner of life, so that in him the leaf may neither wither nor fall away—that is, no word spoken lightly or uselessly—nor may his life ever cease to bear fruit. Blessed is the tree whose leaf is for healing and whose fruit is for life; that is, whose speech brings forth grace for the hearer, and whose work brings forth life for the one who does it.

7. Ad hoc profecto vos, fratres mei, quos carnalis generatio vel consuetudo saecularis plantaverat in terra inaquosa, in terra salsuginis, divina generatio vel mutatio dexterae Excelsi transplantavit super aquas refectionis; ut quibus prius propter sterilitatem parabatur securis et ignis, nunc plantati in domo Domini, in atriis domus Dei nostri, floreatis, et fructum faciatis, et fructus vester maneat. An non aquae refectionis Scripturae Spiritus sancti, in quibus meditamur die ac nocte? an non aquae refectionis, lacrymae compunctionis, quae nobis factae sunt panes die ac nocte? an non aquae refectionis, Sacramenta et subsidia nostrae salutis, quibus pascimur et potamur in altari? In his siquidem omnibus, quasi quibusdam rivulis, fons sapientiae qui oritur in medio paradisi, derivatur foris, et in plateis aquas suas dividit. Ego, inquit Sapientia, quasi aquaeductus exivi de paradiso. Dixi: Rigabo hortum meum plantationum, inebriabo partus mei fructum (Eccli. XXIV, 41, 42). Ecce, ut colligere potestis ex ipsius ore Sapientiae parientis et plantantis, hortus plantationum est congregatio filiorum. Numquid enim ego, qui alios parere facio, ipse non pariam, dicit Dominus? (Isai. LXVI, 9.) Ipse parit, cum ingenerat voluntatem bonam; plantat, cum instituit vitam; rigat, cum sensibus infundit gratiam; colit, cum moribus adhibet disciplinam. Audite me, divini fructus, et quasi rosa plantata super rivos aquarum fructificate (Eccli. XXXIX, 17). Mittite radices ad humorem vitae, id est ad amorem terrae viventium, non hujus in qua omnia senescunt et putrescunt. Non potest arbor facere fructum qui permaneat, nisi sursum in coelestibus radicem figat, ut quae sursum sunt, non quae super terram, quaerat et sapiat. Quod enim physici dicunt hominem esse arborem inversam, eo quod nervi corporis radicem et initium in vertice habeant, ego illo potius sensu interpretor, quod radicem amoris et desiderii figere debeat in coelo, in summo vertice rerum capite nostro Jesu Christo. Qui illuc radices miserit, et de illo fonte aeterno succum vitae et gratiae jugiter biberit, non timebit cum venerit aestus judicii; sed afferens et offerens fructum quem fecit plurimum, id remunerationis accipiet ut floreat in aeternum ante Dominum, cui est honor et gloria per omnia saecula saeculorum. Amen.

English translation

7. For this very purpose, my brothers, you whom carnal generation or worldly custom had planted in a waterless land, in a land of saltiness, the divine generation or the change wrought by the right hand of the Most High has transplanted upon the waters of refreshment; so that you, for whom formerly, because of barrenness, the axe and the fire were being prepared, now, planted in the house of the Lord, in the courts of the house of our God, may flourish, and bear fruit, and your fruit may remain. Are not the waters of refreshment the Scriptures of the Holy Spirit, on which we meditate day and night? Are not the waters of refreshment the tears of compunction, which have become bread for us day and night? Are not the waters of refreshment the Sacraments and supports of our salvation, by which we are fed and given drink at the altar? In all these, as it were through certain little streams, the fountain of wisdom which rises in the midst of paradise is channeled abroad, and divides its waters in the streets. 'I,' says Wisdom, 'came forth from paradise like a watercourse. I said: I will water my garden of plantings, I will make drunk the fruit of my offspring' (Eccli. XXIV, 41, 42). See, as you can gather from the very mouth of Wisdom bringing forth and planting, the garden of plantings is the congregation of her sons. 'For shall not I, who make others to bring forth, myself also bring forth?' says the Lord (Isai. LXVI, 9.). He himself brings forth, when he begets a good will; he plants, when he establishes a life; he waters, when he pours grace into the senses; he cultivates, when he applies discipline to conduct. 'Hear me, divine fruits, and bear fruit like the rose planted beside streams of water' (Eccli. XXXIX, 17). Send down your roots to the moisture of life, that is, to the love of the land of the living, not of this land in which all things grow old and rot. A tree cannot bear fruit that will endure unless it fixes its root above, in the heavenly places, so that it may seek and savor the things that are above, not the things that are upon the earth. For as the natural philosophers say that man is an inverted tree, because the nerves of the body have their root and beginning in the crown of the head, I interpret this rather in this sense: that he ought to fix the root of love and desire in heaven, in the highest crown of all things, our head, Jesus Christ. Whoever sends his roots there, and continually drinks from that eternal fountain the sap of life and grace, will not fear when the heat of judgment comes; but bringing and offering the fruit that he has produced in abundance, he will receive this as his reward: that he may flourish forever before the Lord, to whom be honor and glory through all ages of ages. Amen.
