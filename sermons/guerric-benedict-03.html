Introduction & Scripture references

Editorial introduction

Guerric places friendship with God before human favor. He considers humility, charity, reputation and fraternal correction, including the hostility that truthful correction can provoke. The sermon appears in the 1879 edition of Patrologia Latina under the feast of Saint Benedict. The collection is associated approximately with Guerric's abbacy in 1138–1157; the individual sermon is undated.

SERMON III.

¶cols. 107-108
Latin original

De amicitia Dei prae omnibus quaerenda.

English translation

On seeking the friendship of God above all things.

¶cols. 107-108
Latin original

1. Dilectus a Deo et hominibus (Eccli. XLV, 1). Paucis expressit bonum ac beatum virum, qualem nimirum constat dilectum Domini fuisse Benedictum. Paucis, inquam, comprehendit summam perfectionis, plenitudinem gratiae et virtutis, simulque beatitudinem vitae perennis, et consolationem praesentis. Quid enim deesse potest ad aeternam felicitatem illi qui a Deo diligitur? quid ad praesentem consolationem, illi qui ab hominibus diligitur? Illi nempe qui a Deo diligitur, etsi aliquid deesse videtur, non ob aliud deest, nisi ut nihil ei desit, et suo, ut putatur, defectu perfectior sit. Nam virtus in infirmitate perficitur. Paule, ait Dominus, sufficit tibi gratia mea (II Cor. XII, 9). Cui gratia Dei sufficit, sine damno gravi, imo cum lucro non mediocri aliquid gratiae deficit, quandoquidem ipse defectus et infirmitas virtutem perficit; et imminutio cujusdam gratiae, summam omnium gratiam Dei ampliorem ei et stabiliorem efficit. Longe fac, Domine, a servis tuis gratiam illam, quaecunque sit, quae adimere aut imminuere gratiam tui possit; unde videlicet aliquis, gloriosior in oculis suis, odiosior fiat in oculis tuis. Non est illa gratia, sed ira, illis plane quibus iratus es dari digna; quibus propter dolos posuisti talia, dejiciens eos dum allevantur, et elidens valide, dum super ventum ponuntur. Ut igitur illa sola gratia, sine qua nullus a te diligitur, salva nobis maneat, omnem aliam gratiam tua nobis gratia subtrahat, aut certe gratiam utendi simul conferat; quatenus habentes gratiam, per quam serviamus placentes cum metu et reverentia, de gratia muneris gratiam promereamur remuneratoris; et quanto quis fuerit gratiosior, tanto tibi fiat gratior.

English translation

1. "Beloved of God and men" (Eccli. XLV, 1). In few words he has expressed the good and blessed man, such as Benedict is known indeed to have been, the beloved of the Lord. In few words, I say, he has comprehended the sum of perfection, the fullness of grace and virtue, and at the same time the blessedness of life everlasting and the consolation of the present life. For what can be lacking to eternal felicity in him who is loved by God? What can be lacking to present consolation in him who is loved by men? Indeed, for him who is loved by God, even if something seems to be lacking, it is lacking for no other reason than that nothing may be lacking to him, and that by his seeming defect he may be made more perfect. For virtue is perfected in weakness. "Paul," says the Lord, "My grace is sufficient for thee" (II Cor. XII, 9). To him for whom the grace of God suffices, some grace is lacking without grave loss—indeed with no small gain—since the very defect and weakness perfects virtue; and the diminishing of a certain grace makes God's grace, the highest of all graces, fuller and more stable for him. Keep far, O Lord, from Thy servants that grace, whatever it be, which could take away or diminish Thy grace; whereby, namely, someone, more glorious in his own eyes, becomes more hateful in Thine eyes. That is not grace but wrath, truly worthy to be given to those with whom Thou art angry; to whom, on account of deceits, Thou hast appointed such things, casting them down while they are being raised up, and dashing them violently while they are set upon the wind. Therefore, that that grace alone, without which none is loved by Thee, may remain safe to us, let Thy grace withdraw from us every other grace, or at least grant together with it the grace of using it rightly; so that, having the grace by which we may serve, pleasing with fear and reverence, we may merit from the grace of the gift the grace of the Rewarder; and the more gracious anyone is, the more may he become pleasing to Thee.

Latin original

2. Sane mirabili arte, sed mirabiliori charitate, prudens clementia clemensque prudentia Dei, saluti consuluit humanae, ut, cum cunctos diligat, non tamen facile de dilectione sua certos aut securos esse sinat; nec ob aliud magnam multitudinem dulcedinis suae timentibus se abscondat, nisi ut, dum semper custodit humiles, semper habeat amabiles. Sunt enim justi et sapientes, et opera eorum in manu Dei, et tamen nescit homo utrum amore an odio dignus sit; sed omnia in futurum servantur incerta (Eccli. IX, 1, 21) Moderator nempe omnium sic eis dispensat gratiam munerum et operum, ut ex quibusdam quae concedit, consolationem quod digni sint amore tribuat; ex quibusdam quae subtrahit, suspicionem ac metum quod odio digni sint injiciat. Consolatur, cum diluculo visitat; terret, cum subito probat. Nunc mortificat, nunc vivificat; nunc deducit ad inferos, nunc reducit. Nunc pauperem facit et ditat, nunc humiliat et sublevat (I Reg. II, 6, 7). Et in tanta vicissitudinum varietate tanto certius salutem nostram operatur, quanto nos incertiores de ea, cum majore metu ac tremore ei cooperamur. Ait quidem Paulus: Certus sum quia nihil me separabit a charitate Dei (Rom. VIII, 38, 39). Sed vere Paulus hic erat, non ego aut tu, quibus omnia in futurum servari expedit incerta. Paulus, inquam, hic erat qui hoc dicebat, cui nimirum et illud dicere competebat: Vivo jam non ego, vivit vero in me Christus (Galat. II, 20), qui scilicet ita totus in quemdam divinum conversus erat affectum, ut adhaerens Domino unus cum ipso esset spiritus. Vide tamen hunc ipsum Paulum, qui ita modo certus erat, alias trepidum ac sollicitum, et castigantem corpus suum, ne forte, cum aliis praedicaverit, ipse reprobus efficiatur (I Cor. IX, 27): vide etiam colaphizari a Satana (II Cor. XII, 7), ne forte extollatur. In ipso itaque Paulo jam tibi est videre quomodo omnis certitudo quae ad horam confortatur et convalescit de spiritus consolatione, item infirmatur et evanescit superveniente tentatione. Ego igitur miser et ipsa indignus vita, qua consolatione, qua fiducia me dignum esse amore praesumam, cui omnia et interiora et exteriora manifestum odium denuntiant; cujus vita, non tentatio, non militia est adversus peccatum, sed ultronea servitus peccati; qui tam mente quam carne servio legi peccati; qui cum prudentia carnis et amicitia mundi, quae ambae inimicae sunt Deo, foedus miserum pepigisse videor, plane foedus cum morte, pactum cum inferno? Scio tamen, scio qui dixit: Delebitur foedus vestrum cum morte, et pactum cum inferno non stabit (Isai. XXVIII, 18); quia nimirum inimicis Dei nil amicum esse poterit, et non mortem aut infernum, sed omnem quoque creaturam ad ultionem inimicorum zelus armabit. Quam beatus in die illo quicunque erit ille dilectus a Deo! quam laudabiles quibus tunc laus erit a Deo, quibus dicet evidenti rerum argumento: Vos amici mei estis, eo quod feceritis quae praecepi vobis! (Joan. XV, 14). Et haec quidem prima virtus, istud optimum gratiae munus, praecipuus vitae fructus: quia beatitudinis certissimum pignus, Dei scilicet amicitia promereri; quo propitio poterit homini aliquis inimicari, sed nihil proficiet inimicus in eo, et filius iniquitatis non apponet nocere ei (Psal. LXXXVIII, 23).

English translation

2. Indeed, by a wonderful art, but by a more wonderful charity, the prudent clemency and the clement prudence of God has taken counsel for human salvation, so that, although He loves all, He does not easily allow them to be certain or secure of His love; nor does He, for any other reason, hide from those who fear Him the great abundance of His sweetness, except that, while He always keeps them humble, He may always have them lovable. For there are just and wise men, and their works are in the hand of God, and yet man does not know whether he is worthy of love or of hatred; but all things are kept uncertain for the future (Eccli. IX, 1, 21). The Ruler of all things indeed so dispenses to them the grace of gifts and works, that from certain things which He grants, He gives the consolation that they are worthy of love; from certain things which He withdraws, He instills the suspicion and fear that they are worthy of hatred. He consoles when He visits at daybreak; He terrifies when He suddenly puts to the test. Now He brings death, now He brings life; now He leads down to the underworld, now He brings back. Now He makes poor and makes rich, now He humbles and lifts up (I Reg. II, 6, 7). And in so great a variety of changes, He works our salvation all the more surely, the more uncertain we are about it, since we cooperate with Him with greater fear and trembling. Paul indeed says: "I am certain that nothing shall separate me from the charity of God" (Rom. VIII, 38, 39). But truly this was Paul speaking, not I or you, for whom it is expedient that all things be kept uncertain for the future. Paul, I say, was the one who said this, to whom indeed it was also fitting to say: "I live, now not I, but Christ liveth in me" (Galat. II, 20)—he who was so wholly turned into a certain divine affection that, clinging to the Lord, he was one spirit with Him. Yet see this very Paul, who was thus certain at one moment, at other times fearful and anxious, chastising his own body, lest perhaps, when he had preached to others, he himself should become reprobate (I Cor. IX, 27): see him also buffeted by Satan (II Cor. XII, 7), lest perhaps he be exalted. In Paul himself, then, you may now see how all certainty which is strengthened for an hour and grows strong from the consolation of the spirit, likewise grows weak and vanishes when temptation comes upon it. I, therefore, wretched and unworthy even of life itself—with what consolation, with what confidence shall I presume myself worthy of love, I to whom all things, both inward and outward, proclaim manifest hatred; whose life is not a temptation, not a warfare against sin, but a willing servitude to sin; who serve the law of sin as much in mind as in flesh; who seem to have struck a wretched covenant with the prudence of the flesh and the friendship of the world, both of which are enemies to God—plainly a covenant with death, a pact with hell? Yet I know, I know Him who said: "Your covenant with death shall be done away, and your pact with hell shall not stand" (Isai. XXVIII, 18); because surely nothing can be friendly to the enemies of God, and zeal will arm not only death and hell but every creature as well for the vengeance upon enemies. How blessed on that day will whoever is beloved of God be! How praiseworthy will those be to whom praise shall then come from God, to whom He will say, with manifest proof of the fact: "You are my friends, because you have done what I have commanded you!" (Joan. XV, 14). And this indeed is the first virtue, this the best gift of grace, the chief fruit of life: because it is the most certain pledge of blessedness, namely, to merit the friendship of God; with God favorable to him, someone may indeed be an enemy to a man, but the enemy will profit nothing against him, and the son of iniquity shall not have power to hurt him further (Psal. LXXXVIII, 23).

Latin original

3. Jam vero si post hanc et per hanc summam omnium causamque bonorum, gratiam etiam illam promerearis, ut, dilectus a Deo, etiam dilectus sis ab hominibus, quae consolatio est illa in miseriis hujus vitae, quae requies, quod gaudium, quae deliciae; praesertim si didiceris favore gratiae humanae non evanescere, sed exardescere ad diligendum Deum amplius, propter quem diligaris ab hominibus? Alioquin nempe si aliunde oriatur, vel alio referatur, quid est favor humanus, nisi ventus urens, aura corrumpens vel pestilens, latrunculus spolians, homicida insidians, coluber in via, cerastes in semita, mordens ungulas equi, ut cadat ascensor ejus retro? Vir quippe vanus erigitur in superbiam, et vanitate sensus sui, velut equo praecipiti, fertur in ruinam. Hujusmodi nimirum serpens vulnerat et subvertit vestigia, dum, veluti blandiens capiti, tenera illius facit laudari primordia. Haud secus, nisi fallor, contingit homini qui, antequam diligere sciat, diligi nimie affectat, et, antequam vel sibi ipsi amicus esse didicerit, amicos sibi omnes conciliare quaerit; facit divitias non cum judicio, in medio dierum suorum derelinquet eas, et in novissimo suo erit insipiens, cum omnes amici ejus spreverint eum, pro quibus sprevit ipse Deum. Tunc fiet sermo qui scriptus est: Quoniam Deus dissipavit ossa eorum qui hominibus placent: confusi sunt, quoniam Deus sprevit eos (Psal. LII, 6).

English translation

3. Now if, after this and through this love, supreme above all and the cause of good things, you should also merit that grace, that, being loved by God, you might also be loved by men, what consolation is that in the miseries of this life, what rest, what joy, what delight—especially if you have learned not to grow vain through human favor, but rather to be kindled to love God the more, on whose account you are loved by men? Otherwise, indeed, if it arises from elsewhere, or is referred to something else, what is human favor but a scorching wind, a corrupting or pestilent breeze, a thieving brigand, a lurking murderer, a serpent in the way, a horned snake in the path, biting the horse's heels so that its rider falls backward? For the vain man is lifted up into pride, and by the vanity of his own mind, as if by a headlong horse, is carried to ruin. This kind of serpent indeed wounds and overturns the footsteps, while, as if flattering the head, it makes his tender beginnings to be praised. Not otherwise, unless I am mistaken, does it happen to a man who, before he knows how to love, excessively desires to be loved, and, before he has even learned to be a friend to himself, seeks to win all men as friends to himself; he gathers riches without judgment, in the midst of his days he will abandon them, and in his last end he will be a fool, when all his friends have despised him, for whose sake he himself despised God. Then will be fulfilled the saying that is written: 'For God has scattered the bones of those who please men: they are confounded, because God has despised them' (Psal. LII, 6).

4. Primo itaque omnium illa, quae prima et finis est omnium, quaerenda est dilectio Dei, cujus merito digni simus etiam ab hominibus diligi; et cujus studio docti simus, dilectione hominum qualiter nos oporteat uti. Cum autem rem in tuto locaveris; ita scilicet firmato affectu mentis, ut nonnisi in Deo et propter Deum diligi velis: tunc omnino volo ut dulcedo morum tuorum, humilitas obsequiorum, honestas studiorum, ad omnem te commendet conscientiam hominum, ut ultro rapias affectus omnium, testimonio praediceris omnium; quatenus ipsa religio, quae te commendat, justa vicissitudine per te commendabilis fiat. Sic in te quoque filio adoptivo, Unigeniti complebitur oratio: Pater, clarifica Filium tuum, ut Filius tuus clarificet te (Joan. XVII, 1); dum videntes claritatem operum tuorum glorificabunt Patrem tuum. Neque id dicimus, quod inter dilectionem Dei et dilectionem proximi sit aliquis ordo temporis, quamvis sit ordo affectionis. Statim quippe ab initio et illam oportet observare, et istam non negligere; quando nec Deus sine proximo, nec proximus sine Deo vere potest diligi; quamvis ordinata affectio ignorare non possit quae de duabus dilectionibus praeponderet alteri, quae debeat alteri formam et modum praescribere, finemque praefigere. Sed hoc dicimus, quod aliud est amari, aliud amari studere. Quod profecto studium sicut periculose assumitur ab illis qui plus vanitate quam charitate blandi sunt, sic periculose negligitur ab his qui plus superbia quam sapientia austeri sunt. Quam ob rem beatum dixerunt, qui inter utrumque vitium, illius scilicet vanae amicabilitatis, et superbae severitatis, mediam tenere potuit regalem viam veritatis; id est ut, vera charitate plenus, nec per vanitatem hoc studium sectetur, nec per tumorem dedignetur.

English translation

4. Therefore, before all things, that love of God must be sought which is the first and the end of all things, by whose merit we may be worthy to be loved by men as well; and by whose discipline we may be taught how we ought to make use of the love of men. But when you have placed the matter in a safe position—that is, when the affection of your mind has been so fixed that you wish to be loved only in God and for God's sake—then I wholly desire that the sweetness of your character, the humility of your service, the uprightness of your pursuits, should commend you to the conscience of all men, so that you may win the affections of all unsought, and be proclaimed by the testimony of all; so that religion itself, which commends you, may by a just reciprocity become commendable through you. Thus in you too, as an adopted son, the prayer of the Only-begotten will be fulfilled: 'Father, glorify your Son, that your Son may glorify you' (Joan. XVII, 1); while men, seeing the splendor of your works, will glorify your Father. Nor do we say this as though there were some order of time between the love of God and the love of neighbor, although there is an order of affection. For from the very beginning one must both observe the former and not neglect the latter, since neither can God truly be loved without neighbor, nor neighbor without God; although a well-ordered affection cannot fail to know which of the two loves outweighs the other, which ought to prescribe form and measure to the other, and set its end before it. But this we say: that it is one thing to be loved, another to strive to be loved. Which pursuit indeed is dangerously undertaken by those who are winsome more from vanity than from charity, and is just as dangerously neglected by those who are austere more from pride than from wisdom. For this reason they have been called blessed who, between both vices—namely that of vain amiability and that of proud severity—have been able to hold the middle royal road of truth; that is, so that, being full of true charity, they neither pursue this pursuit through vanity, nor disdain it through arrogance.

5. Illa tamen est vis et natura veri amoris, ut, etiam cum non affectat, tamen efficiat se reamari: quoniam veritas, etiam sine alieno, suffragio facile se ad omnem conscientiam commendat, nisi dira malignae mentis obsistat nequitia, quae parata est sinistre interpretari omnia. Ad hunc tamen sanctum amorem conciliandum quidam proprium donum habent ex Deo; qui exhilarans facies eorum in oleo, perfudit eos quodam placore ac nitore gratioso, omnia tam facta quam dicta ipsorum gratificans in oculis omnium; cum multi qui forsitan non minus, imo et amplius amant, non facile eamdem gratiam inveniant. Ad omnes autem pertinet ut, providentes bona non solum coram Deo sed etiam coram hominibus (Rom. XII, 17 et II Cor. VII, 212), nec conscientiam negligant amore famae, nec famam fiducia conscientiae. Nam et de ipsa conscientia quomodo tibi blandiri potes, nisi sine querela sis inter fratres; imo, nisi te fratrem inter fratres in omni conversatione tua demonstres? Putas enim satis esse, si non scandalizas? Imo scandalizas, si non aedificas; si non, testimonium habens bonum intus et foris, quantum ad gradum tuum attinet, Deum ubique glorificas. Sane malis, quibus non placet nisi malum, quibus justus gravis est etiam ad videndum, si illis placere te non sinit, non tua negligentia (cum omnes etiam inimicos diligas), sed ipsorum nequitia, qui retribuunt mala pro bonis, odium pro dilectione tua; consolatur te qui ait. Beati eritis cum vos oderint homines (Luc. VI, 22), et ille qui ait quia, talibus si placeret, Christi servus non esset (Galat. I, 10).

English translation

5. Yet it is the force and nature of true love that, even when it does not aim at it, it nevertheless brings it about that it is loved in return: because truth, even without another's support, easily commends itself to every conscience, unless the dire wickedness of a malignant mind stands in the way, which is ready to interpret everything in the worst sense. Yet for the winning of this holy love, some have a particular gift from God; who, gladdening their faces with oil, has suffused them with a certain graciousness and gracious brightness, making both their deeds and their words pleasing in the eyes of all; while many who perhaps love no less—indeed even more—do not easily find the same grace. But it belongs to all to take thought for what is good, not only before God but also before men (Rom. XII, 17 et II Cor. VII, 212), neither neglecting conscience out of love of reputation, nor neglecting reputation out of confidence in conscience. For how can you flatter yourself even about your conscience itself, unless you are without reproach among the brethren—indeed, unless you show yourself a brother among brothers in all your conduct? Do you think it enough if you do not give scandal? Nay, you do give scandal if you do not build up; if you do not, having good testimony both within and without, as far as pertains to your rank, glorify God everywhere. Indeed, as for the wicked, whom nothing pleases but evil, to whom the just man is grievous even to look upon—if they do not allow you to please them, it is not through your negligence (since you love even your enemies), but through their own wickedness, who repay evil for good, hatred for your love; he consoles you who says, 'Blessed shall you be when men hate you' (Luc. VI, 22), and he who says that if he pleased such men, he would not be a servant of Christ (Galat. I, 10).

6. Verum illud miserabile est malum, omnique lacrymarum flumine plangendum, quod quidem saepius experiuntur non modo praelati, sed etiam subditi, qui fratres suos aemulantur Dei aemulatione; quod scilicet et boni, licet non ex ea parte qua sunt boni, bonos inveniuntur odisse. Oderunt corripientem in porta (Amos V, 10), et inimici hominis domestici ejus (Mich. VII, 6), ita ut etiam Paulus ingemiscat quod filiis et amicis inimicus factus sit, vera dicens (Galat. IV, 16). Ergo et ipse expertus est quod comicus ait, « Veritas odium parit (6)3; » imo et illud quod sponsa memorat: Filii matris meae pugnaverunt contra me (Cantic. I, 5), cum ego pugnarem pro eorum salute. O victrix regina, o triumphatrix magnifica, pugna, ne cedas, ne discedas, aut lassescas. Noli vinci a malo, sed vince in bono malum (Rom. XII, 21). Sapientia vincit malitiam (Sap. VII, 30); quanto magis infirmitatem aut imprudentiam? Neque contradictionem carnalium, malitiam interpretari potest charitas spiritualium; sed magis ignorantiam, aut infirmitatem qua praeventi sunt. Quanquam nec ipsis Apostolis contumelia visa sit quod mali a Domino sunt appellati. Quare autem haec ita loquor, fratres mei, dilecti Deo et mihi? Nunquid quia malum hujusmodi suspicer in aliquo vestrum, contra quod mihi vel fratribus meis sit pugnandum? Nunquid enim aliquis invenitur in vobis, non dico rebellis, sed vel durus et intractabilis? Loquor haec non quia ita in vobis sit, sed ne aliquando sit; ne forte aliquando cuipiam aliqua infirmitas subrepat, ut quamcunque correptionem charitatis, nisi cum charitate suscipiat. Sic fiet ut tam is qui corripit, quam ille qui corripitur, dilectus sit a Deo et hominibus, et memoria utriusque in benedictione sit; quod meritis beati Benedicti patris nostri praestare dignetur ille singulariter benedictus Dei Patris Jesus Christus, qui est Deus benedictus in saecula. Amen.

English translation

6. But that is a wretched evil, to be mourned with every flood of tears, which indeed not only superiors but also subjects often experience, who are zealous for their brothers with a godly zeal; namely, that even good men—though not in that respect in which they are good—are found to hate good men. "They hate him who reproves in the gate" (Amos V, 10), and "a man's enemies are those of his own household" (Mich. VII, 6), so that even Paul groans that he has become an enemy to his sons and friends by telling them the truth (Galat. IV, 16). Therefore he too experienced what the comic poet says, "Truth begets hatred3"; indeed, he experienced also that which the Bride recalls: "The sons of my mother have fought against me" (Cantic. I, 5), while I was fighting for their salvation. O victorious queen, O magnificent triumphant one, fight on; do not yield, do not withdraw, nor grow weary. "Be not overcome by evil, but overcome evil with good" (Rom. XII, 21). "Wisdom overcomes malice" (Sap. VII, 30); how much more, then, weakness or imprudence? Nor can the charity of spiritual men interpret the opposition of carnal men as malice; but rather as ignorance, or as a weakness by which they have been overtaken. Although it did not even seem an insult to the Apostles themselves that they were called evil by the Lord. But why do I speak this way, my brothers, beloved of God and of me? Is it because I suspect such an evil in any of you, against which I or my brothers must fight? Is anyone found among you who is—I do not say rebellious—but even hard and intractable? I say these things not because it is so among you, but lest it ever be so; lest perhaps at some time some weakness should creep upon someone, so that he would not receive any correction of charity except with charity. Thus it will come about that both he who corrects and he who is corrected will be loved by God and by men, and the memory of each will be in blessing; which may he who is singularly blessed of God the Father, Jesus Christ, who is God blessed forever, deign to grant through the merits of our blessed father Benedict. Amen.
