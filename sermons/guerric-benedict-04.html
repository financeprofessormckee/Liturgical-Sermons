Introduction & Scripture references

Editorial introduction

Guerric compares Benedict with Moses through faith and gentleness. He distinguishes living faith from feigned belief and connects vigilance before God with hope of the promised reward. The sermon appears in the 1879 edition of Patrologia Latina under the feast of Saint Benedict. The collection is associated approximately with Guerric's abbacy in 1138–1157; the individual sermon is undated.

De lenitate et fide.

English translation

On meekness and faith.

¶cols. 111-112
Latin original

1. In fide et lenitate ipsius sanctum fecit illum (Eccli. XLV, 4). Haec quidem de Moyse scripta sunt, sed hodie beato Benedicto non incongrue, quantum arbitror, aptata sunt. Qui, cum omnino plenus fuerit Spiritu sanctorum, multo magis de spiritu Moysi credendus est habuisse non minimum. Si enim Dominus de spiritu Moysi tulit, et posuit super omnem illam suffraganeam turbam seniorum, qui in partem ministerii sunt asciti (Num. XI, 24-27), quanto magis super istum qui plenitudinem totius ministerii tanto verius quanto spiritualius implevit? Ille dux fuit exeuntium de Aegypto; iste, renuntiantium saeculo: legislator ille, legislator et iste: ille tantum minister litterae occidentis; iste, spiritus vivificantis: ille ad duritiam cordis Judaeorum dedit eis justificationes non bonas, praeter pauca moralia; iste solam puritatem evangelicam simplicemque morum tradidit disciplinam: ille tam multa scribit intellectu difficilia, factu impossibilia, vel inutilia; iste rectissimam vitae scribit Regulam, sermone luculentam, discretione praecipuam. Denique dux ille filiorum Israel, quos de Aegypto eduxit, in requiem promissam non induxit; dux noster recta via, orientis tramite, tanquam signifer exercitus monachorum hodie praecessit nos in regnum coelorum. Itaque non erit absurdum si credatur illius adaequasse meritum, cujus invenitur etiam superasse ministerium. Nec incongrue videbitur ad ipsum esse translatum quod de illo scriptum legitur: In fide et lenitate ipsius sanctum illum Dominus; praesertim cum harum praecipue virtutum, fidei scilicet et lenitatis, nobis ipse magister sit, qui nequaquam aliter vixit quam docuit.

English translation

1. "In his faith and meekness he made him holy" (Eccli. XLV, 4). These words indeed were written of Moses, but today, as far as I judge, they have not been unfittingly applied to blessed Benedict. For since he was altogether full of the Spirit of the saints, he is all the more to be believed to have had no small share of the spirit of Moses. For if the Lord took from the spirit of Moses and placed it upon that whole assisting crowd of elders who were taken into a part of his ministry (Num. XI, 24-27), how much more upon him who fulfilled the fullness of that whole ministry all the more truly as he fulfilled it more spiritually? That man was the leader of those going out of Egypt; this one, of those renouncing the world: that man was a lawgiver, and this one also a lawgiver: that man was only a minister of the letter that kills; this one, of the spirit that gives life: that man, on account of the hardness of the Jews' hearts, gave them statutes that were not good, apart from a few moral precepts; this one handed down only the purity of the Gospel and a simple discipline of morals: that man writes so many things difficult to understand, impossible or useless to do; this one writes a most upright Rule of life, brilliant in style, outstanding in discretion. Finally, that leader of the sons of Israel, whom he led out of Egypt, did not bring them into the promised rest; our leader, by the straight way, along the path of the rising sun, has today gone before us like a standard-bearer of an army of monks into the kingdom of heaven. Therefore it will not be absurd if it is believed that his merit equaled that of the one whose ministry he is even found to have surpassed. Nor will it seem unfitting that what is read as written of that man should be transferred to him: "The Lord made him holy in his faith and meekness"; especially since he himself is our master chiefly of these virtues, namely faith and meekness, he who never lived otherwise than he taught.

2. Quid enim praeclarius illius fide, qui in aetate puerili mundum sibi arridentem irridens, tam mundi quam corporis sui florem quasi jam aridum calcavit, magis appetens pro Deo mala mundi perpeti, quam in eo temporaliter prosperari? Quid similius illi fidei Moysi, quam Apostolus commendans ait: Fide Moyses grandis factus negavit se filium filiae Pharaonis, magis eligens affligi cum populo Dei, quam temporalis peccati habere jucunditatem (Hebr. XI, 24, 25)? Quid autem sanctius lenitate Patris nostri, qui nec malitia falsorum fratrum vitae ipsius insidiantium: et pro vino venenum propinantium, potuit exasperari? Moysen quidem Scriptura praedicat mittissimum1 fuisse super omnes homines qui morabantur in terra (Num. XII, 132); nunquid tamen exacerbatum spiritum ejus negat? nunquid non iratum et valde iratum adversus aemulos memorat (Id. XVI, 15)? Hujus autem magistri nostri mansuetudinem, non solum erga maledicos, sed etiam erga maleficos, mirabilem me legisse memini, iracundiam non memini. Quanquam nec in ipso, sicut nec in Moyse, praeconio sanctae lenitatis praejudicet, si justus zelo contra peccantes fervet, sine quo lenitas tepor aut timiditas est. Quomodo namque lenitas illa sanctum quempiam faceret, quae in Heli, qui alius sanctus erat, damnata est? (I Reg. II). Fratres, habete pacem inter vos, mandat ille Magister pacificus et lenis; prius tamen praemittit: habete salem in vobis (Marc. IX, 49); sciens nimirum pacis lenitatem vitiorum esse nutricem, nisi prius asperitas zeli mordacem eis insperserit salem; sicut et carnes vermescere facit clementia temporis, nisi desiccaverit eas fervor salis. Habete itaque pacem inter vos, sed quae sale condita sit sapientiae; sectamini lenitatem, sed quae ferveat fide.

English translation

2. For what is more splendid than the faith of him who, in his boyhood, mocking the world that smiled upon him, trampled underfoot the flower both of the world and of his own body as though already withered, desiring rather to suffer the evils of the world for God's sake than to prosper temporally in it? What is more like that faith of Moses which the Apostle commends, saying: "By faith Moses, when he was grown a man, denied himself to be the son of Pharaoh's daughter, choosing rather to be afflicted with the people of God than to have the pleasure of temporal sin" (Hebr. XI, 24, 25)? But what is holier than the meekness of our Father, who could not be provoked even by the malice of false brothers plotting against his very life, and offering him poison instead of wine? Scripture indeed proclaims that Moses was the meekest1 of all men who dwelt on the earth (Num. XII, 132); yet does it not also record his spirit as exasperated? Does it not relate him as angry, and very angry, against his rivals (Id. XVI, 15)? But I recall having read of the wonderful gentleness of this our master, not only toward those who spoke evil of him, but even toward those who did evil to him; I do not recall any anger in him. Although neither in him, nor in Moses, should this prejudice the praise of holy meekness, if a just man burns with zeal against sinners, without which meekness is mere lukewarmness or timidity. For how could that meekness which was condemned in Eli, who was otherwise a holy man, have made anyone holy? (I Reg. II). "Brothers, have peace among yourselves," that peaceful and gentle Master commands; yet he first says beforehand: "Have salt in yourselves" (Marc. IX, 49); knowing surely that the meekness of peace is a nurse of vices, unless the sharpness of zeal first sprinkles them with biting salt; just as the mildness of the weather causes flesh to breed worms, unless the heat of salt dries it out. Have peace, then, among yourselves, but peace seasoned with the salt of wisdom; pursue meekness, but meekness that burns with faith.

Latin original

3. In fide et lenitate vos quoque sancti eritis; nec jam lenitas suspecta erit, si fides praecesserit: tantum fides non ficta, sed vera sit; fides non mortua, sed viva et vivida sit. Non modo viva vel vivida, sed et constans et intrepida fides erat Moysi, de quo Paulus scribit: Fide reliquit Aegyptum, non veritus animositatem regis. Animosi sunt reges, sed animosior est fides, quippe quae potentiam eorum nullam esse videt, ac per hoc omnem persequentium insaniam secura, quasi superior, irridet; promptior et fortior ad perferendum, quam illorum furor ad persequendum. Duo sane commendare mihi videtur Apostolus in illa memorabili fide Moysi, cui occasione lectionis hodiernae comparandam diximus fidem sancti patris nostri Benedicti: scilicet quod idem Moyses, qui fidei exemplum factus est, hujus mundi contempsit prospera, nec timuit adversa. Prospera contempsit, majores divitias aestimans thesauris Aegyptiorum improperium Christi: adversa non timuit, non veritus scilicet animositatem regis. Ad utrumque vero causam Apostolus subjunxit, unde scilicet id potuerit, ut discamus unde fides nostra infirmetur in nobis. Majores divitias reputabat thesauris Aegyptiorum improperium Christi: aspiciebat enim, inquit, in remunerationem. Non est veritus animositatem regis: invisibilem enim, inquit, tanquam videns sustinuit (Hebr. XI, 27, 26). Nimirum inde fit ut nihil reputentur temporalia, si prae oculis habeantur aeterna; inde fit ut facile potestas contemnatur humana, si tanquam semper imminens potestas timeatur divina. Utrumque vero sola fides agit, cujus tam vividi, tam perspicaces sunt oculi, ut et in ea quae futura sunt prospectum vivaciter porrigat, et in ea quae, licet praesentia sint, occulta tamen, intuitum perspicaciter figat. Fidei siquidem, quam aeternus Spiritus illuminat, nec mora temporis, nec moles corporis praejudicare potest quin et tempus praecedat, praesumendo quod futurum est, et corpus excedat, intuendo quod spiritus est. Gemina siquidem est fidei virtus, contemplantis non ea quae videntur, sed quae non videntur; sicut gemina est ratio eorum quae non videntur. Aut enim ideo non videntur quia praesentia non sunt, aut ideo quia, etsi praesentia sunt, spiritualia sunt. Praesentia non sunt bona futura quae promittuntur; praesens est, sed occultus est, quia spiritus est, Deus ipse qui promittit vel minatur. Porro fides, quia est substantia sperandarum rerum, bona futura, quae sperat, quasi praesentia praesumit, et jam quasi subsistere in corde credentis facit. Item quia est argumentum non apparentium (Ibid. 1), Deum, etsi non appareat, praesentem tamen esse sibi coarguit, probat et convincit. Illi qui dicebat: Qui conresuscitavit et consedere fecit in coelestibus in Christo (Ephes. II, 6), fides erat substantia rerum sperandarum; illi qui invisibilem tanquam videns sustinebat (Hebr. XI, 27), argumentum erat non apparentium. Qui enim dicebat: Spe salvi facti sumus (Rom. VIII, 24), nonne quod sperabat et per patientiam exspectabat, jam per fidem in corde suo subsistere demonstrabat? Et qui proponebat Dominum in conspectu suo semper, nonne persuasum tenebat, argumento fidei, eum praesentem esse, qui non apparebat? (Psal. XV, 8).

English translation

3. In faith and gentleness you too will be holy; and gentleness will no longer be suspect, if faith has gone before it—provided only that the faith be not feigned but true, not dead but living and vigorous. Not merely living or vigorous, but also steadfast and fearless was the faith of Moses, of whom Paul writes: "By faith he left Egypt, not fearing the fierceness of the king." Kings are fierce, but faith is fiercer, since it sees that their power is nothing, and therefore, secure as one who is superior, it laughs at all the madness of persecutors; it is more ready and stronger to endure than their fury is to persecute. Two things, surely, the Apostle seems to me to commend in that memorable faith of Moses, which, on the occasion of today's reading, we said should be compared to the faith of our holy father Benedict: namely, that this same Moses, who became an example of faith, both despised the prosperities of this world and did not fear its adversities. He despised prosperity, esteeming the reproach of Christ greater riches than the treasures of the Egyptians; he did not fear adversity, that is, he was not afraid of the fierceness of the king. For both the Apostle adds the cause, namely whence he was able to do this, so that we may learn whence our faith grows weak in us. He esteemed the reproach of Christ greater riches than the treasures of the Egyptians: "for he looked," it says, "unto the reward." He did not fear the fierceness of the king: "for he endured, as seeing him who is invisible" (Hebr. XI, 27, 26). Surely it comes about from this that temporal things are reckoned as nothing, if eternal things are kept before the eyes; it comes about from this that human power is easily held in contempt, if divine power is feared as always imminent. But faith alone does both of these things, whose eyes are so lively, so keen-sighted, that it both stretches its gaze vividly toward things that are future, and fixes its sight keenly upon things which, although present, are nonetheless hidden. For to faith, which the eternal Spirit illumines, neither the delay of time nor the bulk of the body can be an obstacle, so that it both goes before time, presuming what is to come, and goes beyond the body, beholding what is spirit. The power of faith is indeed twofold, contemplating not those things which are seen, but those which are not seen; just as the reason of things not seen is also twofold. For either they are not seen because they are not present, or they are not seen because, although present, they are spiritual. The future goods that are promised are not present; but he who promises or threatens, God himself, is present, yet hidden, because he is spirit. Furthermore, faith, because it is the substance of things to be hoped for, presumes the future goods which it hopes for as if they were present, and makes them, as it were, already subsist in the heart of the believer. Likewise, because it is the evidence of things not appearing (ibid. 1), it proves, demonstrates, and convinces that God, even though he does not appear, is nevertheless present to it. To him who said: "Who raised us up together, and made us sit together in the heavenly places in Christ" (Ephes. II, 6), faith was the substance of things to be hoped for; to him who endured, as seeing him who is invisible (Hebr. XI, 27), faith was the evidence of things not appearing. For did not he who said, "We are saved by hope" (Rom. VIII, 24), show that what he hoped for and awaited through patience already subsisted in his heart through faith? And did not he who set the Lord always in his sight hold it firmly persuaded, by the evidence of faith, that he who did not appear was present? (Psal. XV, 8).

4. Huic fidei prorsus aptissime congruit quod Scriptura dicit: Justus ex fide vivit (Habac. II, 4, et Rom. I, 17). Haec enim justum facit, justumque custodit, et, ut vivat in aeternum, interim spei gaudio pascit. Quid enim sic hominem a peccato revocat et servat sicut fides, quae invisibilem tanquam videns sustinet et observat? quid ita spe gaudentem facit sicut fides, quae semper in remunerationem aspicit? Nos enim, fratres, quare negligentes sumus, nisi quia praesentiam Judicis nostri minus vigilanter intendimus? aut quare tristitia pene absorbemur, nisi quia remunerationem promissam minus fideliter cogitamus? Haec siquidem duo mala sunt, negligentia scilicet et tristitia, quibus miserabiliter infidelitas nostra laborat; quia videlicet aut negligimus mandata, aut, si necessitate timoris a negligentia coercemur, nequaquam hilariter operamur, nec, sicut fides postulat, spe remunerationis in labore consolamur. Credere, inquit, oportet accedentes ad Deum quia est, et inquirentibus se remunerator est (Hebr. XI, 6). Si non dissimulemus quia est, habemus custodiam timoris; si non dissimulemus quia inquirentibus se remunerator est, habemus consolationem spei. Ubi custodia timoris, nullus locus negligentiae; ubi consolatio spei, nullus locus tristitiae. Sicut autem de fide, qua Deus esse creditur, timor salutis nascitur, ita cum minime creditur, aut dissimulatur, omnibus malis frena laxantur. Quare enim corrupti et abominabiles facti sunt in iniquitatibus, nisi quia dixit ille generalis insipiens in corde suo: Non est Deus (Psal. LII, 2, 1)? aut certe si nescire Deum non permittitur, non est timor Dei ante oculos ejus, sed dolose agit in conspectu ejus, dissimulans quod novit invitus, ut inveniatur iniquitas ejus ad odium (Psal. XXXV, 2, 3). Merito prorsus dolosus ad odium, qui ignorantiam Dei non habet, sed odium. Quomodo enim non odit Deum, cujus declinat aspectum, contemnit imperium, vellet non esse judicium? Ignorans quidem ignorabitur, odiens autem et odietur. Ille reprobabitur veritate judicii, in istum vindicabitur severitate odii.

English translation

4. To this faith what Scripture says applies most fittingly: "The just man lives by faith" (Habac. II, 4, et Rom. I, 17). For faith makes a man just, and keeps him just, and meanwhile, that he may live forever, feeds him with the joy of hope. For what so recalls a man from sin and keeps him from it as faith, which, as though seeing the Invisible One, endures and keeps watch? What so fills a man with the joy of hope as faith, which always looks toward the reward? For why, brothers, are we negligent, except because we attend less watchfully to the presence of our Judge? Or why are we almost swallowed up by sadness, except because we think less faithfully upon the promised reward? These indeed are two evils—negligence and sadness—under which our unfaithfulness miserably labors; for either we neglect the commandments, or, if we are restrained from negligence by the necessity of fear, we by no means work cheerfully, nor, as faith requires, do we console ourselves in our labor with the hope of reward. "It is necessary," he says, "that he who comes to God believe that he exists, and that he is a rewarder to those who seek him" (Hebr. XI, 6). If we do not pretend otherwise but believe that he exists, we have the guard of fear; if we do not pretend otherwise but believe that he is a rewarder to those who seek him, we have the consolation of hope. Where there is the guard of fear, there is no room for negligence; where there is the consolation of hope, there is no room for sadness. But just as from the faith by which God is believed to exist, a saving fear is born, so when he is not believed in at all, or is dissembled, the reins are loosed to every evil. For why are men become corrupt and abominable in their iniquities, except because that universal fool said in his heart, "There is no God" (Psal. LII, 2, 1)? Or certainly, if it is not permitted him not to know God, then "the fear of God is not before his eyes," but he deals deceitfully in his sight, dissembling what he unwillingly knows, "that his iniquity may be found unto hatred" (Psal. XXXV, 2, 3). Rightly indeed is he deceitful unto hatred, who has not ignorance of God, but hatred. For how does he not hate God, whose gaze he avoids, whose command he despises, whose judgment he would wish not to exist? He who is ignorant will indeed be ignored; but he who hates will also be hated. The former will be condemned by the truth of judgment; upon the latter vengeance will be taken by the severity of hatred.

5. Adeo, fratres, periculosior est fides ficta, fide dolosa, quae se ipsam dissimulat, quam fides nulla. Fides nulla, per insipientiam dicit in corde suo: Non est Deus; fides ficta per nequitiam dolose agit in conspectu ejus, et se scire Deum vel sciri a Deo dissimulat; dum sciens et prudens, imo imprudens et impudens sub oculis ejus peccat. Nescio tamen quomodo possit dici fides, quae fictos et dolosos potius facit quam fideles. Scio quidem quia confessione fidei plena est terra, sed tamen audio prophetam plangentem, quia periit fides de terra (Jerem. VII, 28). Putas enim, Filius hominis si nunc veniret, inveniret fidem super terram (Luc. XVIII, 8)? Nunquid fidem reputaret hanc fidem fictam negligentium et contemnentium, qua levius damnat caecitatem infidelium, qua meliorem judicat credulitatem daemonum? Daemones credunt et contremiscunt (Jacob. II, 19); homines credunt nec contremiscunt. Daemones quem credunt verentur; homines dum quem credunt, nec timent nec verentur, gravius de contemptu judicantur. Non fallamur ergo, fratres, generali nomine fidei, quasi quaecunque fides ad justitiam debeat reputari; sed meminerimus Doctor gentium in fide et veritate, qualem definierit fidem, qua Deo est placendum: Fides, inquiens, est substantia rerum sperandarum argumentum non apparentium. Haec est fides quae per dilectionem operatur; ut, suadente conscientia meritorum, de fide spes nascatur, fidesque supponatur velut subjectum et fundamentum, cui superaedificentur bona aeterna, quae speranda sunt. Sine ista fide impossibile est placere Deo (Hebr. XI, 1, 6), et cum ista impossibile est displicere Deo. Oculi tui Domine, respiciunt fidem (Jerem. 3 v.3), dixit ille qui jugiter in conspectu tuo stabat per fidem. Omnino juste et merito. Prorsus debita vicissitudo, ut oculi tui, Domine, respiciant fidem, quia oculi mei semper ad Dominum fidelem.

English translation

5. So much, brothers, is a feigned faith, a deceitful faith that dissembles itself, more dangerous than no faith at all. No faith at all, through folly, says in its heart, "There is no God"; feigned faith, through wickedness, deals deceitfully in his sight, and pretends either that it does not know God or that it is not known by God; while, knowingly and deliberately—nay, imprudently and shamelessly—it sins under his eyes. Yet I do not know how that can be called faith which makes men feigning and deceitful rather than faithful. I know indeed that the earth is full of the confession of faith, but yet I hear the prophet lamenting, because "faith has perished from the earth" (Jerem. VII, 28). Do you suppose that if the Son of Man came now, he would find faith upon the earth? (Luc. XVIII, 8). Would he reckon as faith this feigned faith of the negligent and the contemptuous, compared with which he condemns unbelievers' blindness more lightly and judges demons' belief better? "The demons believe and tremble" (Jacob. II, 19); men believe and do not tremble. The demons fear him whom they believe; men, while they believe him whom they fear not and do not revere, are more gravely judged for their contempt. Let us not, then, brothers, be deceived by the general name of faith, as though any faith whatsoever ought to be reckoned unto righteousness; but let us remember how the Doctor of the Gentiles defined, in faith and truth, the kind of faith by which God is to be pleased: "Faith," he says, "is the substance of things to be hoped for, the evidence of things not appearing." This is the faith that works through love; so that, with the consciousness of merits giving assurance, hope may be born from faith, and faith may be set beneath it as subject and foundation, upon which the eternal goods that are to be hoped for are built. "Without this faith it is impossible to please God" (Hebr. XI, 1, 6), and with it, it is impossible to displease God. "Your eyes, Lord, look upon faith" (Jerem. 3 v.3), said he who stood continually in your sight through faith. Altogether justly and rightly. It is indeed a fitting exchange, that your eyes, Lord, should look upon faith, since my eyes are ever toward the faithful Lord.

6. Nos autem, fratres, si tanquam sine fide simus, Deum post dorsum nostrum ponimus, ut, dissimulantes timorem ejus, in vanitates potius respiciamus, quo merito nos respiciendos putamus? Imo respiciemur, sed quo vultu? Vultus Domini super facientes mala (Psal. XXXIII, 17); sed quam iratus, quam terribilis, quam importabilis, tunc demum scietur, quando fugient qui oderunt eum a facie ejus. Et a facie tua, Domine, quo fugient, nisi in tenebras exteriores, in illud chaos et abyssum ignis et caliginis? Tunc enim dicent montibus, Cadite super nos; et collibus, Operite nos (Luc. XXIII, 30), levius aestimantes absorberi voragine inferni, quam sustinere faciem Dei irati. Enim vero tunc stabunt justi in magna constantia; tunc fides, quae modo sollicita stat in conspectu Domini ad videndam voluntatem ipsius, secura stabit ad videndam gloriam ejus. Vigilate, fratres, state in fide (I Cor. XVI, 13). Quem fides timore sollicitat, non potest dormitare per negligentiam; quem fides in spe radicat, non potest titubare per diffidentiam. Omnia autem vestra in charitate fiant, ut fidei lenitas jungatur, quatenus de unoquoque vestrum dicatur, In fide et lenitate ipsius sanctum fecit illum Dominus (Eccli. English translation

6. But we, brothers, if we are as though without faith, placing God behind our backs so that, disregarding fear of him, we look instead toward vanities—with what justice do we think we deserve to be looked upon? Indeed we shall be looked upon, but with what face? "The face of the Lord is against those who do evil" (Psal. XXXIII, 17); but how angry, how terrible, how unbearable that face will be, will only then be known, when those who hated him flee from before his face. And from your face, Lord, where will they flee, except into outer darkness, into that chaos and abyss of fire and gloom? For then they will say to the mountains, "Fall on us," and to the hills, "Cover us" (Luc. XXIII, 30), judging it lighter to be swallowed up in the chasm of hell than to endure the face of an angry God. But then indeed the just will stand in great steadfastness; then faith, which now stands anxiously in the sight of the Lord to see his will, will stand secure to see his glory. Watch, brothers, stand firm in faith (I Cor. XVI, 13). He whom faith troubles with fear cannot slumber through negligence; he whom faith roots in hope cannot waver through distrust. But let all your things be done in charity, so that gentleness may be joined to faith, so that it may be said of each one of you, "In his faith and gentleness the Lord made him holy" (Eccli. XLV, 4); which may he grant, the Holy One of holies, who lives and reigns through all ages of ages. Amen.
