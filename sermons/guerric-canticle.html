Editorial introduction

Guerric takes the Bridegroom's words "You who dwell in the gardens, the friends are listening; let me hear your voice" and declines them for himself, as one who dwells among the tombs of the flesh. The brothers dwell in gardens when they meditate on Scripture, and Christ, the true gardener, plants and waters the Church. Paul walked in the gardens of paradise and sang of Christ to the friends who listened. The words call the devout to psalmody and prayer, with the angels listening, and the preacher to speak, with the faithful listening. The sermon follows the All Saints sermon in the 1879 edition of Patrologia Latina and carries no liturgical heading. The collection is associated approximately with Guerric's abbacy in 1138–1157; the individual sermon is undated.

In verba Cantici canticorum: « Quae habitas in hortis, amici auscultant, fac me audire vocem tuam. » (Cant. VIII, 13.)

English translation

On the Words of the Song of Songs: "You who dwell in the gardens, the friends are listening; let me hear your voice." (Cant. VIII, 13.)

¶cols. 209-210; cols. 211-212
Latin original

1. In conventu sodalium et amicorum, id est, Ecclesia sanctorum, cujus vocem deceat audiri, sponsus ipse sponsae loquens, innuit cum ait: Quae habitas in hortis, amici auscultant, fac me audire vocem tuam. Non sum ego cui hoc dictum debeat videri: non sum qui habitem in hortis; magis mihi videor esse de illis qui habitant in sepulcris. Corpora enim peccatorum, quid sunt nisi sepulcra mortuorum? Qui ergo dediti sunt corporibus suis, non in hortis habitant, sed in sepulcris: Deumque exasperant, donec qui educit vinctos in fortitudine, forti voce inclamet, Lazari, veni foras; suisque discipulis super eo praecipiat, Solvite eum et sinite abire (Joan. XI, 43, 44). Magna sane differentia est sepulcrorum et hortorum. Haec plena omni spurcitia et ossibus mortuorum; illi, omni suavitate et gratia florum, aut fructuum. Quid enim si aliquando sepulcra videntur in hortis? Nam et Dominus in horto sepultus fuit. Etsi enim sepulcra in hortis, nunquid tamen horti in sepulcris? Ita fortassis, sed in sepulcris justorum. Ibi plane vernabit gratissima quaedam amoenitas hortorum, verno scilicet illo tempore resurrectionis eorum, cum reflorebit caro eorum, et non solum ossa justi sicut herba germinabunt, sed et totus justus germinabit sicut lilium, et florebit in aeternum ante Dominum. Non sic impii, non sic, qui sepultura asini sepeliuntur (Jerem. XXII, 19), et sine omni spe melioris resurrectionis subditi corruptioni, hac praesenti futuram auspicantur. De sepulcris eorum dicere coeperam, quod quantum a spurcitia illorum abhorret florentium species hortorum, tantum, imo incomparabiliter amplius distat oblectatio spiritualium a voluptate gaudiorum carnalium.

English translation

1. In the assembly of companions and friends, that is, the Church of the saints, whose voice it is fitting should be heard, the Bridegroom himself, speaking to the Bride, hints at this when he says: You who dwell in the gardens, the friends are listening; let me hear your voice. I am not one to whom this should seem to be said: I am not one who dwells in gardens; I seem to myself rather to be among those who dwell in tombs. For what are the bodies of sinners but tombs of the dead? Those, then, who are given over to their bodies do not dwell in gardens but in tombs, and they provoke God to anger, until he who leads forth the bound in strength cries out with a mighty voice, "Lazarus, come forth," and commands his disciples concerning him, "Loose him and let him go" (Joan. XI, 43, 44). There is indeed a great difference between tombs and gardens. The one is full of all filth and the bones of the dead; the other, of all the sweetness and grace of flowers or fruits. For what if tombs are sometimes seen in gardens? For even the Lord was buried in a garden. Though tombs may be in gardens, are gardens therefore in tombs? Perhaps so—but in the tombs of the just. There, surely, a most pleasing charm of gardens will bloom, namely in that springtime of their resurrection, when their flesh will flower again, and not only will the bones of the just sprout like grass, but the whole just person will sprout like a lily, and will flourish forever before the Lord. Not so the wicked, not so those who are buried with the burial of an ass (Jerem. XXII, 19), and who, subject to corruption without any hope of a better resurrection, in this present corruption begin the corruption to come. I had begun to speak of their tombs, to say that as much as the appearance of flourishing gardens recoils from their filth, so much—indeed incomparably more—does the delight of spiritual things differ from the pleasure of carnal joys.

2. Vos igitur, ni fallor, estis qui in hortis habitatis, qui scilicet in lege Domini meditamini die ac nocte, et quot libros legitis, tot hortos perambulatis; quot sententias eligitis, tot poma carpitis. Et beati quibus omnia poma nova et vetera servata sunt: id est, tam prophetarum, quam evangelistarum, vel apostolorum eloquia reposita sunt, ut et unicuique vestrum illud sponsae ad sponsum videatur dictum, Omnia poma, nova et vetera, dilecte mi, servavi tibi (Cantic. VII, 13). Scrutamini igitur Scripturas. Vos enim non falso vitam in ipsis putatis vos habere, qui nihil aliud in illis quaeritis quam Christum, cui testimonium perhibent Scripturae. Beati plane qui scrutantur testimonia ejus; in toto corde exquirunt eum. Mirabilia testimonia tua, Domine; ideo scrutata est ea anima mea (Psal. CXVIII, 2, 129). Scrutinio quidem opus est, non solum ut eruantur mystica, sed etiam ut sugantur moralia. Ideoque vos, qui perambulatis hortos Scripturarum, nolite negligenter et otiose transvolare; sed scrutantes singula, velut apes sedulae mel de floribus, spiritum de sermonibus colligite. Spiritus enim meus, inquit Jesus, super mel dulcis, et haereditas mea super mel et favum (Eccli. XXIV, 27). Ita probantes quid sapiat manna absconditum, eructabitis illud Davidicum: Quam dulcia faucibus meis eloquia tua! super mel et favum ori meo (Psal. CXVIII, 103).

English translation

2. You, then, unless I am mistaken, are they who dwell in gardens, who meditate on the law of the Lord day and night, and for as many books as you read, so many gardens you walk through; for as many sentences as you choose, so many fruits you pluck. And blessed are those for whom all fruits, new and old, are kept in store: that is, the sayings of the prophets, as well as of the evangelists and apostles, are laid up, so that to each of you that word of the Bride to the Bridegroom may seem said: "All fruits, new and old, my beloved, I have kept for you" (Cantic. VII, 13). Search the Scriptures, then. For you do not falsely suppose that you have life in them, you who seek in them nothing other than Christ, to whom the Scriptures bear witness. Blessed indeed are they who search his testimonies; they seek him with their whole heart. "Wonderful are your testimonies, O Lord; therefore has my soul searched them" (Psal. CXVIII, 2, 129). There is indeed need for searching, not only that mystical meanings may be dug out, but also that moral lessons may be sucked out. Therefore you who walk through the gardens of the Scriptures, do not fly over them carelessly and idly; but searching out each thing, like busy bees gathering honey from flowers, gather spirit from the words. For "my spirit," says Jesus, "is sweeter than honey, and my inheritance above honey and the honeycomb" (Eccli. XXIV, 27). Thus, proving what the hidden manna tastes like, you will belch forth that word of David: "How sweet are your words to my palate! above honey and the honeycomb to my mouth" (Psal. CXVIII, 103).

3. Ab his hortis in alios, ubi requies secretior et voluptas beatior, et species mirabilior, sponsus, ni fallor, vos introducit, cum intentos laudibus suis in voce exsultationis et confessionis, rapit in locum tabernaculi admirabilis usque ad domum Dei, lucem scilicet inaccessibilem, ubi habitat, ubi pascit, ubi cubat in meridie. Si enim devotio psallentium et orantium habet aliquid de illa pia curiositate quaerentium, Rabbi, ubi habitas? puto quia merentur audire, Venite et videte. Venerunt, inquit, et viderunt, et apud eum manserunt die illo (Joan. I, 38, 39). Quandiu apud Patrem luminum sumus, apud quem non est transmutatio, nec vicissitudinis obumbratio; noctem nescimus, tantummodo die beato fruimur. Cum inde labimur, in noctem nostram recidimus. Heu mihi! quam cito defecerunt dies mei, quam cito sicut fenum arui, qui quandiu fui in horto cum illo, sicut paradisus Dei virui et florui! Cum illo hortus voluptatis: sine illo locus horroris et vastae solitudinis. Existimo enim qui in hortum illius intrat, quod et ipse hortus fiat; sitque anima illius velut hortus irriguus, ut et sponsus in laudem ejus dicat: Hortus conclusus, soror mea sponsa (Cant. IV, 12). An non sunt hortus, in quibus fit quod ipse hortulanus loquitur plantationi, quam plantavit Pater suus? Audite me, inquit, divini fructus, et quasi rosa plantata super rivos aquarum fructificate (Eccli. XXXIX, 17). Quasi Libanus, odorem suavitatis habete. Florete flores quasi lilium, date odorem, et frondete in gratiam.

English translation

3. From these gardens into others, where the rest is more secret and the pleasure more blessed, and the beauty more wonderful, the Bridegroom, unless I am mistaken, leads you in, when, as you are intent upon his praises in a voice of exultation and confession, he snatches you away into the place of the wonderful tabernacle, even to the house of God, that is, the inaccessible light, where he dwells, where he feeds, where he lies down at midday. For if the devotion of those singing psalms and praying has something of that holy curiosity of those who seek, "Rabbi, where do you dwell?" I think that they deserve to hear, "Come and see." "They came," it says, "and saw, and remained with him that day" (Joan. I, 38, 39). As long as we are with the Father of lights, with whom there is no change nor shadow of alteration, we know no night; we enjoy only the blessed day. When we slip away from there, we fall back into our own night. Woe to me! How quickly my days have failed, how quickly I have withered like grass—I, who, as long as I was in the garden with him, flourished and blossomed like the paradise of God! With him, the garden is a place of delight; without him, a place of horror and vast solitude. For I think that whoever enters into his garden becomes himself a garden; and that his soul becomes like a watered garden, so that the Bridegroom too may say in praise of it: "A garden enclosed, my sister, my spouse" (Cant. IV, 12). Are they not a garden, in whom happens what the Gardener himself speaks to the planting which his Father planted? "Listen to me," he says, "you divine fruits, and bear fruit like the rose planted beside the streams of water" (Eccli. XXXIX, 17). "Have a sweet fragrance like frankincense. Bloom like the lily, give forth fragrance, and put forth leaves in grace."

4. O Domine Jesu, vere hortulane, operare in nobis quod exigis a nobis; nam sine te nihil possumus facere. Tu enim verus es hortulanus, idem creator qui cultor vel custos horti tui, qui verbo plantas, spiritu rigas, virtute incrementum das. Errabas, Maria, quae eum existimabas hortulanum illius horti pauperis et exigui, in quo sepultus fuit. Hortulanus est totius mundi, hortulanus est coeli, hortulanus est Ecclesiae, quam hic plantat et rigat, donec incremento consummato transplantet eam in terram viventium, secus decursus aquarum viventium, ubi non timebit cum venerit aestus, sed erit folium ejus viride, nec aliquando desinet facere fructum. Beati qui habitant in illis hortis tuis, Domine! in saecula saeculorum laudabunt te. Paulus habitabat in illis, cujus conversatio erat in coelis: qui mente saepius excedens perambulabat paradisum beatae voluptatis, hortos deliciarum Dei, ubi et pertransiens rosas Martyrum, et lilia Virginum, desinens etiam mirari altitudinem cedrorum Dei delectabatur potius carpere de fructu ligni vitae, quod est in medio paradisi, in ipso nimirum gustans plenius et felicius quam suavis est Dominus. Ideoque cum inde redibat, amicis auscultantibus memoriam abundantiae suavitatis ejus profusius eructabat, et ex abundantia cordis os redundabat. Quia enim sicut adipe et pinguedine repleta erat anima ejus, ideo labiis exsultationis laudabat os ejus. Cor quippe sapientis erudiet os ejus, et labiis illius addet gratiam (Prov. XVI, 23). Eructabat itaque cor ejus verbum bonum, ac de bono thesauro bonus bona proferebat, sponsumque ipsum qui in amicis auscultabat, velut jucundo carmine oblectabat. Prorsus carmen jucundum, suavisque melodia, ubi tam canorum Spiritus sancti organum,, tanta morum et sermonum consonantia, tanta dulcedine amoris, tanta gratia oris Christum Jesum jubilum cordis sui personabat. Si enim lingua suavis vim carminis non haberet, scriptum non esset: Tibiae et psalterium faciunt suavem melodiam, et super utraque lingua suavis (Eccli. XL, 21). Si carmine hujusmodi Psalmista Deum oblectari nesciret, nequaquam diceret: Jucundum sit ei eloquium meum (Psal. CIII, 311). Sed nec ipse sponsus diceret: Fac me audire vocem tuam; sonet vox tua in auribus meis. Vox enim tua suavis (Cantic. VIII, 13, et II, 14), quia exaltationes Dei in faucibus tuis, tanquam habitantis in hortis, in deliciis paradisi. E contra vero, Non est speciosa laus in ore peccatoris (Eccli. XV, 9), qui habitat in sepulcris. Cujus enim vita Deum exasperat, lingua non delectat, sed terribiliter increpatur voce divina: Quare tu enarras justitias meas; (Psal. XLIX, 16.) Cantica lyrae tuae non audiam (Amos V, 25).

English translation

4. O Lord Jesus, true gardener, work in us what you require of us; for without you we can do nothing. For you are the true gardener, being yourself the creator who is also the tiller and keeper of your garden, who plant by the word, water by the Spirit, and give increase by power. You were mistaken, Mary, when you thought him the gardener of that poor and small garden in which he had been buried. He is the gardener of the whole world, the gardener of heaven, the gardener of the Church, which he plants and waters here, until, the growth being completed, he transplants her into the land of the living, beside the courses of living waters, where she shall not fear when the heat comes, but her leaf shall be green, and she shall never cease to bear fruit. Blessed are they who dwell in those gardens of yours, Lord! They shall praise you forever and ever. Paul dwelt in them, whose citizenship was in heaven: who, often going out of himself in mind, walked through the paradise of blessed delight, the gardens of God's delights, where, passing by the roses of the Martyrs and the lilies of the Virgins, and ceasing even to marvel at the height of the cedars of God, he delighted rather to pluck from the fruit of the tree of life, which is in the midst of paradise, in it indeed tasting. And so, when he returned from there, with his friends listening, he poured forth more abundantly the memory of the abundance of its sweetness, and out of the abundance of the heart his mouth overflowed. For because his soul was filled, as it were, with fat and fatness, therefore his mouth praised with lips of exultation. For the heart of the wise shall instruct his mouth, and shall add grace to his lips (Prov. XVI, 23). And so his heart poured forth a good word, and as a good man out of a good treasure brought forth good things, and he delighted, as it were with a pleasant song, the very Bridegroom who was listening in his friends. Truly a pleasant song, and a sweet melody, where so tuneful an instrument of the Holy Spirit, so great a harmony of conduct and of words, with so much sweetness of love, with so much grace of mouth, sounded forth Christ Jesus, the jubilation of his heart. For if a sweet tongue did not have the power of song, it would not be written: 'The pipe and the psaltery make sweet melody, but a pleasant tongue surpasses them both' (Eccli. XL, 21). If the Psalmist did not know that God is delighted by a song of this kind, he would by no means say: 'Let my speech be pleasing to him' (Psal. CIII, 311). Nor would the Bridegroom himself say: 'Make me hear your voice; let your voice sound in my ears. For your voice is sweet' (Cantic. VIII, 13, et II, 14), because 'the exaltations of God are in your throat,' as of one dwelling in the gardens, in the delights of paradise. But on the contrary, 'Praise is not seemly in the mouth of a sinner' (Eccli. XV, 9), who dwells among tombs. For whoever's life provokes God, his tongue does not delight him, but he is terribly rebuked by the divine voice: 'Why do you declare my justices?' (Psal. XLIX, 16.); 'I will not hear the songs of your lyre' (Amos V, 25).

5. Sed quoniam Scriptura quam assumpseram in excusationem sermonis, occasionem et materiam ministravit sermonis; si placet, finiamus quod de illa coeperamus. Dupliciter itaque potest intelligi, quod a sponso dicitur, Quae habitas in hortis, amici auscultant, fac me audire vocem tuam: vel quod devotum amatorem invitet ad psallendum vel orandum; vel quod sanctum praedicatorem excitet ad loquendum. Quod ut magis persuadeat, amicos auscultare memorat, angelos scilicet orantem vel psallentem; fideles praedicantem. Ubi primo intuemur, qua disciplina cordis et corporis nos oporteat in conspectu angelorum psallere vel orare, ne vacui dimissi vacuos nos dimittant, qui venerant ut vota deferrent, et dona referrent; imo recedant inimici qui venerant amici. Quinimo sponsus ipse, qui stat ad ostium et pulsat, si nulla ei digna devotio de intus respondens aperiat, recedet conquerens, et dicens: Attendi et auscultavi, nemo quod bonum est loquitur. Nullus est qui agat poenitentiam super peccato suo, dicens: Quid feci? omnes conversi sunt ad cursum suum, quasi equus impetu vadens in praelium (Jerem. VIII, 6). Si autem praedicatorem exhortatur, fiduciam ei loquendi tribuit de benevolentia et attentione auditorum, quia scilicet non increduli obloquuntur, aut litigant; non aemuli detrectant, aut subsannant; non tepidi dormitant, aut oscitant: sed amici attenti auscultant, quorum amor et meritum possit evangelizanti impetrare verbum et spiritum. Et bene amici auscultant. Est enim velut proprium amici, scilicet quod devotus audiat vocem sponsi, sicut Joannes ait: Amicus sponsi est qui stat, non vagans animo, aut prostratus somno, audit et gaudet gaudio propter vocem sponsi (Joan. III, 29), quam agnoscit etiam in servis. Et nos itaque inde probemus nos amicos, ut sive per os loquentis, sive legentis aut cantantis vox sponsi sonuerit, sic stemus ad audiendum, quatenus auditui nostro det gaudium et laetitiam; et non solum cum gaudio, suscipere verbum, sed et fructum afferre in patientia.

English translation

5. But since the Scripture which I had taken up as an excuse for my sermon has furnished the occasion and material for the sermon, let us, if it please you, finish what we had begun concerning it. What is said by the Bridegroom—'You who dwell in the gardens, the friends are listening; make me hear your voice'—can therefore be understood in two ways: either as inviting the devout lover to sing psalms or to pray; or as rousing the holy preacher to speak. To make this more persuasive, he recalls that friends are listening—namely, the angels listening to one praying or singing psalms, and the faithful listening to one preaching. Here we first consider with what discipline of heart and body we ought to sing psalms or pray in the sight of the angels, lest, being sent away empty, they in turn send us away empty—they who had come to carry away our vows and bring back gifts; nay, lest those who had come as friends depart as enemies. Indeed, the Bridegroom himself, who stands at the door and knocks, if no devotion worthy of him answers from within and opens to him, will withdraw complaining, and say: 'I attended and listened; no one speaks what is good. There is none who does penance for his sin, saying, What have I done? They have all turned aside to their own course, like a horse rushing with impetuosity into battle' (Jerem. VIII, 6). But if he is exhorting the preacher, he grants him confidence to speak on account of the benevolence and attentiveness of his hearers—namely, that the unbelieving do not speak against him or quarrel; that rivals do not disparage or mock; that the lukewarm do not doze or yawn: but that attentive friends listen, whose love and merit can obtain for the one preaching the gospel both the word and the spirit. And rightly do friends listen. For it is, as it were, proper to a friend that the devout one should hear the voice of the Bridegroom, just as John says: 'He that stands is the friend of the bridegroom'—not wandering in mind, nor laid low in sleep—'he hears him, and rejoices with joy because of the bridegroom's voice' (Joan. III, 29), which he recognizes even in his servants. Let us, then, likewise prove ourselves to be friends, so that whether through the mouth of one speaking, or reading, or singing, the voice of the Bridegroom sounds forth, we may so stand ready to listen that it gives to our hearing joy and gladness; and that we may not only receive the word with joy, but also bring forth fruit in patience.
