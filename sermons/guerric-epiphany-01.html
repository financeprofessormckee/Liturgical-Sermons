Introduction & Scripture references

Editorial introduction

Guerric's first Epiphany sermon asks what gifts Christ's poor can offer the newborn King. Seven numbered sections interpret myrrh as penitential sorrow and toil, incense as devotion, and gold as wisdom. The address to brothers whose clothing scarcely suffices in winter places the argument in its monastic setting. This reading follows the 1879 Patrologia Latina edition; the approximate 1138–1157 composition range is distinct from that printing date. Editorial notes explain retained quotation variants and anomalous Scripture locators. The source explicitly names Epiphany.

¶cols. 45–46; cols. 47–48
Latin original

1. Afferte Domino, filii Dei, afferte (Psal. xxviii, 1). Satius est ei afferre, quam sanguisugae, id est concupiscentiae, cujus duae filiae, voluptas et vanitas, non dant requiem die ac nocte, dicentes: Affer, affer (Prov. xxx, 15). Quid igitur afferemus Domino? Afferte Domino gloriam auri, et honorem thuris: afferte Domino gloriam nomini ejus, myrrham (Psal. xxviii, 1, 2) sepulturae ejus. Sed dicit mihi discipulus Christi, filius Petri: Argentum et aurum non est mihi (Act. iii, 6). Nec sacculi peregrinantur mecum myrrhae et thuris. Ergone vacuus apparebis in conspectu Domini, et cunabula novi Regis nullis honorabis xeniis? O dives paupertas, o locuples nuditas, si tamen Christiana et voluntaria! Quibus enim divitiis non abundas, non solum auri, sed etiam auri primi, auri igniti; non solum myrrhae et thuris, sed etiam universi pulveris pigmentarii? Imo qui sunt alii qui possint abundare divitiis hujusmodi, nisi pauperes Christi? In viis, inquit, justitiae ambulo, ut ditem diligentes me, et thesauros eorum repleam. Nec deest ei unde id possit: Mecum sunt, inquit, divitiae et gloria, opes superbae, et justitia (Prov. viii, 20, 21, 18). Bonae opes prorsus divitiae salutis, in quibus ita superbitur, ut justitia non deseratur. Fratres, superbia ista est gloria exsultantium in Domino, et insultantium mundo, quod nihil habeat tam pretiosum, ut paupertati valeat comparari sanctorum. O qui pusillo animo estis, cur non superbitis mecum in opibus istis? Superbiam istam non damnat, sed remunerat Magister humilitatis, si videlicet ita magnanimus sis in contemptu mundi, ut omnem gloriam ejus quasi de sublimi despicias, et nihili pendas prae amore et gloria tuae nuditatis. Omnino dives es, si in paupertate gloriaris, et de te quoque gratias aget congratulatio Apostoli: Gratias, inquit, ago Deo meo, quod in omnibus divites facti estis in illo, in omni verbo et sanctitate, ut nihil vobis desit in ulla gloria (I Cor. i, 4, 5, 7)1. Longe aliter illum alloquitur Dominus, qui sibi applaudebat de affluentia mundi hujus: Tu, inquit, dicis Quia dives sum, et nullius egeo; et tu miser es, et miserabilis, et pauper et caecus, et nudus (Apoc. iii, 17).

English translation

1. Bring to the Lord, children of God, bring (Psalm xxviii, 1). It is better to bring to him than to the leech, that is, covetous desire, whose two daughters, pleasure and vanity, give no rest day or night, saying: Bring, bring (Proverbs xxx, 15). What, then, shall we bring to the Lord? Bring to the Lord the glory of gold and the honour of incense; bring to the Lord the glory due to his name, myrrh (Psalm xxviii, 1, 2) for his burial. But a disciple of Christ, a son of Peter, says to me: Silver and gold I have none (Acts iii, 6). Nor do bags of myrrh and incense travel with me. Will you then appear empty-handed before the Lord, and honour the new King's cradle with no gifts? O rich poverty, O wealthy nakedness, provided that it is Christian and voluntary! For what riches do you not abound in: not only gold, but the finest gold, gold tried by fire; not only myrrh and incense, but every powder of the perfumer? Indeed, who else can abound in such riches except Christ's poor? I walk, he says, in the ways of justice, to enrich those who love me and fill their treasuries. Nor does he lack the means: With me, he says, are riches and glory, magnificent wealth and justice (Proverbs viii, 20, 21, 18). The riches of salvation are truly good wealth, in which one takes pride without abandoning justice. Brothers, this pride is the glory of those who exult in the Lord and mock the world because it has nothing so precious that it can compare with the poverty of the saints. O faint-hearted ones, why do you not take pride with me in these riches? The Master of humility does not condemn this pride but rewards it, provided that you are so great-hearted in despising the world that you look down, as from on high, upon all its glory and count it as nothing beside the love and glory of your nakedness. You are altogether rich if you glory in poverty, and the Apostle's thanksgiving will give thanks for you too: I thank my God, he says, that in all things you have been made rich in him, in every word and in holiness, so that you lack nothing in any glory (1 Corinthians i, 4, 5, 7)1. Very differently does the Lord address the man who congratulated himself upon this world's abundance: You say, I am rich and need nothing; and you are wretched and miserable and poor and blind and naked (Apocalypse iii, 17).

Latin original

2. Sane inter divitias hujusmodi, pro quibus Paulus congratulabatur discipulis, non dubitaverim aurum, thus, et myrrham posse reperiri, quae digne et grate Christo nato queant offerri. At ego, inquies, non sum mihi conscius tale quid accepisse, nec aliquid tam pretiosum, ut est aurum, thus et myrrha, penes me posse inveniri. Ego enim, videns paupertatem meam, vix mendicabo stipem quotidianam, pauperem exigens vitam. Putas, inquit, te nihil tale accepisse? vide magis ne acceptam a Patre substantiam dissipaveris vivendo luxuriose. Sed omitto istud; non enim improperandum est, ut Sapiens ait, homini avertenti se a peccato (Eccli. viii, 6). Vellem magis ut non pigeret te tentare et quaerere si forte vel minimum aliquid paternae substantiae resedisset apud te, unde initium recuperandi posses capere. Vellem foderes intra te; solent namque pretiosi latere thesauri in abditis terrae. In agro absconditus erat thesaurus ille qui desiderio sui compulit hominem omnia sua vendere (Matth. xiii, 44). In agro se dicebant thesauros absconditos habere decem viri Israelitae, et per hoc gladium evaserunt homicidae (Jerem. xli, 8). O quanti thesauri bonorum operum, quantae divitiae piorum fructuum absconditi latent in agro humani corporis, et quanto plures in abdito cordis, si modo sit qui exerceat et fodiat! Neque illud Platonicum dico, quod anima ante corpus artes didicerit, quae oblivione et mole corporis obrutae, disciplina et industria refodiendae sint: sed quod ratio hominis et ingenium, juvante gratia, omnium sit virtutum seminarium. Si ergo ad cor redeas, et corpus exerceas, non diffidas te inventurum thesauros desiderabiles; et si non auri statim in initio, aut thuris, certe vel myrrhae non inutilis. Inutilem aut vilem tu ne dixeris, quam Christus acceptat in munere, qua sui corporis sepulturam non solum praesignari, cum ei offerretur, sed etiam consummari voluit, cum ex ea perungeretur.

English translation

2. Surely among those riches for which Paul congratulated his disciples, I would not doubt that gold, incense and myrrh can be found which may worthily and acceptably be offered to the newborn Christ. But I, you will say, am not aware of having received anything of the kind, nor can anything as precious as gold, incense and myrrh be found in my possession. Seeing my poverty, I can scarcely beg my daily pittance as I lead a poor life. Do you think, he says, that you have received nothing of the kind? Consider rather whether you have squandered the substance received from your Father by living in luxury. But I leave that aside; for, as the Wise Man says, one must not reproach a man who turns away from sin (Ecclesiasticus viii, 6). I would rather that you should not be reluctant to try and search whether perhaps some smallest part of your Father's substance remains with you, from which you might begin to recover. I would have you dig within yourself; for precious treasures often lie hidden in the secret places of the earth. Hidden in a field was that treasure which, through the desire for it, compelled a man to sell all he had (Matthew xiii, 44). Ten men of Israel said that they had treasures hidden in a field, and by this escaped the murderer's sword (Jeremiah xli, 8). O what treasures of good works, what riches of devout fruits lie concealed in the field of the human body, and how many more in the hidden heart, if only someone will cultivate and dig! I am not speaking of that Platonic notion that the soul learned the arts before entering the body, and that, buried by forgetfulness and the body's weight, they must be dug out again by instruction and effort; rather, that human reason and natural ability, with grace assisting, are a seedbed of all the virtues. If, then, you return to your heart and exercise your body, do not doubt that you will find desirable treasures; if not gold or incense immediately at the beginning, then certainly at least useful myrrh. Do not call useless or worthless what Christ accepts as a gift, by which he willed his body's burial not only to be foreshadowed when it was offered to him, but also to be accomplished when he was anointed with it.

¶cols. 47–48
Latin original

3. Si ergo manifestius vis audire, myrrha in corde tuo dolor est, myrrha in corpore tuo labor est, si tamen iste vel ille poenitentialis est. Myrrham siquidem utrumque esse non solum probat etymologia nominis, aut qualitas saporis, sed etiam medicinalis operatio virtutis. Myrrha namque amarissimum gustum sui etiam nomine prodit, effectu autem, praeter alias sui utilitates, corruptioni resistit. Et quid gustu amarius, quid effectu salubrius, quam dolor quo peccator ad poenitentiam contristatur? Recogitat annos suos in amaritudine animae suae, dicens Deo, Noli me condemnare (Job. x, 2); sed omnis haec amaritudo nihil aliud quam myrrha est, vindicans a corruptione tam luxuriarum, in quibus computruit, quam vermium immortalium, quos meruit. Quid vero labor corporis? Non tam myrrha est, quam fasciculus myrrhae, si credere volumus his qui de saeculo nuper venere; quibus regula jejuniorum et vigiliarum, quotidianum opus manuum, asperitas vestium, et omnia pene amara, quia insolita, velut in unum colligata fasciculum, imposita sunt ad ferendum. Et quidem amarissime portarent hunc myrrhae fasciculum, nisi quia, etsi nondum gaudent communicare passionibus Dilecti, ut dicant, Fasciculus myrrhae dilectus meus mihi (Cantic. i, 12), dulce tamen nimium habent amaritudinem cordis amaritudine mitigare exteriori, tanquam choleram stomachi potu absinthii. Sicut enim stomachus, immoderato usu dulcium corruptus, potione purgatur amara, sic eorum qui suaviter vixerunt in amaricata conscientia, nunquam melius quam contrario, id est, vita et consuetudine curantur austera. Maxime autem, si saepius detur eis bibere vinum myrrhatum Dominicae passionis, id est potentur vino compunctionis, quod quanto amarius est ex recordatione peccati, tanto salubrius est peccatori. Ideo Jesus noluit bibere (Matth. xxvii, 48)2, quia peccatum non fecerat, quod ei crucifixor imputabat, qui de amaritudine vitis alienae ei propinabat qui de germine vitis verae, vinum novum cum amicis in regno Patris habebat bibere.

English translation

3. If, then, you wish to hear it more plainly, myrrh in your heart is sorrow; myrrh in your body is toil, provided that the one or the other is penitential. Both are myrrh, as is shown not only by the word's derivation or the nature of its taste, but also by the medicinal working of its power. For myrrh reveals its intensely bitter taste even by its name, while in its effects, besides its other uses, it resists corruption. And what is more bitter to taste, what more wholesome in its effect, than the sorrow by which a sinner is grieved into repentance? He recalls his years in the bitterness of his soul, saying to God: Do not condemn me (Job x, 2). Yet all this bitterness is nothing other than myrrh, rescuing him from the corruption both of the lusts in which he rotted and of the undying worms he has deserved. What, then, of bodily toil? It is not so much myrrh as a bundle of myrrh, if we believe those who have recently come from the world. The rule of fasts and vigils, daily manual work, rough clothing, and almost everything bitter because unfamiliar have been laid upon them to bear, as though bound into one bundle. They would indeed carry this bundle of myrrh with great bitterness, except that, even if they do not yet rejoice to share the sufferings of the Beloved and say, My beloved is a bundle of myrrh to me (Song of Songs i, 12), they nevertheless find it exceedingly sweet to soothe the bitterness of the heart with outward bitterness, like the stomach's bile with a drink of wormwood. For just as a stomach spoiled by excessive consumption of sweets is purged by a bitter draught, so those who have lived pleasantly are never better cured in their embittered conscience than by the opposite, that is, an austere life and habit. Especially is this so if they are often given the myrrhed wine of the Lord's Passion to drink, that is, the wine of compunction: the more bitter it is from remembering sin, the more wholesome it is for the sinner. For this reason Jesus would not drink (Matthew xxvii, 48)2, because he had committed no sin, which his crucifier imputed to him by offering him the bitterness of a foreign vine, whereas he was to drink new wine from the fruit of the true vine with his friends in his Father's kingdom.

Latin original

4. Caeterum quanquam perfectis, qualis Timotheus erat, exercitatio corporalis ad modicum utilis sit comparatione pietatis (I Tim. iv, 5)2; quantum tamen rudibus et imperfectis, quales nos sumus, utilis sit, vos ipsi, fratres, vobis testes estis; quomodo scilicet redimit de corruptione vitam nostram amaritudo parcimoniae et laboris. Ipsi etiam scitis quomodo vermescerent corda, quomodo vermescerent corpora, si non quotidie de manibus laborantium distillaret myrrha. An non vermis luxuria? Nescio si alter nocentior. Ingreditur blandiendo, mordet ridendo, transforat delectando, perimit consensu voluntario. An non vermis acedia et tristitia? Sicut tinea, inquit, vestimento, et vermis ligno, ita tristitia viri nocet cordi (Prov. xxv, 20). Quid omnia desideria mala? an non vermes et ipsa? At in desideriis est omnis otiosus, et desideria occidunt pigrum (Id. xxi, 26, 25). Beatus igitur cui ad mortificandos vermes hujuscemodi, manus propriae distillant myrrham per universum sui, jamque praevenit ungere corpus suum in sepulturam. Sed suum dicam, an Jesu? Imo et Jesu. Corpus enim illius, membrum est Jesu. Quisquis ille est qui sic unctus est, non est quod timeat vermem qui non moritur; quia jam mortuus est ei vermis unde illi moritur.

English translation

4. Yet although for the perfect, such as Timothy was, bodily exercise is useful for little in comparison with godliness (1 Timothy iv, 5)2, you yourselves, brothers, can testify how useful it is for beginners and the imperfect, such as we are: how the bitterness of frugality and toil redeems our life from corruption. You yourselves know how hearts would breed worms, how bodies would breed worms, if myrrh did not drip daily from the hands of those who labour. Is lust not a worm? I do not know whether another is more harmful. It enters by flattering, bites with a laugh, pierces by giving pleasure, and kills through voluntary consent. Are sloth and sadness not a worm? As the moth, he says, harms clothing, and the worm wood, so a man's sadness harms his heart (Proverbs xxv, 20). What of all evil desires? Are they not worms too? But every idle man is among desires, and desires kill the sluggard (ibid. xxi, 26, 25). Blessed, then, is the man whose own hands drip myrrh over his whole being to mortify worms of this kind, and who thus anoints his body beforehand for burial. But should I say his body, or Jesus'? Indeed, Jesus' too. For his body is a member of Jesus. Whoever is thus anointed has no reason to fear the worm that does not die, because the worm through which he dies has already died for him.

Latin original

5. Myrrham itaque, et myrrham electam atque probatissimam Domino cum regibus rex et ipse obtulisti, si interim, cum non sufficit manus tua ad offerendum aurum sapientiae aut thus devotionis, saltem cor contritum corpusque afflictum cum amaritudine poenitentiae Domino sanctificasti. Existimo enim, sine praejudicio tamen melioris intellectus, myrrham esse primam oblationem incipientium, deinde thus proficientium, demumque aurum perfectorum; atque idcirco cum simul ab Evangelista nominata sunt, pretiosiora quaeque et digniora pro merito sui praelata esse in ordine nominum. Alias namque secundum ordinem profectuum myrrham thuri praepositam invenis, ut est illud, Sicut virgula fumi ex aromatibus myrrhae et thuris (Cantic. iii, 6). Et, Vadam ad montem myrrhae, et ad collem thuris (Id. iv, 6). Non parvus siquidem profectus est, cum de myrrha ad thus proficitur, cum de ista, quae ad usus est infirmitatis humanae, ad illud quod divinis et festivis deputatum est sacrificiis, pervenitur; ut qui sacrificium spiritus contribulati, corporisque humiliati offerebat non sine myrrha amaritudinis, jam sacrificium laudis offerat cum thure devotionis, et, juxta promissionem Domini, tanta consolatio immittatur lugentibus Sion, ut det eis coronam pro cinere, oleum gaudii pro luctu, pallium laudis pro spiritu moeroris (Isa. lxi, 3). Tunc cantat qui lugebat: Convertisti planctum meum in gaudium mihi, conscidisti saccum meum, spiritum moeroris; et circumdedisti me laetitia, pallio scilicet laudis, ut cantet tibi gloria mea, et non compungar (Psal. xxix, 12, 13). Gaudens enim gaudebo in Domino, et exsultabit anima mea in Deo meo, quia induit me vestimento salutis, et indumento justitiae circumdedit me (Isa. lxi, 10).

English translation

5. You too, then, have offered myrrh to the Lord with the kings, yourself a king, and myrrh choice and most excellent, if meanwhile, when your hand cannot supply the gold of wisdom or the incense of devotion, you have at least consecrated to the Lord a contrite heart and an afflicted body with the bitterness of penance. For I think, without prejudice to a better understanding, that myrrh is the first offering of beginners, then incense that of those making progress, and finally gold that of the perfect; and that, when the Evangelist named them together, each more precious and worthy gift was therefore placed earlier in the order of names according to its worth. Elsewhere you find myrrh placed before incense according to the order of progress, as in the saying, Like a slender column of smoke from the spices of myrrh and incense (Song of Songs iii, 6); and, I will go to the mountain of myrrh and to the hill of incense (ibid. iv, 6). Indeed, it is no small progress to advance from myrrh to incense, from what serves human infirmity to what is assigned to divine and festal sacrifices; so that he who offered the sacrifice of a troubled spirit and a humbled body, not without the myrrh of bitterness, may now offer a sacrifice of praise with the incense of devotion. And, according to the Lord's promise, such consolation is sent to those mourning in Sion that he gives them a crown for ashes, the oil of joy for mourning, a mantle of praise for the spirit of sorrow (Isaiah lxi, 3). Then he who mourned sings: You have turned my lament into joy for me; you have torn my sackcloth, the spirit of sorrow; and surrounded me with gladness, that is, the mantle of praise, so that my glory may sing to you and I may not be pierced with sorrow (Psalm xxix, 12, 13). For rejoicing I will rejoice in the Lord, and my soul will exult in my God, because he has clothed me in the garment of salvation and surrounded me with the robe of justice (Isaiah lxi, 10).

Latin original

6. O fratres, quibus minus sufficiunt vestimenta vestrae paupertatis in hac asperitate hiemis, qui dicitis, Ante faciem frigoris hujus quis sustinebit? cur non induimini, quaeso, hoc vestimento laetitiae et salutis, hoc pallio laudis? Si Dominum laudatis, in Domino laudabitur anima vestra, et pallio laudis quo opertus est laudabilis nimis, vos quoque operiet et confovebit, quemadmodum gallina pullos suos sub alis, si modo velitis. At quoties voluit, et noluistis! Nunquid enim hoc pallium breve est, ut utrumque operire non possit? Non angustiamini, inquit, in nobis, angustiamini autem in visceribus vestris. Dilatamini et vos (II Cor. vi, 12, 13), et pallium dilatabit se usque ad vos. Non est abbreviata manus ejus, ut salvare nequeat: nec grande est ei ut consoletur nos, nisi pravitas nostra prohibeat. O qui algetis, pallium hoc calidum est; quia calor est, qui non modo interius, sed exterius calefacere potest. Nonne vestimenta tua calida sunt, cum terra tua perflata fuerit austro (Job xxxvii, 17); imo ignis missus in ossibus, ac vehementer accensus (Thren. i, 13)? Hoc igne thus illud adole, quod ubi cum regibus obtuleris, dirigatur oratio tua sicut incensum in conspectu Domini: et cum de sacrificio tuo odoratus fuerit odorem suavitatis, dicat tibi, Odor vestimentorum tuorum sicut odor thuris (Cantic. iv, 11).

English translation

6. O brothers, whose poor clothing does not suffice in the harshness of this winter, who say, Who can endure before the face of this cold? Why, I ask, do you not put on this garment of gladness and salvation, this mantle of praise? If you praise the Lord, your soul will be praised in the Lord, and with the mantle of praise in which he who is exceedingly praiseworthy is clothed, he will cover and warm you too, as a hen her chicks beneath her wings, if only you will. But how often he has willed it, and you have not! Is this mantle so short that it cannot cover both? You are not straitened in us, he says, but in your own affections. Be enlarged yourselves too (2 Corinthians vi, 12, 13), and the mantle will widen to reach you. His hand is not shortened so that he cannot save; nor is it difficult for him to console us, unless our wickedness prevents it. O you who are cold, this mantle is warm; for it is a warmth that can heat not only within but without. Are not your garments warm when the south wind has blown over your land (Job xxxvii, 17); indeed, is it not fire sent into the bones and fiercely kindled (Lamentations i, 13)? With this fire burn that incense; when you have offered it with the kings, let your prayer be directed like incense before the Lord. And when he has smelled a sweet fragrance from your sacrifice, may he say to you: The fragrance of your garments is like the fragrance of incense (Song of Songs iv, 11).

7. Jam vero de auro sapientiae (quae oblatio, ut diximus, perfectorum est) loquatur vobis qui illud assecutus est, quia nec novit illud nisi qui accipit. Qui ergo sapientiam didicit, sapientiam loquatur inter perfectos; et de auro quod possidet, murenulas aureas auribus vestris fabricet. Ego enim non arbitror comprehendisse me; sequor autem si quo modo comprehendam (Philipp. iii, 13, 12). Magnus prorsus ac beatus qui invenit sapientiam, et affluit prudentia: sapientiam, in contemplatione aeternorum; prudentiam, in administratione temporalium: vel, ut moderatius sapientem definiam, qui novit se ipsum regere et alium. Pretiosus est hoc aurum3 cunctis opibus, etiam thure et myrrha; et omnia quae desiderantur in virtutibus vel gratiis, huic non valent comparari. Denique sapiens, tanquam vir virtutum, dives in omnibus, et si praevalet aurum offerre in sublimi contemplatione vel prudenti administratione, non tamen negligit offere thus in devotione divini operis, myrrham in mortificatione sui. Nos igitur, fratres, quod habemus offeramus in gloriam novi Regis: quod minus habemus, ab ipso petamus cui offerre cupimus. Si quis indiget sapientia, postulet a Deo, qui dat omnibus affluenter (Jac. i, 5). Munera mea, data mea, ait Pater luminum, a quo omne donum perfectum, et omne datum optimum; ipsi gloria in saecula saeculorum. Amen.

English translation

7. Now let one who has attained it speak to you of the gold of wisdom, which, as we have said, is the offering of the perfect; for no one knows it except the one who receives it. Let him, then, who has learned wisdom speak wisdom among the perfect, and from the gold he possesses fashion little golden ornaments for your ears. For I do not consider that I have attained; but I press on, if somehow I may attain (Philippians iii, 13, 12). Truly great and blessed is the man who finds wisdom and abounds in prudence: wisdom in contemplating eternal things, prudence in administering temporal things; or, to define the wise man more modestly, one who knows how to govern himself and another. This gold is more precious3 than all wealth, even incense and myrrh; and all that is desired in virtues or graces cannot compare with it. Finally, the wise man, as a man of virtues, rich in all things, even when able to offer gold in lofty contemplation or prudent administration, nevertheless does not neglect to offer incense in devotion to the divine office, and myrrh in the mortification of himself. Let us, then, brothers, offer what we have to the glory of the new King; what we lack, let us ask from him to whom we desire to offer it. If anyone lacks wisdom, let him ask of God, who gives abundantly to all (James i, 5). My gifts, my offerings, says the Father of lights, from whom comes every perfect gift and every bestowal that is best; to him be glory for ever and ever. Amen.
