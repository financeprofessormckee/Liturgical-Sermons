Editorial introduction

Guerric reads Christ's questions to the crowds about John: no reed shaken by the wind, no man in soft garments, but a prophet. John's firmness before Herod, his contempt of bodily comfort and his prophetic grace are set against those who seek ease. Those who cannot match John's rough clothing and food should at least not be reeds in the wind. The sermon appears under the heading for the Nativity of Saint John the Baptist in the 1879 edition of Patrologia Latina. The collection is associated approximately with Guerric's abbacy in 1138–1157; the individual sermon is undated.

English translation

On fortitude and mortification.

¶cols. 173-174
Latin original

1. Coepit Jesus dicere ad turbas de Joanne: Quid existis videre in deserto? (Matth. XI, 7.) Ecce illud quod sponsa de sponso gloriatur in Cantico amoris sui, Ego dilecto meo, et dilectus meus mihi (Cantic. II, 16): Joannes Jesu, et Jesus Joanni. Joannes Jesum praedicat, Joannem Jesus commendat. Par pari redditur, et tam amica quam justa vicissitudine charitas in invicem provocatur, aut remuneratur. Ego enim, inquit Jesus, diligentes me diligo, et glorificantes me glorificabo (Prov. VIII, 17). Habet pietas promissionem vitae quae nunc est, et futurae; et Dominus dilectum sibi quem glorificaturus est in futuro redditione praemii, etiam nunc incipit glorificare redditione [Al. exhibitione] praeconii, testemque suum etiam apud homines proprii sublimat honore testimonii. Quid existis, inquit, videre in deserto? Vos hominem miramini commorantem in deserto: sed per istum pinguescent speciosa deserti, et florebit solitudo, cum ubique novi cultores eremi de Joannis succrescent exemplo. Tunc erit desertum quasi deliciae paradisi, et solitudo quasi hortus Domini. Tunc gloria Libani dabitur ei, decor Carmeli et Saron (Isai. XXXV, 2). Sed quid, inquam, existis videre in deserto? arundinem vento agitatam? (Matth. XI, 7.) Et si moretur in desertis, non est tamen arundo deserti, sed cedrus paradisi, columna coeli, gloria generis humani, miraculum mundi, virtute et merito ultra mensuram hominum, conditione citra naturam angelorum.

English translation

1. Jesus began to say to the crowds concerning John: What did you go out into the desert to see? (Matth. XI, 7.) Behold that which the bride boasts of concerning the bridegroom in the Song of her love: I am my beloved's, and my beloved is mine (Cantic. II, 16): John is Jesus', and Jesus is John's. John preaches Jesus, Jesus commends John. Like is repaid with like, and charity, by so friendly as well as just a reciprocity, is either provoked or rewarded in turn. For Jesus says, I love those who love me, and I will glorify those who glorify me (Prov. VIII, 17). Godliness has the promise of the life that now is, and of that which is to come; and the one dear to him, whom the Lord will glorify in the future by the giving of a reward, he even now begins to glorify by the giving [Other reading: showing] of a proclamation, and he exalts his own witness, even among men, by the honor of his testimony. What, he says, did you go out to see in the desert? You marvel at a man dwelling in the desert: but through him the fair places of the desert shall grow fat, and the solitude shall flourish, when everywhere new cultivators of the wilderness shall spring up after John's example. Then the desert shall be as the delights of paradise, and the solitude as the garden of the Lord. Then the glory of Lebanon shall be given to it, the beauty of Carmel and Sharon (Isai. XXXV, 2). But what, I say, did you go out to see in the desert? A reed shaken by the wind? (Matth. XI, 7.) And though he dwells in the deserts, yet he is not a reed of the desert, but a cedar of paradise, a pillar of heaven, the glory of the human race, the wonder of the world, beyond the measure of men in virtue and merit, yet short of the nature of angels in his condition.

2. Non est arundo vento agitata, sed palma fortior omni procella, quam nullus turbo quatiat: aut certe cypressus in monte Sion aeternae plantata, altius scilicet quam ut ventorum rabiem timeat. Non est obnoxius tempestatibus hujus aeris, quia superior est cunctis cupiditatibus mundi. In coelo radicem fixit, quo nullus spiritus procellarum aspirat, unde minas et bella ventorum, et cuncta in saeculo adversantia securus irrideat. Irascatur Herodes, insidietur Herodias, de interioribus eorum ebulliat tempestas, ac nequam spiritus procellarum, qui in abyssis cordium eorum cubat, omnes vires suas et argumenta malitiae commoveat: nihil illum terrebit, nihil a sententia movebit, quominus libere nuptias eorum incestas arguat. Quomodo enim flecterent adversa, quem mollire non potuerunt prospera? Flaverunt siquidem aliquando et molles, atque blandientes venti, aurae scilicet favoris popularis: sed nec illis a rectitudine sua potuit inclinari. Vos misistis ad Joannem et testimonium perhibuit veritati. Miserunt enim Judaei sacerdotes et levitas, ut interrogarent eum, Tu quis es? Et confessus est, et non negavit: Quia non sum ego Christus (Joan. I, 19, 20): cum tamen ei flaret favor popularis, et blandiretur existimatio communis, non ut Christum praedicaret se esse, sed ut credentibus tantum non contradiceret. Sed et alias cum discipuli ejus dicerent ei, Rabbi, cui tu testimonium perhibuisti, ecce hic baptizat, et omnes veniunt ad eum: numquid hoc vento potuit agitari, aut de sua desertione, et alterius frequentia et gloria, ad dolorem vel invidiam moveri? Imo constantia immobili ventum repellens et frangens, respondit: Ipsi vos mihi testimonium perhibetis, quia dixerim, Non sum ego Christus, sed quia missus sum ante illum (Joan. III, 26, 28).

English translation

2. He is not a reed shaken by the wind, but a palm stronger than any storm, which no whirlwind can shake: or rather a cypress planted on the eternal Mount Sion, that is, planted too high to fear the raging of the winds. He is not subject to the storms of this air, because he is above all the lusts of the world. He has fixed his root in heaven, where no spirit of storms breathes, whence he may securely mock the threats and wars of the winds, and all things in the world that are opposed to him. Let Herod rage, let Herodias lay snares, let the storm boil up from their inward parts, and let the wicked spirit of the tempests, which lies in the depths of their hearts, stir up all his powers and the devices of his malice: nothing will terrify him, nothing will move him from his resolve to freely rebuke their incestuous marriage. For how could adversities bend him whom prosperity could not soften? For once gentle and flattering winds also blew—namely the breezes of popular favor: but not even by these could he be bent from his uprightness. You sent to John, and he bore witness to the truth. For the Jews sent priests and Levites to ask him, Who are you? And he confessed, and did not deny: I am not the Christ (Joan. I, 19, 20): although the favor of the people was blowing upon him, and common opinion was flattering him, not that he should preach himself to be the Christ, but that he should merely not contradict those who believed it. But also at another time, when his disciples said to him, Rabbi, he to whom you bore witness, behold, he baptizes, and all come to him: could he be shaken by this wind, or be moved to sorrow or envy at his own forsaking and another's increase and glory? Rather, with unshaken constancy repelling and breaking the wind, he answered: You yourselves bear me witness that I said, I am not the Christ, but that I am sent before him (Joan. III, 26, 28).

3. Sed quid existis videre? hominem mollibus vestitum? Laudaverat eum de constantia animi, et nunc laudat de contemptu corporis sui, ac postremo de superexcellentia gratiae prophetalis, atque dignitate angelici officii et nominis. His etenim duobus gradibus ad spiritualia ac divina nititur humilitas humana; si videlicet adversus irruentium tentationum ventos immobilis persistat, et desiderio spiritualium bonorum corpus affligat. Sicut enim in initiis, ut vincamus tentationes, corpus est castigandum, ne regnet in eo peccatum: sic etiam victis tentationibus in eodem tamen est persistendum, non solum metu recidendi, sed etiam desiderio proficiendi; ut scilicet de mortificatione carnis spiritus vegetior fiat, et quanto levius ac subtilius vinculum gestat, tanto ad spiritualia liberior assurgat. Joannes qui antequam exiret de ventre, sanctificatus erat, cui illud dicere competebat: Ab infantia mea mecum crevit sanctitas, et de utero matris meae egressa est mecum (Job XXXI, 18); quem nullus tentationis ventus agitare poterat: nec mollibus tamen vestiebatur, nec suavibus vescebatur; sciens utique, quia sicut peccatoribus, ut sancti fiant, sic sanctis, ut sanctiores fiant, necessaria est severior vivendi disciplina. Nunc autem Deo gratias, qui dedit nobis, si tamen dedit, sine pugna victoriam, sine poenitentia veniam, sine opere justitiam, sanctimoniam sine labore, deliciis carnalibus simul et spiritualibus affluere. Induimur, si non purpura et bysso, certe mollioribus et calidioribus quam sint purpura et byssus; et epulamur quotidie splendide: et nihilominus distenti et ructantes crapulam, dormiemus cum Lazaro quondam paupere in sinu Abrahae, vel potius in sinu Christi cum Joanne? Plane si ita est, melius nobiscum actum est, quam cum illis, qui tantis emere laboribus quod nos delicati gratuito possidemus. Plane si ita est, ridenda est magis quam praedicanda vita Joannis: et omnem nationem filiorum Dei, qui dura et arcta via secuti sunt Unicum Patris, reprobare magis expedit, quam imitari.

English translation

3. But what did you go out to see? A man clothed in soft garments? He had praised him for steadfastness of soul, and now he praises him for contempt of his own body, and finally for the surpassing excellence of his prophetic grace, and the dignity of his angelic office and name. For by these two steps human humility strains toward spiritual and divine things: namely, if it stands unmoved against the winds of assailing temptations, and afflicts the body out of desire for spiritual goods. For just as at the beginning, in order to conquer temptations, the body must be chastised, lest sin reign in it, so also, once temptations have been conquered, one must nevertheless persist in the same discipline, not only from fear of falling back but also from desire of advancing—so that, by the mortification of the flesh, the spirit may become more vigorous, and the lighter and finer the bond it bears, the freer it may rise toward spiritual things. John, who was sanctified before he came forth from the womb, to whom it was fitting to say, 'From my infancy holiness grew up with me, and it went forth with me from my mother's womb' (Job XXXI, 18); whom no wind of temptation could stir—yet he was not clothed in soft garments, nor did he feed on delicacies; knowing well that, just as for sinners in order to become holy, so also for the holy in order to become holier, a stricter discipline of life is necessary. But now, thanks be to God, who has given to us—if indeed he has given it—victory without struggle, pardon without penance, righteousness without labor, holiness without toil, so that we may abound in carnal and spiritual delights at once! We are clothed, if not in purple and fine linen, certainly in garments softer and warmer than purple and fine linen; and we feast splendidly every day; and nevertheless, bloated and belching from our surfeit, shall we sleep with Lazarus, once a poor man, in the bosom of Abraham—or rather, in the bosom of Christ with John? Clearly, if this is so, it has gone better for us than for those who had to purchase with such great labors what we, in our softness, possess as a free gift. Clearly, if this is so, the life of John is more to be laughed at than preached, and it is more fitting to reject than to imitate the whole nation of the sons of God who followed the Only-begotten of the Father along a hard and narrow way.

4. Verisimilius autem videtur quod non ita est; sed magis sicut Veritas dicit, quia per multas tribulationes oportet nos intrare in regnum Dei (Act. XIV, 21): et sicut discipulus Veritatis perhibet, quia vidua in deliciis vivens mortua est (I Tim. V, 5, 6). Ideo Dominus cum de asperitate vestis laudasset amicum in omni domo sua fidelissimum, Ecce, inquit, qui mollibus vestiuntur, in domibus regum sunt (Matth. XI, 8): volens nimirum intelligi, quia qui sectantur delicias carnis, regno hujus saeculi militant, non regno Dei. Heu! quam terribilem sententiam coelitus audivi contra mollitiem illius magnae et purpuratae meretricis! Quantum, inquit, glorificavit se et in deliciis fuit, tantum date illi tormentum et luctum (Apoc. XVIII, 7). Utinam tamen his deliciis, tantum mollitie vestium, et suavitate epularum contenta esset tenuitudo delicatorum, nec alias spinas vitiorum germinaret in eis rhamnus voluptatum, ut hoc esset omne peccatum eorum, deliciari, non luxuriari! Ipsi viderint, si ita exstinctus est in eis ingenitus ille pestifer ignis, ut tot fomentis circumpositis nequeat reaccendi; praesertim ventis ex adverso flantibus, id est, verbis et risibus lascivis tentantibus. Soleo tamen audire quia saepe vivit etiam in moriente, viget in sene: suscitatur et exsurgit etiam sine fomite, furit nullo irritante. Ipsi ergo viderint sibi. Nihil enim mihi de aliorum conscientiis judicare, cum sciam multos in omni copia et gloria mundi temperanter et pudice vixisse: et e diverso nonnullos in habitu asperiore et victu parciore nequiter egisse. Sed et scio regem Israel sub purpura sua cilicio ad carnem fuisse indutum (IV Reg. VI, 30); et panem solum nonnunquam regibus appositum: cum hodie delicatissima quaeque ac mollia tegat pulla vestis poenitentium, vel magis poenitentiam profitentium; et fastidiis eorum non satis sint opes mundi, non satisfaciant artes et tormenta coquorum. Verum quia in hujusmodi causis facile quisque sibi invenit patrocinium; alius, infirmitatis, alius, societatis cui morem gerit; alius, honoris quem gerit: esto, vivant ut volunt, comedant ut volunt; tantum ne excedat ulterius hoc malum, ad id scilicet, quod qui perpetrare volunt, confiteri nolunt, quia tueri non possunt; et incipiant notari proverbio mulieris nequam, quae comedit, et tergens os suum dicit: Non sum operata malum (Prov. XXX, 20). Nam et de magna illa Babylone, de cujus medio delicati poenitentes isti videbantur exisse, audeam dicere: Quantum vult purpurata sit, quantum vult delicata sit; tantum meretrix non sit, nec divaricet pedes suos omni transeunti.

English translation

4. But it seems more likely that this is not so; rather, as the Truth says, that we must enter the kingdom of God through many tribulations (Act. XIV, 21), and as the disciple of the Truth testifies, that the widow who lives in delights is dead (I Tim. V, 5, 6). Therefore, when the Lord had praised his friend—most faithful in all his house—for the roughness of his garment, he said, 'Behold, those who are clothed in soft garments are in the houses of kings' (Matth. XI, 8): wishing it to be understood, no doubt, that those who pursue the delights of the flesh serve the kingdom of this world, not the kingdom of God. Alas! What a terrible sentence I have heard from heaven against the softness of that great harlot clothed in purple! 'As much,' it says, 'as she glorified herself and lived in delights, so much torment and mourning give to her' (Apoc. XVIII, 7). Would that the frailty of the delicate were content with these delights alone—the softness of garments and the sweetness of feasts—and that the bramble of pleasures did not also germinate other thorns of vice in them, so that this alone might be their whole sin: to take delight, not to indulge in lust! Let them see to it themselves, whether that inborn pestilent fire is so extinguished in them that it cannot be rekindled though so much kindling is heaped around it—especially when winds blow against it from the other side, that is, when lascivious words and laughter tempt it. Yet I am accustomed to hear that it often lives even in the dying man, and thrives in the old man; it is stirred up and rises even without kindling, it rages though nothing incites it. Let them, then, see to it themselves. For it is nothing to me to judge the consciences of others, since I know that many have lived temperately and chastely amid all the abundance and glory of the world, while, on the other hand, some have acted wickedly under a rougher habit and a more frugal diet. But I also know that the king of Israel wore, under his purple, a hairshirt next to his flesh (IV Reg. VI, 30), and that sometimes bread alone was set before kings—whereas today the dark garment of penitents, or rather of those who merely profess penitence, covers the most delicate and soft of things, and the riches of the world are not enough to satisfy their fastidiousness, nor do the arts and torments of cooks suffice. But since in such matters everyone easily finds himself an excuse—one, the excuse of infirmity; another, of the company whose custom he follows; another, of the rank he bears—so be it: let them live as they wish, let them eat as they wish; only let this evil go no further, namely, to the point where those who wish to commit it are unwilling to confess it, because they cannot defend it, and begin to be marked by the proverb of the wicked woman, who eats, and wiping her mouth says, 'I have done no evil' (Prov. XXX, 20). For indeed, concerning that great Babylon, out of whose midst these delicate penitents seem to have come forth, I would dare to say: Let her be clothed in purple as much as she wishes, let her be as delicate as she wishes; only let her not be a harlot, nor spread her legs for every passerby.

5. Tu ergo si non potes imitari Joannem in asperitate vestitus, vel parcimonia victus: vel in hoc imitari contende, ut non sis arundo vento agitata; et, ut Sapiens ait, Non te ventiles in omnem ventum, et non eas in omnem viam. Sic enim peccator probatur in duplici lingua. Sed esto firmus, inquit, in via Domini (Eccli. V, 11, 12), ne te projiciat ventus a facie terrae seu loci, cui professus es; seu regni, cui praeparandus es. Solet repente ventus vehemens irruere a regione deserti, et concutere quatuor angulos domus filiorum Job (Job I, 19), et fit ruina ejus magna, si tamen super arenam fundata sit, non supra petram; si arundinea sit, non lapidea: imo si is qui aedificat eam, et inhabitat, arundo sit; foris per hypocrisim nitidus, jactantia vel promissione cassa sonorus; intus veritate vacuus. In secreto hujus arundinis Behemoth dormire amat, quae et in locis humentibus (Job XL, 10, 16), ubi nulla fluxarum rerum penuria, sed ad luxum superfluunt omnia, saepius et uberius crescit atque luxuriat (Isai. XXXVI, 7). Huic arundini si quis innitatur, manum ejus perforabit: quia si quis in adjutorium operis eum asciverit, vel alicujus ei muneris functionem imposuerit, sperantem in se gravi scandalo vulnerabit. Patientia tamen Domini hujusmodi arundinem etiam quassatam non conterit, sed sustinet, ut in melius convertatur (Isa. XXIV, 20). Nonnunquam etiam increpat feras arundinis (Psal. LXVII, 31), quae cum principe suo in secreto ejus dormiunt: nonnunquam justi tanquam scintillae aeris candentis in arundineto discurrunt (Sap. III, 7), qui tanquam carbones desolatorii, quidquid in eo steriliter silvescit incendunt, novisque fructibus purum praeparant locum. Hoc agebat Joannes, vox clamantis in deserto, non arundo, sed scintilla in arundineto: cujus sermo, ut scriptum est, sermo scintillae ad commovendum cor (Sap. II, 2) pigrum et stolidum, ut pararet viam Domino. Hoc etiam nunc in nobis agat eadem vox, cooperante Verbo ipso, cujus erat vox, Jesu Christo Domino nostro, qui vivit et regnat per omnia saecula saeculorum. Amen.

English translation

5. You, then, if you cannot imitate John in roughness of clothing or frugality of food, strive at least to imitate him in this: that you not be a reed shaken by the wind; and, as the Wise man says, "Do not winnow yourself to every wind, and do not go in every way." For in this way a sinner is proved to have a double tongue. But "be firm," he says, "in the way of the Lord" (Eccli. V, 11, 12), lest the wind cast you down from the face of the land or place to which you have professed yourself, or of the kingdom for which you are to be made ready. A violent wind is accustomed to rush in suddenly from the region of the desert and strike the four corners of the house of the sons of Job (Job I, 19), and its ruin is great — if, that is, it was founded upon sand and not upon rock; if it was made of reed and not of stone; or rather, if the one who builds it and dwells in it is himself a reed — outwardly sleek through hypocrisy, resounding with boasting or with empty promise, but within empty of truth. In the hiding-place of such a reed Behemoth loves to sleep, a reed that also in moist places (Job XL, 10, 16) — where there is no lack of transient things, but all things overflow unto luxury — grows and flourishes more often and more abundantly (Isai. XXXVI, 7). If anyone leans upon this reed, it will pierce his hand: for if anyone takes him on to help in some work, or lays upon him the carrying out of some office, he will wound with grievous scandal the one who trusted in him. Yet the patience of the Lord does not crush even a reed of this kind when it is bruised, but sustains it, that it may be turned to something better (Isa. XXIV, 20). Sometimes, too, he rebukes the beasts of the reed (Psal. LXVII, 31), who sleep with their prince in its hiding-place: and sometimes the just run to and fro in the reed-bed like sparks of glowing bronze (Sap. III, 7), who, like desolating coals, burn up whatever grows there wild and barren, and make ready a clean place for new fruits. This is what John did, the voice of one crying in the desert — not a reed, but a spark within the reed-bed: whose speech, as it is written, was "the speech of a spark" (Sap. II, 2) to stir the sluggish and foolish heart, that he might prepare the way of the Lord. May that same voice now also work this in us, with the cooperation of the Word himself, whose voice it was — Jesus Christ our Lord, who lives and reigns through all ages of ages. Amen.
