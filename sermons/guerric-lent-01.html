Introduction & Scripture references

Editorial introduction

Guerric distinguishes sorrow over adversity from sorrow over sin. Consolation, confession and penance lead into his account of purification through love, suffering and the judgment to come. The sermon appears in the 1879 edition of Patrologia Latina. The collection is associated approximately with Guerric's abbacy in 1138–1157; the individual sermon is undated.

De tribulatione et consolatione.

English translation

On Tribulation and Consolation.

¶cols. 91-92; cols. 93-94
Latin original

1. Benedictus Deus, Pater misericordiarum et Deus totius consolationis, qui consolatur nos in omni tribulatione nostra (II Cor. 1, 3, 4). Multae tribulationes justorum, et de omnibus his liberabit eos Dominus (Psal. XXXIII, 20). Duplex autem genus tribulationum patimur, quia non solum duplici natura subsistimus, carne scilicet ac spiritu, sed et duplici qualitate vivimus; partim scilicet carnaliter, partim spiritualiter. In mundo eramus toti carnales; in coelo erimus toti spirituales: nunc autem ex parte carnales, ex parte spirituales. Prout autem proficimus in spiritu, aut magis efficimur spirituales et minus carnales, aut magis remanemus carnales et minus spirituales. Propterea duplex modo consurgit tribulatio; quia, quod in nobis carnale est, contristat adversitas; quod spirituale, iniquitas. Si nihil in nobis esset carnale, nulla nos contristaret, imo nulla esset adversitas: si nihil spirituale, non nos nostra contristaret, sed magis delectaret iniquitas, sicut de quibusdam dicitur: Laetantur cum male fecerint, et exsultant in rebus pessimis (Prov. II, 14). Illa ergo nostra de adversitate tristitia, carnalis et secundum saeculum est: haec autem de iniquitate, spiritualis et secundum Deum. Illa mortem operatur, si desit consolatio: haec salutem operatur, si non desit consolatio. Multum tamen interest in adversitate exteriori, cujusmodi sit consolatio tristium; quia consolationi divitum comminatio divina vae intentat aeternum (Luc. VI, 24). Quia nimirum plerumque qui humiliari poterant ex adversitate, gravius superbiunt de prosperitate, proditque ex adipe iniquitas eorum: imo et audacter provocant Deum, cum ipse dederit omnia in manibus eorum.

English translation

1. Blessed be God, the Father of mercies and the God of all comfort, who comforts us in all our tribulation (II Cor. 1, 3, 4). Many are the tribulations of the just, and out of all these the Lord will deliver them (Psal. XXXIII, 20). Now we suffer a twofold kind of tribulation, because we not only subsist in a twofold nature—namely flesh and spirit—but also live by a twofold quality; that is, partly carnally, partly spiritually. In the world we were wholly carnal; in heaven we shall be wholly spiritual: but now we are partly carnal, partly spiritual. But insofar as we advance in the spirit, either we become more spiritual and less carnal, or we remain more carnal and less spiritual. Therefore a twofold tribulation now arises; because adversity grieves what is carnal in us, while iniquity grieves what is spiritual. If there were nothing carnal in us, no adversity would grieve us—indeed, there would be no adversity at all; if there were nothing spiritual, our own iniquity would not grieve us, but would rather delight us, as is said of certain men: 'They are glad when they have done evil, and rejoice in most wicked things' (Prov. II, 14). That sorrow of ours, then, which comes from adversity, is carnal and according to the world; but this sorrow, which comes from iniquity, is spiritual and according to God. The former works death, if consolation is lacking; the latter works salvation, if consolation is not lacking. Yet in outward adversity it matters greatly what kind of consolation the sorrowful have; for upon the consolation of the rich the divine threat holds out eternal woe (Luc. VI, 24). For indeed it often happens that those who could have been humbled by adversity grow more gravely proud through prosperity, and their iniquity comes forth from their fatness: nay, they even boldly provoke God, though he himself has given all things into their hands.

Latin original

2. Nos autem et cum abundat illa consolatio temporalis, oportet esse gratiores et humiliores: et cum deficit, de aeterna redemptione certiores et laetiores. Cum tamen suppetit sive salus corporum, sive tranquillitas temporum aut copia rerum; ea moderatione utenda ac dispensanda est, ut sit non occasio peccati, sed adjutorium virtutis, ac potius de bonis bona faciamus quam de bonis mali fiamus. Illa namque consolatione, qua se diffundit voluptas, vel extollit vanitas, renuat consolari anima mea (Hebr. XII, 5); magisque eligat cum Moyse et viris Israeliticis affligi, quam temporalis peccati habere jucunditatem. Sive autem consolemur exterius, sive tribulemur, benedictus Deus, qui consolationem internam et aeternam dedit in cordibus nostris, id est, gaudium spei, quae suadet nos etiam gloriari in tribulationibus nostris, pollicens, quia si compatimur, et conregnabimus (II Tim. II, 12). Illam sane tribulationem, quam interius de iniquitate nostra patimur, tanto molestiorem portamus, quanto periculosiorem experimur; quia nimirum quotidie de vita periclitamur, ut veraciter confiteamur: Nisi quia Dominus adjuvit me, paulo minus habitasset in inferno anima mea. Gratias tibi, Domine Jesu, quia si gravia sunt pericula, facilia sunt remedia; quia, si dicebam, Motus est pes meus; misericordia tua, Domine, adjuvabat me (Psal. XCIII, 17, 18). Et quod semel in Petro figurasti corporaliter (Matth. XIV, 29-31), in filiis Petri quotidie adimples spiritualiter. Jussisti nos venire ad te super aquas, id est, hoc mare magnum et spatiosum manibus: ecce virtute tua ambulamus, sed pondere nostro gravamur, et plerumque irruente spiritu procellarum impellimur, ut etiam mergi incipiamus, id est, pene jam jamque tentatori consentiamus. Sed, si celeriter confiteamur pedem motum, id est, titubasse animum, et fideliter invocemus auxilium, tu misericorditer porrigis manum, firmasque et dirigis gressum.

English translation

2. But we, when that temporal consolation abounds, ought to be the more grateful and the more humble; and when it fails, the more certain and the more joyful concerning the eternal redemption. Yet when there is at hand either health of body, or tranquility of the times, or abundance of goods, it must be used and administered with such moderation, that it may be not an occasion of sin but a help to virtue, and that we may rather make good things out of goods than become evil men because of goods. For let my soul refuse to be consoled by that consolation in which pleasure spreads itself abroad, or vanity exalts itself (Hebr. XII, 5); and let it rather choose to be afflicted with Moses and the men of Israel, than to have the temporal delight of sin. But whether we be consoled outwardly or be afflicted, blessed be God, who has given an inward and eternal consolation in our hearts, that is, the joy of hope, which persuades us even to glory in our tribulations, promising that if we suffer with him, we shall also reign with him (II Tim. II, 12). That tribulation indeed, which we suffer inwardly from our own iniquity, we bear it as all the more troublesome, the more dangerous we find it to be; for indeed daily we are in peril of our life, so that we may truly confess: 'Unless the Lord had helped me, my soul had almost dwelt in hell.' Thanks be to thee, Lord Jesus, for though the dangers are grievous, the remedies are easy; for, 'if I said: My foot is moved; thy mercy, O Lord, assisted me' (Psal. XCIII, 17, 18). And that which thou didst once figure bodily in Peter (Matth. XIV, 29-31), thou dost daily fulfill spiritually in the sons of Peter. Thou hast bidden us come to thee upon the waters, that is, upon this great and wide sea: behold, by thy power we walk, but we are weighed down by our own weight, and often, when the spirit of storms rushes in, we are driven on so that we even begin to sink—that is, we are almost on the very point of consenting to the tempter. But if we quickly confess that our foot is moved, that is, that our soul has wavered, and faithfully call upon thy help, thou dost mercifully stretch forth thy hand, and dost steady and direct our step.

¶cols. 93-94
Latin original

3. Super hoc quotidie justus decantat: Impulsus sum, ut caderem, sed non cecidi, quia Dominus suscepit me (Psal. CXVII, 13). Sed, cum ceciderit non collidetur, quia Dominus supponit manum suam (Psal. XXXVI, 24): id est, cum peccaverit, non condemnabitur, quia advocatum habet Jesum, eo quod etsi septies in die cadit, septies resurgit. Non delectatur jacere ubi cadit, et in luto volutari; sed ocius exsurgens excutit se de pulvere, et sordes diluit satisfactione. Per hoc namque justus fit, quod semper in initio sermonis accusator est sui (Prov. XVIII, 17), memor consilii divini, quo dictum est ei: Dic iniquitates tuas, ut justificeris (Isai. XLIII, sec. LXX). Ita fit ut, dum seipsum judicat, et Deum de se vindicat, illum quem judicem metuebat justum, reus advocatum habeat. Justus enim Dominus, et justitias dilexit (Psal. X, 8); nec potest injustas causas suscipere aut tueri, qui, cum acceperit tempus, etiam justitias judicabit. Et tamen qui judicem se minatur futurum superbe de justitia praesumentium, advocatum se pollicetur humiliter peccata confitentium; quoniam apud illum, in cujus conspectu non justificabitur omnis homo, nullatenus causam testificari nostram melius possumus, quam si nosmetipsos accusantes et punientes, justitiam ipsius in nos transferamus, et partes judicis adversus reos exsequamur.

English translation

3. Concerning this the just man daily sings: 'I was thrust, that I might fall, but I did not fall, because the Lord upheld me' (Psal. CXVII, 13). 'But when he shall fall, he shall not be bruised, because the Lord puts his hand under him' (Psal. XXXVI, 24): that is, when he has sinned, he will not be condemned, because he has Jesus as his advocate, for although he falls seven times a day, he rises seven times. He does not delight to lie where he falls, and to wallow in the mire; but rising up quickly, he shakes the dust from himself, and washes away his filth by satisfaction. For by this a man becomes just, because at the very outset of his speech he is always his own accuser (Prov. XVIII, 17), remembering the divine counsel by which it was said to him: 'Declare thy iniquities, that thou mayest be justified' (Isai. XLIII, sec. LXX). Thus it comes about that, while he judges himself and vindicates God's justice against himself, the guilty man has as his advocate him whom, as judge, he feared to be just. For 'the Lord is just, and he has loved justice' (Psal. X, 8); nor can he who, when he shall have received the appointed time, will judge even deeds of justice, take up or defend unjust causes. And yet he who threatens that he will be a judge to those who presume proudly upon their own justice, promises that he will be an advocate to those who humbly confess their sins; since before him, in whose sight no man shall be justified, we can in no way better bear witness to our cause than if, accusing and punishing ourselves, we transfer his justice onto ourselves, and carry out the part of a judge against the guilty.

Latin original

4. Sed etsi in aliquo veniali vel per ignorantiam fallimur, vel per infirmitatem vincimur, ut Jebusaeum nostrum nequaquam exterminare praevaleamus, sed semper sub oculis nostris in terra nobiscum habitare patiamur; in hac quoque imperfectione nostra consolatur nos sermo propheticus, qui nos in se transfigurans ait: In ipsis fuimus semper et salvabimur (Isai. LXIV, 5). Imperfectum meum, inquit corpus Christi, viderunt misericordes oculi tui, et in libro tuo omnes scribentur (Psal. CXXXVIII, 16); id est, scripti invenientur tam perfecti quam imperfecti. Pollicetur siquidem Dominus se perfecturum imperfectionem nostram, loquens per Joelem prophetam: Et mundabo, inquit, sanguinem eorum quem non mundaverunt (Joel III, 21). Hinc Isaias, cum praemisisset de perfectione futurae coelestis Jerusalem: Et erit, omnis qui relictus fuerit in Sion, et residuus in Jerusalem, sanctus vocabitur omnis qui scriptus est in vita in Jerusalem: quod in infirmis nonnisi purgatione divina perficienda sit sanctitas illa, subjunxit, dicens: Si abluerit Dominus sordem filiarum Sion, et sanguinem laverit de medio Jerusalem. Consolatur quidem me lavatio quae promittitur; sed terret modus qui subditur: Spiritu, inquit, judicii, et spiritu ardoris (Isai. III, 41). Ignis enim opus cujusque probabit. Si cujus opus arserit, detrimentum patietur: ipse autem salvus fiet merito fundamenti; sic tamen quasi per ignem (I Cor. III, 13, 15).

English translation

4. But even if in some venial matter we are deceived through ignorance, or overcome through weakness, so that we are by no means able to exterminate our Jebusite, but always allow him to dwell with us in the land before our eyes; in this imperfection of ours too the prophetic word consoles us, which, transfiguring us into itself, says: "In these we have always been, and we shall be saved" (Isai. LXIV, 5). "My imperfection," says the body of Christ, "your merciful eyes have seen, and in your book all shall be written" (Psal. CXXXVIII, 16); that is, both the perfect and the imperfect will be found written there. For the Lord promises that he will perfect our imperfection, speaking through the prophet Joel: "And I will cleanse," he says, "their blood which they have not cleansed" (Joel III, 21). Hence Isaiah, having first said concerning the perfection of the future heavenly Jerusalem: "And it shall come to pass, that everyone who is left in Sion, and remains in Jerusalem, shall be called holy, everyone who is written among the living in Jerusalem" — because in the weak that holiness cannot be perfected except by divine purification, he added, saying: "If the Lord shall wash away the filth of the daughters of Sion, and shall wash the blood from the midst of Jerusalem." The washing that is promised indeed consoles me; but the manner that is added terrifies me: "By the spirit," he says, "of judgment, and the spirit of burning" (Isai. III, 41). For fire will test the work of each one. If anyone's work shall burn, he shall suffer loss; but he himself shall be saved on account of the foundation, yet so as by fire (I Cor. III, 13, 15).

Latin original

5. Quanto melius nunc arderemus delectabili amore, quam tunc illo poenali ardore? quanto suavius nunc igne amoris purgaremur, ut mundanum in nobis non relinqueretur quod mundi incendio obnoxium inveniretur, sed, sicut tribus pueris ventus roris fieret nobis fornax Babylonis? (Dan. III, 49, 50.) Alioquin, si tepide et negligenter viventes, ligna, fenum, stipulam nobis coacervamus, et tantam ignium materiam hinc nobiscum portamus; cum examen illius purgatorii tales intraverimus, quis poterit de nobis habitare cum igne devorante? aut quis habitabit ex nobis cum ardoribus sempiternis, quando omne etiam vestimentum mistum sanguine erit in combustionem et cibus ignis? (Isai. IX, 5.) Scio quidem quia, si satis arderem igne charitatis, de me quoque diceretur: Dimittuntur ei peccata multa, quoniam dilexit multum (Luc. VII, 47). Nunc autem, si purgari non mereor igne dilectionis, utinam vel purgarer alicujus igne passionis, fieretque mihi quod propheta imprecatur sibi: Ingrediatur putredo in ossibus meis, et subter me scateat, ut requiescam in die tribulationis (Habac. III, 16). Utinam qui coepit, ipse me conterat; solvat manum suam, et succidat me: et haec mihi sit consolatio, ut affligens me dolore non parcat. Verumtamen nosti, Domine, infirmitatem figmenti mei. Quae est enim fortitudo mea, ut sustineam? aut quis finis meus, ut patienter agam? Nec fortitudo lapidum fortitudo mea, nec caro mea aenea est (Job VI, 9-12). Hoc primum quidem peto, Domine, ne in furore tuo arguas me, neque in ira tua corripias me (Psal. VI, 2). Sed, quoniam audio abs te: Ego quos amo, arguo et castigo (Apoc. III, 19); et: Beatus homo qui corripitur a Domino (Job V, 17): corripe me, Domine, in misericordia, et non in furore tuo, ne forte ad nihilum redigas me (Jerem. X, 24), et dicatur de me: Detraxisti eum ab emundatione (Psal. LXXXVIII, 45). Intuere infirmitatem meam, et secundum illam manum tuam moderare, ne vehementia verberis frangat patientiam cordis; sed magis tribulatio patientiam operetur, patientia probationem, probatio spem. Sed vae mihi, si quotidie uror, et non emundor, ut et de me dici contingat: Multo labore sudatum est, et non exivit de anima rubigo ejus, neque per ignem. Immunditia tua exsecrabilis, quia mundare te volui, et non es mundata a sordibus tuis; sed nec mundaberis prius, donec quiescere faciam indignationem meam in te (Ezech. XXIV, 12, 13).

English translation

5. How much better would it be for us to burn now with delightful love, than then with that punishing fire? How much more sweetly would we be purged now by the fire of love, so that nothing worldly would be left in us found liable to the world's burning, but, as for the three youths, the furnace of Babylon would become for us a wind of dew? (Dan. III, 49, 50.) Otherwise, if living tepidly and negligently we heap up for ourselves wood, hay, and straw, and carry with us from here so much material for fires; when we enter that purgatorial trial being such as this, who among us will be able to dwell with the devouring fire? Or who of us will dwell with the everlasting burnings, when every garment too, mingled with blood, shall be for burning and food for the fire? (Isai. IX, 5.) I know indeed that, if I burned sufficiently with the fire of charity, it would also be said of me: "Many sins are forgiven her, because she loved much" (Luc. VII, 47). But now, if I do not merit to be purged by the fire of love, would that I might at least be purged by the fire of some suffering, and that there might happen to me what the prophet calls down upon himself: "Let rottenness enter into my bones, and swarm beneath me, that I may rest in the day of tribulation" (Habac. III, 16). Would that he who has begun would himself crush me; would that he would loosen his hand and cut me off; and let this be my consolation, that in afflicting me with sorrow he would not spare me. Yet you know, Lord, the weakness of my frame. For what is my strength, that I should endure? Or what is my end, that I should act patiently? Neither is my strength the strength of stones, nor is my flesh of bronze (Job VI, 9-12). This first indeed I ask, Lord: rebuke me not in your fury, neither chastise me in your wrath (Psal. VI, 2). But since I hear from you: "Those whom I love, I rebuke and chastise" (Apoc. III, 19); and: "Blessed is the man whom the Lord corrects" (Job V, 17): correct me, Lord, in mercy, and not in your fury, lest perhaps you reduce me to nothing (Jerem. X, 24), and it be said of me: "You have cut him off from cleansing" (Psal. LXXXVIII, 45). Look upon my weakness, and moderate your hand according to it, lest the violence of the blow break the patience of my heart; but rather let tribulation work patience, patience proof, and proof hope. But woe to me, if I am burned daily and am not cleansed, so that it should happen to be said of me too: "Much labor has been expended, and its rust has not gone out of the soul, not even by fire. Your filthiness is execrable, because I wished to cleanse you, and you have not been cleansed from your filth; nor shall you be cleansed, until I have caused my indignation to rest upon you" (Ezech. XXIV, 12, 13).

Latin original

6. Quam vereor ne id cadat in nos, qui districtione vitae, velut quodam purgatorio igne, quotidie coquimur, et nunquam ab iniquitate nostra, sicut oportet, mundamur! et forsitan non mundabimur, donec sedeat Judex ipse quasi ignis conflans et emundans argentum, et purgabit filios Levi (Malach. III, 2, 3). Verumtamen, quantulumcunque proficiat nunc iste ignis, malo tamen interim mihi hunc adhiberi, quam ad illum penitus differri. Nam, si ad invicem comparentur, iste quidem medicina est misericordis, ille ultio irati. Ita sentiebat, qui dicebat: Domine, ne in furore tuo arguas me, neque in ira tua corripias me (Psal. VI, 2). Scio quidem quia tunc etiam, cum conturbata fuerit anima mea, Domine, in ira misericordiae memor eris. Scio quia etiam in luctu et poenis consolarer me spe meliori: et inimicae potestati, cui emendandus ad tempus traditus essem, verbis prophetae responderem, dicens: Ne laeteris, inimica mea, super me, quia cecidi: consurgam, cum sedero in tenebris; Dominus lux mea est. Iram Domini portabo, quia peccavi ei, donec judicet causam meam. Educet me in lucem, videbo justitiam ejus: et aspiciet me inimica mea, et operietur confusione (Mich. VII, 8-10). Et dicam in illa die: Confitebor tibi, Domine, quoniam iratus es mihi: conversus est furor tuus, et consolatus es me (Isai. XII, 1). Verumtamen cur timebo in die mala? Cur non magis in die bona bonis fruor, et diem malam praecaveo? Nonne ecce nunc tempus acceptabile, ecce nunc dies salutis? Cur non audiam modo consilium Sapientis: Quodcunque potest manus tua instanter operare: quia non est consilium, nec ratio, nec sapientia apud inferos quo tu properas (Eccle. IX, 10); et illud Apostoli: Dum tempus habemus, operemur bonum ad omnes? (Galat. VI, 10.) Coeperamus quidem a consolationibus, sed ad terrores devenimus; quia tunc nimirum consolatio utilis erit, si terrori mista fuerit; tunc ingrediemur in veritate tua (Hebr. IV, 3), Domine, si ita laetatur cor nostrum, ut timeat nomen tuum. Alioquin, si omnino vos securos faceremus, diceretur et de nobis: Popule meus, qui beatum te dicunt, ipsi te decipiunt, et vestigia gressuum tuorum dissipant (Isai. III, 12). Tunc autem vere beati erimus, si miseriam nostram recte agnoscamus et pie doleamus: tunc securi in morte erimus, si semper pavidi suspectique vivamus.

English translation

6. How I fear that this may fall upon us, who are daily cooked, as it were by a kind of purgatorial fire, through the strictness of our life, and yet are never cleansed from our iniquity as we ought to be! And perhaps we shall not be cleansed until the Judge himself sits like a refiner's fire, purifying silver, and shall purge the sons of Levi (Malach. III, 2, 3). Nevertheless, however little this fire now profits, I would rather in the meantime have this fire applied to me than be put off entirely until that other one. For if the two be compared with one another, this one is the medicine of a merciful God, that one the vengeance of an angry God. So thought he who said: Lord, rebuke me not in thy fury, nor chastise me in thy wrath (Psal. VI, 2). Indeed I know that even then, when my soul shall be troubled, Lord, in wrath thou wilt remember mercy. I know that even in mourning and punishments I would console myself with better hope, and to the hostile power, to which I would be handed over for a time to be corrected, I would answer in the words of the prophet, saying: Rejoice not, my enemy, over me, because I have fallen; I shall rise again when I sit in darkness; the Lord is my light. I will bear the wrath of the Lord, because I have sinned against him, until he judge my cause. He will bring me forth into the light, I shall see his justice; and my enemy shall look upon me, and shall be covered with confusion (Mich. VII, 8-10). And I will say in that day: I will give thanks to thee, O Lord, for thou wast angry with me: thy wrath is turned away, and thou hast comforted me (Isai. XII, 1). But why should I fear in the evil day? Why should I not rather enjoy good things in the good day, and take precaution against the evil day? Is it not now the acceptable time, is it not now the day of salvation? Why should I not now heed the counsel of the Wise Man: Whatsoever thy hand is able to do, do it earnestly: for neither counsel, nor reason, nor wisdom shall be in hell, whither thou art hastening (Eccle. IX, 10); and that saying of the Apostle: While we have time, let us do good to all men (Galat. VI, 10.). We began indeed with consolations, but we have come round to terrors; because surely consolation will be profitable only if it be mingled with terror; then shall we enter into thy truth (Hebr. IV, 3), O Lord, if our heart so rejoices that it fears thy name. Otherwise, if we were to make you altogether secure, it would be said of us too: O my people, they that call thee blessed, the same deceive thee, and destroy the way of thy steps (Isai. III, 12). But then shall we truly be blessed, if we rightly acknowledge our wretchedness and devoutly grieve over it; then shall we be secure in death, if we live always fearful and watchful.
