Editorial introduction

The father in the parable of the prodigal son receives the penitent before his confession is complete. Guerric considers the abundance of mercy, union with God and the humility that sustains the virtues. The 1879 edition places the sermon on a Saturday in Lent without specifying the week. The collection is associated approximately with Guerric's abbacy in 1138–1157; the individual sermon is undated.

English translation

ON SATURDAY OF THE WEEK OF LENT. On the parable of the prodigal son. (Luc. XV, 11-32.)

¶cols. 95-96; cols. 97-98
Latin original

1. O Felix humilitas poenitentium! o beata spes confitentium! quam potens es apud omnipotentem, quam facile vincis invincibilem, quam cito tremendum judicem convertis in piissimum patrem! Prodigus iste filius, cujus hodie in magnam aedificationem nostram audivimus peregrinationem aerumnosam, poenitentiam lacrymosam, susceptionem gloriosam: prodigus, inquam, iste tam graviter reus, nondum confessus erat, sed tantum confiteri deliberaverat; nondum satisfecerat, sed tantum ad satisfaciendum animum inclinaverat: et de solo fere proposito conceptae humilitatis veniam statim obtinuit, quae tanto tempore tantis expetitur votis, imploratur lacrymis, ambitur obsequiis. Latronem in cruce sola confessio absolvit; istum, sola voluntas confitendi. Dixi, inquit, Confitebor adversum me injustitiam meam Domino: et tu remisisti impietatem peccati mei (Psal. XXXI, 5): ubique misericordia praevenit. Praevenerat voluntatem confessionis, ipsam inspirando: praevenit et vocem confessionis, quod confitendum erat indulgendo. Cum adhuc, inquit, longe esset, vidit illum pater ipsius, et misericordia motus est; et accurrens cecidit super collum ejus, et osculatus est eum. Quantum haec verba sonare videntur, tardius videbatur patri filio veniam dedisse, quam illi accepisse. Sic festinabat absolvere reum a tormento conscientiae suae, quasi plus cruciaret misericordem compassio miseri, quam ipsum miserum passio sui. Neque hoc dicimus quo in natura incommutabili humanos ponamus affectus, sed ut in amorem illius summae bonitatis affectus noster dulcescat; dum ipsam plus nos amare, quam ipsi nos amemus, ex similitudine discimus humana.

English translation

1. O happy humility of penitents! O blessed hope of those who confess! How powerful you are before the Almighty, how easily you conquer the unconquerable, how swiftly you turn the dreadful Judge into the most loving Father! That prodigal son, whose grievous wandering, tearful repentance, and glorious welcome we heard today to our great edification: that prodigal, I say, so gravely guilty, had not yet confessed, but had only resolved to confess; he had not yet made satisfaction, but had only inclined his mind toward making satisfaction—and from the mere purpose of a humility now conceived, he at once obtained the pardon which is sought for so long with such great prayers, implored with tears, and courted with services. The thief on the cross was absolved by confession alone; this man, by the mere will to confess. "I said," he says, "I will confess against myself my injustice to the Lord: and thou hast forgiven the wickedness of my sin" (Psal. XXXI, 5): everywhere mercy goes before. It had gone before the will to confess, by inspiring it itself; it went before the voice of confession too, by pardoning what was to be confessed. "When he was yet a great way off," it says, "his father saw him, and was moved with compassion; and running to him, fell on his neck, and kissed him." As far as these words seem to suggest, it seemed the father was slower to give pardon to the son than the son was to receive it! So he hastened to absolve the guilty one from the torment of his conscience, as though the compassion of the merciful one tormented him more than the suffering of his own wretchedness tormented the wretched one himself. We do not say this so as to place human feelings within the unchangeable nature of God, but so that our affection may grow sweet toward the love of that highest goodness, while from this human likeness we learn that he loves us more than we love ourselves.

2. Vide autem quomodo ubi abundavit delictum, superabundet gratia. Reus vix poterat sperare veniam; Judex, imo non jam judex, sed advocatus, accumulat gratiam. Cito, inquit, proferte stolam primam, et induite illum, et date annulum in manu ejus, et calceamenta in pedes; et adducite vitulum saginatum, et occidite, et manducemus et epulemur: quia hic filius meus mortuus erat, et revixit. Verum ut haec omnia praetereamus, scilicet stolam primam, id est sanctificationem spiritus, qua baptizatus induitur, et poenitens reinduitur; annulum fidei, quo subarrhatur; calceamenta, quibus ad calcanda serpentum venena munitur, vel ad evangelizandum praeparatur; vitulum saginatum, qui ei in altari immolatur; festiva illa gaudia, quae, pro recepto indicta filio, toto celebrantur coelo: ut haec, inquam, omnia taceamus, et doctioribus tractanda servemus; amplexus ille et osculum paternae pietatis, quid non habet gratiae et dulcedinis? quid non felicissimi gaudii? quid non sanctissimae voluptatis? Cecidit inquit, super collum ejus, et osculatus est eum. Cum sic super eum affectabat, quid agebat amplectens et osculans, nisi illum sibi vel se ipsum illi insinuabat, se illi inspirabat, ut, adhaerendo sibi unus, secum fieret spiritus, sicut adhaerendo meretricibus unum cum eis corpus fuerat affectus? Parum erat illi summae misericordiae viscera suae miserationis non claudere miseris; in ipsa sua eos viscera trahit, suisque inserit membris. Non poterat nos sibi arctius astringere, non poterat non magis intimos habere, quam ut incorporaret sibi, et, sicut charitate, sic virtute ineffabili, non solum assumpto corpori, sed etiam ipsi conniteretur1 spiritui. Quod si tanta est gratia poenitentium, quae erit gloria regnantium? si ista sunt solatia miserorum, quae erunt gaudia beatorum? et qui hoc praerogat in via, quid servat in patria? Utique quod in cor hominis non ascendit; ut ei similes simus, et sit Deus omnia in omnibus.

English translation

2. But see how, where sin abounded, grace abounds the more. The guilty one could scarcely hope for pardon; the Judge—no, no longer a judge, but an advocate—heaps up grace. "Quickly," he says, "bring forth the first robe, and clothe him, and put a ring on his hand, and shoes on his feet; and bring the fatted calf, and kill it, and let us eat and feast: for this my son was dead, and is come to life again." But to pass over all these things—namely, the first robe, that is, the sanctification of the spirit with which the baptized is clothed and the penitent reclothed; the ring of faith, by which he is betrothed; the shoes, with which he is armed to tread upon the venom of serpents, or prepared to preach the Gospel; the fatted calf, which is sacrificed for him upon the altar; those festive joys which, proclaimed for the recovered son, are celebrated throughout all heaven—to pass over all these, I say, and leave them for more learned men to treat: that embrace and kiss of fatherly love—what grace and sweetness does it not contain? What most blessed joy? What most holy delight does it not hold? "He fell," it says, "on his neck, and kissed him." When he showed such feeling toward him, what was he doing in embracing and kissing him, but insinuating him to himself, or himself to him, breathing himself into him, so that, by clinging to him, the two might become one spirit together, just as, by clinging to harlots, he had been made one body with them? It was too little for that highest mercy not to close the inward depths of its compassion to the wretched; it draws them into its very depths, and grafts them into its own members. It could not bind us more closely to itself or hold us in greater intimacy than by incorporating us into itself and, by ineffable power as by charity, uniting us1 not only to the body it had assumed but also to its very spirit. But if such is the grace of penitents, what will be the glory of those who reign? If these are the comforts of the wretched, what will be the joys of the blessed? And he who gives this in advance on the way, what does he keep in store in the homeland? Surely that which has not entered into the heart of man: that we may be like him, and God may be all in all.

3. Jam vero tu, beate peccator (sed licet non ideo beatus quia peccator, sed quia poenitens de peccato), quid tibi, rogo, animi erat inter amplexus patris et oscula, cum prope desperatum refovebat, cum cor mundum tibi innovans, laetitiam salutaris sui tibi refundebat? Et quomodo, inquit, sermo explicabit quod mens non capit? Inenarrabiles gemitus, et inexplicabiles sunt affectus, quos de incomprehensibili velut impraegnatus parturit animus. Angustum est eis cor humanum: unde et scissum effundit se ipsum; et ardorem quem concipit, sed non capit, quibus potest modis, lacrymis, gemitibus, suspiriis, evaporat ac digerit. Norunt haec melius, qui gustaverunt saepius et uberius. Nunc quoque, inquam, cum dimissus post illos amplexus et oscula, te et te ipsum recogitas, cum causam tuam qualis fuerit, qualiterve ab eo judicata sit retractas, et illinc abundantiam delicti tui, hinc superabundantiam gratiae pensas: quid tibi, obsecro, parit cogitatio tua? Quidni, inquit, intolerabilis in meditatione mea exardescat ignis, illinc dolore et pudore, hinc gaudio et amore? Non me hominem putarem, sed lapidem, si ita duri cordis existerem, ut me ipsum non dolerem vel erubescerem, vel ita nequam et ingrati, ut non totus in gaudium vel amorem illius Patris liquefierem.

English translation

3. Now you, blessed sinner (though not blessed because a sinner, but because repentant of sin), what, I ask, was in your heart amid the embraces and kisses of the Father, when he revived you who were nearly in despair, when, renewing a clean heart in you, he poured back into you the joy of his salvation? And how, he says, shall speech unfold what the mind cannot grasp? Unspeakable are the groans, and inexplicable the feelings, which the soul brings forth in labor, as if made pregnant by something incomprehensible. The human heart is too narrow for them; hence, being torn, it pours itself forth, and the fervor it conceives but cannot contain, it releases and works off by whatever means it can—tears, groans, sighs. Those who have tasted this more often and more abundantly know it better. Now too, I say, when, released after those embraces and kisses, you reconsider yourself and yourself again, when you turn over in your mind what your case was, and how it was judged by him, and weigh on the one hand the abundance of your sin, on the other the overabundance of grace: what, I ask, does your reflection bring forth in you? Why, he says, would not an intolerable fire blaze up in my meditation, on the one side from grief and shame, on the other from joy and love? I would not think myself a man but a stone, if I were so hard of heart as not to grieve or blush for myself, or so wicked and ungrateful as not to melt entirely into joy and love of that Father.

4. Custodi igitur, o felix peccator, custodi sollicite ac vigilanter hunc spiritum tuum, hunc justissimum humilitatis ac pietatis affectum; quo semper ita de te sentias in humilitate, de Domino in bonitate. Nihil illo majus in donis Spiritus sancti, nihil pretiosius in thesauris Dei, nihil sanctius inter omnia charismata, nihil salubrius inter omnia sacramenta. Custodi, inquam, si vis ipse custodiri, humilitatem illius sensus et vocis, qua Patri confiteris, et dicis: Pater, jam non sum dignus vocari filius tuus; fac me sicut unum de mercenariis tuis. Nihil ita Patrem promeretur, sicut hujus vocis affectus; nec melius te filium facies dignum, quam si semper confitearis indignum. Haec humilitas non solum peccatores justificat, sed etiam justos consummat, justitiamque eis accumulat, si videlicet se servos fateantur inutiles, etiam cum quae praecepta sunt, fecerint omnia. Peccatum tuum coram te sit semper, et, juxta consilium Sapientis, etiam de propitiato peccato noli esse absque metu (Eccli. V, 5). Incerta et occulta sunt judicia Dei; non temere praesumendum est de illis, cum nil inde certius teneamus, quam quod in conspectu Dei non justificabitur omnis vivens, nisi in quantum se judicat peccatorem. Alioquin quasi pannus menstruatae universae justitiae nostrae (Isai. XLIV, 62). Blande te misericordia suscepit, pie refovit; time judicium, ne gratia, quae data est humili, auferatur superbienti. Elegisti abjectus esse in domo patris tui; contentus eras fieri quasi unus ex mercenariis; sta in sententia, ut, etiamsi promotus es, adhuc ad majora proveharis. Extremum locum semper occupa, aut certe desidera; servitutem tibi mercenarii, non libertatem filii tibi vindica. Devotione quidem filii Patrem cole, conscius quid ipse de te meruerit; sed humilitate et labore mercenarii contentus esto, conscius quid ipse merueris. Nunquam tibi displiceat humilitas, per quam placere coepisti, et sine qua displicere incipies, quantiscunque pollere virtutibus, quantumcunque patri officiosus deservire videaris. Humilitas siquidem omnium virtutum est maxima, cum tamen virtutem se esse nesciat. Ipsa fere omnium radix, et seminarium, et fomes, et incentivum, et ipsa cumulus et fastigium, custodia et disciplina. Ab ipsa incipiunt, per ipsam proficiunt, in ipsa consummantur, per ipsam conservantur. Cumque omnibus ut virtutes sint tribuat, si qua earum defuerit, vel minus perfecta fuerit, ipsa alterius defectu proficiens, dispendium illius de se compensat.

English translation

4. Keep, then, O happy sinner, keep carefully and watchfully this spirit of yours, this most just affection of humility and devotion; by which you should always feel thus about yourself—in humility—and about the Lord—in goodness. Nothing is greater than this among the gifts of the Holy Spirit, nothing more precious among the treasures of God, nothing holier among all the charisms, nothing more wholesome among all the sacraments. Keep, I say, if you wish yourself to be kept, the humility of that feeling and of that voice by which you confess to the Father and say: "Father, I am no longer worthy to be called your son; make me as one of your hired servants." Nothing so wins the Father's favor as the affection of this voice; nor will you make yourself more worthy to be a son than if you always confess yourself unworthy. This humility not only justifies sinners, but also perfects the just, and increases righteousness for them, namely if they confess themselves unprofitable servants even when they have done all that was commanded. Let your sin always be before you, and, according to the counsel of the Wise Man, even concerning sin that has been forgiven, do not be without fear (Eccli. V, 5). The judgments of God are uncertain and hidden; one must not presume rashly about them, since we hold nothing more certain from this than that in the sight of God no living person will be justified, except insofar as he judges himself a sinner. Otherwise, all our righteousness is like a menstrual cloth (Isai. XLIV, 62). Mercy has received you gently, has tenderly nursed you back; fear judgment, lest the grace that was given to the humble be taken away from the one who grows proud. You chose to be an outcast in your father's house; you were content to become as one of the hired servants; stand firm in that resolve, so that even if you are promoted, you may still be advanced to greater things. Always occupy the lowest place, or at least desire it; claim for yourself the servitude of a hired servant, not the freedom of a son. Indeed, worship the Father with the devotion of a son, being conscious of what he himself has deserved from you; but be content with the humility and labor of a hired servant, conscious of what you yourself have merited. Never let humility displease you, through which you began to be pleasing, and without which you will begin to be displeasing, however great the virtues you may possess, however dutifully you may seem to serve the Father. For humility is indeed the greatest of all virtues, even though it does not know itself to be a virtue. It is almost the root, and seedbed, and kindling, and spark of all virtues, and it is itself their crown and summit, their guard and discipline. From it they begin, through it they advance, in it they are perfected, through it they are preserved. And although it grants to all of them that they be virtues, if any of them should be lacking, or less than perfect, humility itself, profiting by the defect of another, compensates by itself for that one's loss.
