Editorial introduction

This fourth Nativity sermon belongs to Guerric of Igny's medieval collection of sermons for the liturgical year, here read from the 1879 edition of Patrologia Latina. Its individual date is unknown; the collection is associated approximately with his abbacy in 1138–1157. Beginning from Paul's fullness of time, Guerric reads it as an abundance of grace, the fulfillment of prophecy and the mature age of faith. Salvation is bought at a trifling price, yet the present age meets it with complaint and ingratitude. He closes with thanks for the Word made hay in the manger for beasts as well as men. An editorial note explains an inaccurate printed locator. The heading names the Nativity without a particular Christmas Mass.

English translation

On the Fullness of Time Brought by Christ

¶cols. 37-38; cols. 39-40
Latin original

1. Ecce jam plenitudo temporis (Galat. IV, 4). Plenitudo ista temporis apud Paulum accipitur, seu propter abundantiam gratiae, seu propter adimpletionem praecedentis prophetiae, seu propter pleniorem aetatem fidei adultae. De singulis videamus. Omnium plenitudo bonorum Christus Dominus est: quippe qui omnibus thesauris sapientiae et scientiae Dei, totiusque gratiae plenus est; imo in quo omnis plenitudo divinitatis habitat corporaliter (Coloss. II, 9). Viderunt apostoli plenum gratiae et veritatis, et de plenitudine ejus omnes acceperunt: adeo ut novissimus eorum et abortivus Paulus, vas electionis, de plenitudine quam acceperat, disseminaret ubique plenitudinem gratiae et veritatis, clamans et dicens: Ecce jam venit plenitudo temporis (Galat. IV, 4). Cum enim plenus omnibus bonis natus esset temporaliter auctor temporis, quidni plenitudinem attulisset temporis? Cum coeli rorarent desuper, et nubes pluerent Justum, Salvatorem terra germinaret, tanta supernae benedictionis ubertas quidni fecunditatem terris omnibus praestaret? Benedixisti, Domine, terram tuam, dedisti benignitatem, et terra nostra dedit fructum suum (Psal. LXXXIV, 2, 13); videlicet de uno grano frumenti, quod germinavit alvus Virginis, ubique terrarum pullulante copiosa messe fidelium. Nolo enim hanc plenitudinem temporis requiras in copia bonorum temporalium, sed aeternorum; non in proventu agrorum, sed coelorum. Si coeli rorant desuper, et nubes pluunt Justum; si terra germinat Salvatorem, et justitia oritur simul; si denique oritur in diebus Domini non solum justitia, sed et abundantia pacis: feliciora tempora ne quaesieris, quando et regnum Dei nihil aliud est quam justitia, et pax, et gaudium in Spiritu sancto (Rom. XIV, 17), quod de illis procedit. Nam et nostrorum temporum ille status judicatur optimus, atque pulcherrimus, cum et justitia moribus imponit disciplinam, et abundantia cum pace quietem vitae praestat et laetitiam. Denique misericordia Domini plena est terra (Psal. CXVIII, 64): benedixit Dominus coronae anni benignitatis suae, et campi ejus repleti sunt ubertate (Psal. LXIV, 12) totius gratiae spiritualis. Et quis, nisi ingratus, plenitudinem neget esse temporis? Quae unquam aurea aetas simile aliquid habuit hujus plenitudini temporis, quando panis angelorum, omnem habens saporem suavitatis et omne delectamentum, apponitur etiam jumentis; et non solum homines, sed et jumenta, cibo pascuntur coelesti? Homines enim et jumenta salvabis, Domine, quemadmodum multiplicasti misericordiam tuam, Deus (Psal. XXXV, 7, 8). Infinite multiplex haec tua, Deus, misericordia, ut qui panis angelorum, non solum hominum dites ac beatifices mensas, sed etiam fenum factus, jumentorum repleas praesepia. Confiteris, o Domine sapientia misericors, quia sapientibus et insipientibus debitor es, ut qui utrosque fecisti, utrisque necessariam alimoniam procures; et tam homines quam jumenta, tam spirituales quam animales, suo quemque gradu et ordine salves. Confiteantur igitur Domino misericordiae ejus, et mirabilia ejus filiis hominum (Psal. CVI, 15): quia misit Verbum suum carnem factum, medicinam et escam omnium; ut etiam incapaces Verbi, sanentur tamen et satientur carne Verbi. Edant itaque pauperes et saturentur, et confiteantur venisse beatam temporis plenitudinem; quando et in praesepibus suis paratum sine sudore vultus sui panem inveniunt coelestem. Numquid ergo mugiet bos, cum ante praesepe sic plenum steterit? (Job VI, 5.) Mugiat, sed prae gaudio spiritus in jubilo cordis; quia praesepe Domini sui, imo Dominum in praesepi suo cognovit.

English translation

1. Behold, now is the fullness of time (Galat. IV, 4). This "fullness of time" is taken by Paul in reference either to the abundance of grace, or to the fulfillment of earlier prophecy, or to the fuller age of mature faith. Let us look at each in turn. Christ the Lord is the fullness of all good things: for he is full of all the treasures of the wisdom and knowledge of God, and of all grace; indeed, in him dwells all the fullness of the Godhead bodily (Coloss. II, 9). The apostles saw him full of grace and truth, and from his fullness all received: so much so that the last of them and the one born as it were out of due time, Paul, the chosen vessel, from the fullness he had received, scattered everywhere the fullness of grace and truth, crying out and saying: Behold, now the fullness of time has come (Galat. IV, 4). For since the author of time, full of all good things, was born in time, why should he not have brought the fullness of time? When the heavens dropped dew from above, and the clouds rained down the Just One, and the earth brought forth the Savior as its growth, why should such abundance of heavenly blessing not bring fruitfulness to all the earth? You have blessed, O Lord, your land; you have given your bounty, and our land has yielded its fruit (Psal. LXXXIV, 2, 13); namely, from the one grain of wheat which sprouted from the Virgin's womb, a plentiful harvest of the faithful has sprung up throughout all the earth. For I do not want you to seek this fullness of time in an abundance of temporal goods, but of eternal ones; not in the yield of fields, but of heavens. If the heavens drop dew from above, and the clouds rain down the Just One; if the earth brings forth the Savior as its sprout, and righteousness arises at the same time; and if, finally, in the days of the Lord there arises not only righteousness but also abundance of peace: do not seek for happier times, since the kingdom of God is nothing other than righteousness, and peace, and joy in the Holy Spirit (Rom. XIV, 17), which proceeds from these things. For indeed that condition of our times is judged best and most beautiful, when righteousness imposes discipline on morals, and abundance together with peace provides quiet of life and gladness. In short, the earth is full of the mercy of the Lord (Psal. CXVIII, 64): the Lord has blessed the crown of the year with his bounty, and his fields are filled with the abundance (Psal. LXIV, 12) of all spiritual grace. And who, unless he is ungrateful, would deny that this is the fullness of time? What golden age ever possessed anything like this fullness of time, when the bread of angels, having every taste of sweetness and every delight, is set before even the beasts of burden; and not only men, but even beasts of burden, are fed with heavenly food? For you will save men and beasts, O Lord; O how you have multiplied your mercy, O God (Psal. XXXV, 7, 8). Infinitely manifold is this your mercy, O God, that you who are the bread of angels not only enrich and make blessed the tables of men, but, having become hay, fill the mangers of beasts of burden. You confess, O Lord, merciful Wisdom, that you are debtor both to the wise and to the foolish, since, having made both, you provide necessary nourishment for both; and you save men as well as beasts, the spiritual as well as the animal, each in its own degree and order. Let his mercies therefore give praise to the Lord, and his wonders to the children of men (Psal. CVI, 15): for he sent his Word made flesh, the medicine and food of all; so that even those incapable of the Word might nevertheless be healed and satisfied by the flesh of the Word. Let the poor therefore eat and be filled, and confess that the blessed fullness of time has come; since even in their mangers they find heavenly bread prepared without the sweat of their brow. Will the ox then bellow, when he has stood before a manger so full? (Job VI, 5.) Let him bellow—but let it be for the joy of spirit, in the jubilation of the heart; because he has recognized the manger of his Lord, indeed, the Lord himself in his manger.

2. Libet autem adhuc manifestius videre, Christus veniens e coelis quantam plenitudinem temporis advexerit, quam facili et exiguo pretio omne pretiosum comparari posse decreverit. Duobus minutis, seu calice aquae frigidae, aut certe sola bona voluntate, jam a diebus ejus emitur regnum coelorum, quanquam etiam nunc vix inveniat emptorem in tanta multitudine divitum. Proh pudor! etiam nos ipsi, qui jam emere coepimus, jam tabulas emptionis conscripsimus, jam pignus haereditatis accepimus; etiam nos, inquam, toties a pacto resilimus, ac pene complaciti poenitemus, querimur, murmuramus, quasi in negotio simus circumventi; quasi magnum sit illud prope nihil, quod exigitur a nobis. Bene de nobis prophetavit Scriptura: Malum est; malum est, dicit omnis emptor, et cum recesserit, gloriabitur (Prov. XX, 14). An non merito poterit gloriari, qui immensum illud et aeternum pondus gloriae, hoc levi et momentaneo tribulationis suae comparaverit, qui sic pro nihilo salvus factus fuerit? At nunc quotidianas hinc inde querelas et molesta murmura audias: Malum hoc, malum illud; grave hoc, importabile illud; quis tanta ac talia ferat? Ita nemo fere est qui digne pro merito sui rem aestimans dicat: Non sunt condignae passiones hujus temporis ad futuram gloriam quae revelabitur in nobis (Rom. VIII, 18), nisi tunc demum cum coeperit revelari. Tunc plane gloriabitur, qui modo detrahit, ac de pretio se gravari gemit; cum scilicet de foro, id est, de mundo hoc, ubi contractus celebratur hujus commercii, recesserit et in domum aeternitatis suae, secum ferens rem tantam tantillo emptam, redierit. Homo plane secundum cor Dei erat ille, homo simplex sine querela, qui ab hac arte, imo inertia et infidelitate emptorum alienus erat; Davidem dico, qui dicebat: Quoniam non cognovi litteraturam, Domine, memorabor justitiae tuae solius (Psal. LXX, 15, 16). Justitiae meae nullatenus memorabor, ut labores meos exaggerem, merita magnificem; potius memorabor justitiae tuae solius, qui gratis obligasti te mihi promissorem. In veritate tua exaudi me, in justitia tua, et non intres in judicium cum servo tuo (Psal. CXLII, 1, 2): qui, si voluero me justificare, os meum condemnabit me. Per hoc enim, inquit, introibo in potentias Domini (Psal. LXX, 16); quia scilicet justitias meas non statuam: per hoc potentem me faciet, et nunc in praelio, et tunc in regno; quia semper infirmitates meas confitebor. Cum enim infirmor, tunc potens sum (II Cor. XII, 10). Sicut ergo prudenter apud Deum causam agit, qui justitiae suae immemor totum misericordiae committit; sic prudenter emit, qui omnem fictionem, atque avaritiam negotiationis scire renuit; qui inventa illa pretiosa margarita, non solum omnia sua, sed et seipsum ultro ac desideranter inpendit. At nos tepidi, ficti, ingrati, indevoti, voluptatum amatores magis quam Dei, vix ab illa voce infidelis et nequissimi murmuris, Malum est, malum est (Prov. XX, 14), valemus compesci; etiam postquam gustavimus, et vidimus, sicut de muliere forti scriptum est, quoniam bona est negotiatio nostra.

English translation

2. But I wish to see still more clearly how great a fullness of time Christ brought when he came from the heavens, and with how easy and small a price he determined that everything precious could be purchased. With two mites, or a cup of cold water, or indeed with good will alone, the kingdom of heaven has been bought ever since his days — though even now it scarcely finds a buyer amid so great a multitude of the rich. For shame! Even we ourselves, who have already begun to buy, who have already drawn up the tablets of purchase, who have already received the pledge of inheritance — even we, I say, so often draw back from the bargain, and, having almost agreed, repent, complain, murmur, as though we had been cheated in the transaction; as though that were a great thing — that next-to-nothing which is required of us. Scripture prophesied well concerning us: "It is bad, it is bad," says every buyer, and when he has gone away, he will boast (Prov. XX, 14). And will he not rightly be able to boast, who has purchased that immense and eternal weight of glory with this light and momentary tribulation of his, who has thus been saved for next to nothing? But now you may hear on every side daily complaints and troublesome murmurs: "This is bad, that is bad; this is heavy, that is unbearable; who can bear such great and such things?" Thus there is scarcely anyone who, rightly valuing the matter according to its worth, says: "The sufferings of this time are not worthy to be compared with the future glory that shall be revealed in us" (Rom. VIII, 18) — except only then, when it has begun to be revealed. Then clearly he will boast, who now disparages it and groans that he is burdened by the price; that is, when he has departed from the marketplace — that is, from this world, where the contract of this trade is transacted — and has returned to the house of his eternity, carrying with him so great a thing bought for so small a price. That man was truly a man after God's own heart, a simple man without complaint, who was a stranger to this art — or rather, to this sluggishness and faithlessness of buyers; I mean David, who said: "Because I have not known letters, O Lord, I will be mindful of your justice alone" (Psal. LXX, 15, 16). "I will by no means be mindful of my own justice, so as to exaggerate my labors or magnify my merits; rather I will be mindful of your justice alone, who freely bound yourself to me as one who promises." "Hear me in your truth, in your justice, and do not enter into judgment with your servant" (Psal. CXLII, 1, 2): for if I wished to justify myself, my own mouth would condemn me. "For by this," he says, "I will enter into the powers of the Lord" (Psal. LXX, 16); that is, because I will not establish my own righteousness: by this he will make me powerful, both now in the battle and then in the kingdom; because I will always confess my weaknesses. "For when I am weak, then I am powerful" (II Cor. XII, 10). Just as, therefore, he pleads his case wisely before God who, forgetful of his own righteousness, commits everything to mercy, so he buys wisely who refuses to know any pretense or greed in the trading; who, having found that precious pearl, spends not only all that he has, but also himself, freely and eagerly. But we, lukewarm, false, ungrateful, undevout, lovers of pleasures more than of God, can scarcely restrain ourselves from that voice of faithless and most wicked murmuring, "It is bad, it is bad" (Prov. XX, 14) — even after we have tasted and seen, as it is written of the valiant woman, that our trading is good.

3. Adhuc ferebat me quidam implacabilis zelus adversus ingratitudinem et infidelitatem hujus temporis nostri; sed revocat me, de qua sermonem institui, sancta ac beata plenitudo temporis Christi, qua etiam hodie plenius pascendi estis. Conjunctim tamen currunt, pariterque feruntur haec duo tempora tam diversa, tamque adversa, tempus scilicet gratiae, et tempus malitiae; cum tamen nonnisi unum tempus inveniatur utriusque. Si enim modo non esset tempus gratiae, non diceret Apostolus: Ecce nunc tempus acceptabile, ecce nunc dies salutis (II Cor. VI, 2). Et iterum, si modo non esset tempus malitiae, non diceret idem apostolus: Redimentes tempus, quoniam dies mali sunt (Ephes. V, 16). Confligunt igitur in tempore uno gratia et ingratitudo, velut in stadio uno. Jam olim siquidem sapientia pugnat adversus malitiam, propter quod et nunc in hujus mundi descendit aream. Pugnat, inquam, nolens vinci a malo, sed volens vincere in bono malum. Abundabat iniquitas, et licet humana congelata esset, divina tamen non frigescebat charitas. Magnus ille ardor charitatis, et revera, sicut scriptum est, aquis multis inexstinguibilis (Cantic. VIII, 7); quod cum extremum jam judicium postularet multitudo peccatorum, Deus tamen Filium suum misit, non ut judicaret mundum, sed ut salvaretur per ipsum (Joan. III, 17). Cum igitur jam pene consummata mundi malitia finem indicaret temporis, Redemptoris adventus novam et inopinatam plenitudinem temporis rebus infudit humanis. Cum enim mundus consenuisset, atque prope interitum esset temporis aetate, subito ad adventum Conditoris sui, nova et insperata renovatus est virtutis juventute, fideique quodam juvenili calore. Fides siquidem, cujus primaevum tempus, velut quaedam pueritia, fuit in Patriarchis, qui exorti sunt primo mane Ecclesiae nascentis; adolescentia vero in Prophetis; ad plenitudinem demum juvenilis excrevit roboris in Apostolis, quando et calorem suae virtutis mundo spectabilem praebuit in tam praeclaris, atque fortissimis innumerabilium martyrum triumphis. Hanc adultam ac plenam aetatem fidei vocat Apostolus plenitudinem temporis, quando scilicet hi qui sub paedagogo legis erant, nihilque a servo differebant, dum erant parvuli, jam adulti libertatem filiorum receperunt per Unigenitum Patris (Galat. VI, 1-9)1. Qui nimirum, ut nulla plenitudo suo deesset tempori, plenus tam gratia quam veritate venit, quatenus et per gratiam impleri faceret legis mandata, et per veritatem impleret ipse promissa; adeo ut quidquid etiam universis retro saeculis mystice gestum aut dictum fuit, totum verius ac plenius impletum sit in hac plenitudine temporis. Nam et hic ipse dies tot sacramenta, tot oracula, quasi recapitulata in brevi hodie exhibuit, ut de eo vere dici possit: Consummatus in brevi, explevit tempora multa (Sap. IV, 13).

English translation

3. A certain relentless zeal against the ingratitude and unfaithfulness of our present time was still carrying me along; but the holy and blessed fullness of the time of Christ, on which I began this sermon, and with which you are to be fed more fully even today, calls me back. Yet these two times, so different and so opposed to each other—namely, the time of grace and the time of malice—run together and are carried along jointly, even though there is found to be but one single time for both. For if the present were not the time of grace, the Apostle would not say: 'Behold, now is the acceptable time; behold, now is the day of salvation' (II Cor. VI, 2). And again, if the present were not the time of malice, the same Apostle would not say: 'Redeeming the time, because the days are evil' (Ephes. V, 16). Grace and ingratitude, therefore, clash together in one single time, as if in one single arena. For wisdom has long since been fighting against malice, and for this reason it has now also come down into the arena of this world. It fights, I say, unwilling to be conquered by evil, but willing to conquer evil with good. Iniquity was abounding, and although human charity had grown cold, divine charity nevertheless did not grow cold. That great ardor of charity is indeed, as it is written, unquenchable by many waters (Cantic. VIII, 7); for though the multitude of sins now demanded the final judgment, yet God sent his Son, not to judge the world, but that the world might be saved through him (Joan. III, 17). Therefore, when the malice of the world, now almost complete, was signaling the end of time, the coming of the Redeemer poured out upon human affairs a new and unexpected fullness of time. For when the world had grown old, and was near destruction through the age of time, suddenly, at the coming of its Founder, it was renewed with a new and unhoped-for youthfulness of virtue, and with a certain youthful warmth of faith. For faith, whose earliest age—a kind of childhood, as it were—was in the Patriarchs, who arose in the first morning of the Church then being born; whose adolescence was in the Prophets; at last grew up into the fullness of youthful strength in the Apostles, when it also displayed to the world the warmth of its vigor, made visible in the splendid and most valiant triumphs of countless martyrs. This mature and full age of faith the Apostle calls the fullness of time—namely, when those who were under the schoolmaster of the law, and who, while they were children, differed in nothing from a slave, now grown up, received the freedom of sons through the Only-begotten of the Father (Galat. VI, 1-9)1. He indeed, so that no fullness might be lacking to his own time, came full both of grace and of truth, so that through grace he might cause the commands of the law to be fulfilled, and through truth he himself might fulfill the promises; to such a degree that whatever had been done or said mystically throughout all the ages gone before was fulfilled more truly and more fully in this fullness of time. For even this very day has displayed, as it were recapitulated in brief, so many mysteries, so many oracles, that of it may truly be said: 'Being perfected in a short time, he fulfilled many years' (Sap. IV, 13).

4. Cum ergo tot modis constet quia venit beata plenitudo temporis, recte querelas insipientium Salomon compescuit, dicens: Ne dicas, Quid, putas, causae est quod meliora fuere tempora priora quam nunc sunt? Stulta est, inquit, hujusmodi interrogatio (Eccle. VII, 11), cum manifeste felicissima tempora fecerit hominibus gratia Dei; ingratitudo autem hominum pessima fecerit ea sibi. O tempus acceptabile, cui tam beatum, tam auspicatum initium dedit dies iste, quo natus conditor aeternus temporis, aeternitatem mortalibus nuntiavit! O vere dies salutis dies iste, quo nata salus mundi se ipsam poculum salutare miscuit aegrotis! O fratres, de die isto loquebatur, ni fallor, Sapiens filio suo: Non defrauderis a die bono. Et exponens quid sit non defraudari: Particula, inquit, boni doni non te praetereat (Eccli. XIV, 14). Diem quippe bonum pro eo dicit, quia datum est hodie donum bonum, prorsus datum optimum, donumque perfectum, descendens a Patre luminum; quod quale sit nemo scit, nisi qui accepit. At vos, fratres, spiritum qui ex Deo est accepistis, ut sciatis quae a Deo donata sunt vobis, qui ex sententia cordium cantatis: Filius datus est nobis (Isai. IX, 6). Filius iste, panis est filiorum, qui de seipso hodie beatum ac solemne universae familiae Patris exhibet epulum.

English translation

4. Since, then, it is established in so many ways that the blessed fullness of time has come, Solomon rightly checked the complaints of fools, saying: 'Do not say, What do you suppose is the reason that former times were better than now are?' 'Such a question,' he says, 'is foolish' (Eccle. VII, 11), since plainly the grace of God has made the times most happy for mankind, but the ingratitude of men has made those same times most wretched for themselves. O acceptable time, to which this day gave so blessed, so auspicious a beginning—the day on which the eternal Founder of time was born and announced eternity to mortals! O truly this day is the day of salvation, on which the salvation of the world, being born, mixed itself as a saving cup for the sick! O brothers, it was of this day, unless I am mistaken, that the Wise Man spoke to his son: 'Do not defraud yourself of a good day.' And explaining what it means not to be defrauded, he says: 'Let not the portion of a good gift pass you by' (Eccli. XIV, 14). He calls it a good day precisely because today a good gift was given—indeed the very best gift given, and a perfect gift, descending from the Father of lights—a gift of such a kind that no one knows it except the one who has received it. But you, brothers, have received the Spirit who is from God, that you may know the things that have been given to you by God, you who sing from the conviction of your hearts: 'A Son is given to us' (Isai. IX, 6). This Son is the bread of sons, who today, from himself, provides a blessed and solemn banquet for the whole family of the Father.

5. Gratias tibi, Pater misericordiarum, qui panem nostrum quotidianum das nobis hodie, tantaque largitate aperuisti manum tuam ad implendum omne animal benedictione, ut ipsa etiam benedictione plenum inveniatur eorum praesepe. Quam miserum, quam brutum ac stolidum, imo quam sibi nequam et invidum illud animal, quod sese isto die bono defraudat, ut eum particula doni boni praetereat, ut scilicet expers gratiae coelestis, quae ei proposita est, fiat, diemque totius refectionis et gaudii tristi ac jejuno corde praetereat; quasi necdum venerit copiosissima plenitudo temporis, nec praesepia simplicium et humilium panis impleverit coelestis! Hujusmodi hominem sibi nequam et invidum, Deo ingratum et injuriosum, notat Sapientia, cum ait: Oculus malus ad mala opera, et non saturabitur pane, indigens et in tristitia erit super mensam suam. Ideo, inquit, non saturabitur bonis anima ipsius, quia oculus ejus ad mala, nec revertetur oculus ejus, ut videat bona, ut cum pietate et fide consideret quae ei apposita sunt in magna divitis mensa. Nequam est enim, inquit, oculus lividi, avertens faciem suam et despiciens animam suam (Eccli. XIV, 10, 8). Procul dubio, fratres, si faciem non avertimus a consideratione illius qui jacet in praesepi, ipso intuitu possumus felicissime pasci, dicemusque: Dominus pascit me, et nihil mihi deerit; in loco pascuae, ibi me collocavit (Psal. XXII, 1, 2). Tunc plane sciemus quia venit desideratissima plenitudo temporis, in quo misit Deus Filium suum, per quem tanta jam replemur plenitudine bonorum: ipsi benedictio et gratiarum actio, et nunc, et per infinita saecula saeculorum.

English translation

5. Thanks be to you, Father of mercies, who give us our daily bread today, and who with such great generosity have opened your hand to fill every living creature with blessing, that even their manger is found full of that same blessing. How wretched, how brutish and foolish—indeed how wicked and grudging toward itself—is that creature which cheats itself on this good day, so that the portion of the good gift passes it by; so that, lacking the heavenly grace set before it, it lets the day of all refreshment and joy pass by with a sad and fasting heart, as though the most abundant fullness of time had not yet come, and the heavenly bread had not filled the mangers of the simple and the humble! Wisdom marks out such a man, wicked and grudging toward himself, ungrateful and offensive toward God, when it says: 'An evil eye is bent toward evil deeds, and he shall not be satisfied with bread; he shall be needy and sorrowful at his own table.' 'Therefore,' it says, 'his soul shall not be filled with good things, because his eye is bent toward evil things, nor will his eye turn back to see good things,' so that he might with devotion and faith consider what has been set before him at the great table of the rich host. 'For wicked,' it says, 'is the eye of the envious man, turning away his face and despising his own soul' (Eccli. XIV, 10, 8). Without doubt, brothers, if we do not turn our face away from gazing on him who lies in the manger, by that very gaze we can be most happily fed, and we shall say: 'The Lord feeds me, and I shall want nothing; in a place of pasture, there he has set me' (Psal. XXII, 1, 2). Then we shall clearly know that the most longed-for fullness of time has come, in which God sent his Son, through whom we are now filled with so great a fullness of good things: to him be blessing and thanksgiving, both now and through the endless ages of ages.
