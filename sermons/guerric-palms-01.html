Editorial introduction

Guerric of Igny’s first Palm Sunday sermon presents Christ’s service as the remedy for human pride. Christ is imagined addressing the disobedient servant, offering to bear his burdens, feed him and redeem him; the meditation then turns to the Passion and a prayer for grace to imitate humility, patience and obedience in the common life. The collection belongs approximately to Guerric’s abbacy in 1138–1157, but no individual delivery date is established. This reading follows the 1879 Patrologia Latina edition. The source explicitly names Palm Sunday. Historical polemic is retained and identified in an editorial note.

Latin original

1. Hoc sentite in vobis, quod et in Christo Jesu, qui, cum in forma Dei esset (Philipp. ii, 5, 6), etc. Audiat servus nequam et fugitivus, hominem dico, qui, cum esset in natura et conditione servili et necessitate serviendi, servire detrectans, arripere sibi conatus est libertatem et aequalitatem Domini sui. Christus cum in forma Dei esset, non rapina, sed natura aequalis, quia coomnipotens, coaeternus et consubstantialis, exinaniens semetipsum, non solum formam servi accepit, in similitudinem hominum factus; sed etiam ministerium servi implevit, humilians semetipsum, et obediens factus Patri usque ad mortem, mortem autem crucis (Ibid. 8). Sed parum tibi videatur quod, cum esset Filius et coaequalis, tanquam servus servivit Patri, si non et servo suo plus quam servus servivit. Factus quidem erat homo, ut serviret Creatori. Et quid justius quam ut servias illi a quo creatus es, sine quo nec esse potes? et quid beatius aut sublimius quam servire illi cui servire regnare est? Non serviam, inquit homo Creatori. Ego igitur serviam tibi, inquit Creator, homini. Tu recumbe; ego ministrabo, ego tibi pedes lavabo. Tu quiesce; ego languores tuos feram, infirmitates portabo. Utere me, ut libet, in omni necessitate tua, non modo ut servo tuo, sed etiam ut jumento tuo, ut peculio tuo. Si fatigatus aut oneratus es, ego te et onus tuum feram, ut legem meam primus impleam. Alter, inquit, alterius onera portate, et sic adimplebitis legem Christi (Galat. vi, 2). Si esuris, aut sitis, et ad manum forte nihil melius habes, nec alter tibi vitulus aeque saginatus praesto est, ecce ego paratus immolari, ut carnes meas comedas, et sanguinem bibas. Nec verearis ex morte servi damna servitii ejus pati; etiam comestus et bibitus, manebo tibi integer et vivus, serviamque ut prius. Si in captivitatem ductus aut venumdatus es, ecce vende me, teque pretio mei, vel me ipso pretio redime. Vile quidem mancipium videor; sed etsi noctu et clanculo distrahar quasi res furtiva, etsi ab avarissimis Judaeorum sacerdotibus1 emar; attamen saltem triginta argenteos appretiari potero; hoc pretio mei poterit emi sepultura peregrinorum, me pretio vita sepultorum. Si infirmaris, et mori times, ego moriar pro te, ut de sanguine meo tibi conficias medicamenta vitae.

English translation

1. Have this mind in you which was also in Christ Jesus, who, being in the form of God (Philipp. ii, 5, 6), etc. Let the wicked runaway servant hear this—I mean man, who, although by nature and condition a servant and under the necessity of serving, refused to serve and tried to seize for himself the freedom and equality of his Lord. Christ, being in the form of God, equal by nature rather than by robbery, because he is equally almighty, coeternal and consubstantial, emptied himself. He not only took the form of a servant, being made in the likeness of men, but also fulfilled a servant’s ministry, humbling himself and becoming obedient to the Father unto death, even death on a cross (Ibid. 8). Yet think it little that, although the Son and coequal, he served the Father as a servant, unless he also served his own servant more than a servant would. Man had indeed been made to serve the Creator. What could be more just than to serve him by whom you were created, without whom you cannot even exist? And what could be happier or more exalted than to serve him whom to serve is to reign? “I will not serve,” man says to the Creator. “Then I will serve you,” the Creator says to man. “Recline at table; I will minister to you, I will wash your feet. Rest; I will bear your ailments and carry your infirmities. Use me as you please in every need, not only as your servant but also as your beast of burden, as your property. If you are tired or burdened, I will carry you and your burden, so that I may be the first to fulfil my own law. Bear one another’s burdens, he says, and so you will fulfil the law of Christ (Galat. vi, 2). If you are hungry or thirsty and happen to have nothing better at hand, and no other calf as well fattened for you is at hand, here I am, ready to be sacrificed, so that you may eat my flesh and drink my blood. Do not fear that the servant’s death will cost you his service: even when eaten and drunk, I shall remain whole and alive for you, and I shall serve as before. If you have been taken captive or sold, sell me, and redeem yourself with my price, or with myself as the price. I seem a worthless slave; yet even if I am sold by night and in secret as stolen property, even if I am bought by the most avaricious priests of the Jews1, I can still be valued at least at thirty pieces of silver. With this price paid for me a burial place for strangers can be bought; with me as the price, life for the buried. If you are sick and afraid to die, I shall die for you, so that from my blood you may prepare medicines of life for yourself.”

2. Euge, serve bone et fidelis, servisti revera, servisti in omni fide et veritate, servisti in omni patientia et longanimitate. Non tepide, qui exsultasti ut gigas ad currendam viam (Psal. xxiii, 6)2 obedientiae non ficte, qui etiam animam post tot et tantos labores superimpendisti; non murmurose, qui, innocens flagellatus, nec os aperuisti. Scriptum est, et justum est: Servus sciens voluntatem domini sui, et non faciens digna, plagis vapulabit multis (Luc. xii, 47, 48). Sed iste servus, obsecro, quid non dignum fecit? quid debuit facere et non fecit? Bene omnia fecit, clamant qui facta ejus observabant, et surdos fecit audire et mutos loqui (Marc. vii, 37). Omnia fecit digna, et quomodo sic omnia passus est indigna? Dorsum posuit ad flagella, plagis vapulavit non paucis nec parvis; indicio sunt sanguinis rivuli qui de tot partibus profluunt corporis. Contumelia et tormento interrogatus fuit, veluti servus aut latro subditus quaestioni, qua confessio extorqueatur criminis. O detestabilem superbiam hominis servire contemnentis, quae alio exemplo non poterat humiliari, nisi servitute et tali servitute Domini sui! et utinam vel sic possit! utinam vel nunc habeat et referat gratiam tantae humilitati ac bonitati! Sed, ut mihi videtur, adhuc audio eumdem Dominum apud Isaiam conquerentem de ingratitudine nequam servi, ubi scilicet ait: Non te servire feci in oblatione, nec laborem tibi praebui in thure. Verum tamen me servire fecisti in peccatis tuis, laborem praebuisti mihi in iniquitatibus tuis (Isai. xliii, 23, 24). Et quem laborem? Usque ad defatigationem, esuriem et sitim; sed usque ad sudorem, sudorem autem sanguinis decurrentis in terram (Luc. xxii, 44); sed usque ad mortem, mortem autem crucis: ne omnia modo replicem, quod alapis caesus, sputis illitus, coronatus spinis, confixus clavis, lancea perforatus, aceto et felle potatus. Hoc torcular, inquit, calcavi solus, et de gentibus non est vir mecum (Isai. lxiii, 3). Vos ergo, qui statis tota die otiosi, attendite et videte si est labor sicut labor meus.

English translation

2. Well done, good and faithful servant! You have truly served; you have served with all faithfulness and truth, with all patience and forbearance. Not lukewarmly, for you rejoiced like a giant to run the course (Psal. xxiii, 6)2 of obedience; not insincerely, for after so many and such great labours you spent your very life besides; not with murmuring, for, though innocent and scourged, you did not even open your mouth. It is written, and it is just: “The servant who knows his lord’s will and does not act worthily will be beaten with many blows” (Luc. xii, 47, 48). But this servant, I ask you, what did he do that was unworthy? What ought he to have done that he did not do? “He has done all things well,” cry those who observed his deeds, “and has made the deaf hear and the mute speak” (Marc. vii, 37). He did everything worthily; how, then, did he suffer all these unworthy things? He offered his back to the scourges; he was beaten with blows neither few nor small, as the streams of blood flowing from so many parts of his body bear witness. He was questioned with insult and torture, like a slave or robber subjected to torment to wring out a confession of crime. O detestable pride of man, who disdains to serve! No other example could humble it except the service, and such service, of his own Lord. If only even this could do so! If only even now he would feel and show gratitude for such humility and goodness! Yet I seem still to hear the same Lord complaining in Isaiah about the wicked servant’s ingratitude, where he says: “I did not make you serve me with offerings, nor impose labour on you with incense. Yet you have made me serve with your sins; you have imposed labour on me with your iniquities” (Isai. xliii, 23, 24). And what labour? To the point of exhaustion, hunger and thirst; even to sweat, sweat of blood running down to the ground (Luc. xxii, 44); even to death, death on a cross. I need not now recount everything: how he was struck with blows, smeared with spittle, crowned with thorns, fastened with nails, pierced with a lance, given vinegar and gall to drink. “This winepress,” he says, “I have trodden alone, and of the nations there is no man with me” (Isai. lxiii, 3). You, then, who stand idle all day, attend and see whether there is any labour like my labour.

3. Utique valde laborasti, Domine mi, in serviendo mihi; justum profecto et aequum esset ut saltem de caetero tu quiesceres, tuusque tibi servus vel ordine vicis suae, quia ad ipsum ventum est, serviret. Quam magno, Domine mi, inutile tibi meum redemisti servitium, qui nec ministeriis indiges Angelorum! Quam suavi et benigna arte pietatis recuperasti tibi et subdidisti contumacem servum, vincens in bono malum, humilitate confundens superbum, beneficiis obruens ingratum! Sic nimirum, sic sapientia vincit malitiam; sic carbones ignis congessisti super caput contumacis, quibus accenderetur ad poenitentiam. Vicisti ergo, Domine, vicisti rebellem: ecce do manus in vincula tua, jugoque tuo suppono cervicem. Dignare tantum ut tibi serviam, patere ut tibi laborem. Accipe me servum sempiternum, licet inutilem, nisi nunc quoque mecum sit et mecum laboret gratia tua, semper et praeveniens et subsequens. Praeveniat nos, prior ostendens humilitatis et patientiae exempla, subsequatur nos, adjuvans ut imitemur ostensa. Quam felices nos, o fratres mei, si super hoc audiamus consilium Apostoli: Hoc sentite, inquit, in vobis quod et in Christo Jesu (Philipp. ii, 5) praecessisse cognoscitis! hoc est, nullus extollatur supra se, sed magis humilietur infra se; qui major est, aliis serviat; si quis laeditur3, prior satisfaciat; in commune usque ad mortem quisque obediat. His vestigiis, fratres, sequemur Christum in forma Dei, in qua vivit et regnat per omnia saecula saeculorum. Amen.4

English translation

3. You have indeed laboured greatly, my Lord, in serving me. Surely it would be just and right that at least from now on you should rest, and that your servant should serve you, if only because his turn has now come. At what a price, my Lord, you have redeemed my service, useless to you who do not even need the ministries of angels! With what a gentle and kindly art of loving mercy you have won back and subjected your stubborn servant, overcoming evil with good, confounding the proud by humility, overwhelming the ungrateful with benefits! In this way, truly, wisdom conquers malice; in this way you have heaped coals of fire upon the stubborn man’s head, to kindle him to repentance. You have conquered, then, Lord; you have conquered the rebel. Here I offer my hands for your bonds and bow my neck beneath your yoke. Only deign to let me serve you; allow me to labour for you. Receive me as your servant for ever, though a useless one, unless even now your grace is with me and labours with me, always both going before and following after. May it go before us, first showing examples of humility and patience; may it follow us, helping us to imitate what has been shown. How happy we shall be, my brothers, if in this we heed the Apostle’s counsel: “Have this mind in you,” he says, “which was also in Christ Jesus” (Philipp. ii, 5), as you know it was in him before you. That is, let no one exalt himself above himself, but rather humble himself beneath himself; let the greater serve the others; if anyone is injured, let him be the first to make amends3; let each obey in the common life unto death. In these footsteps, brothers, we shall follow Christ in the form of God, in which he lives and reigns for ever and ever. Amen.4

2. Editorial note

col. 129 (PDF 69)

The edition cites Psalm 23:6 for the running giant. The image is in Psalm 18:6 in historical Vulgate numbering (Psalm 19 in modern numbering). The following Luke 12:47–48 reference is also retained; the saying about many blows occurs in verse 47.

3. Editorial note

“Going before and following after” describes grace that initiates and assists our imitation of Christ. Guerric asks the injured person—not the person causing injury—to be first to make amends.
