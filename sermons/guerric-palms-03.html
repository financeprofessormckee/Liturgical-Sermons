Introduction & Scripture references

Editorial introduction

Guerric sets the procession and the Passion side by side: Christ honored as king and Christ condemned as a thief. He hears the Son's cry of abandonment and answers it with the future glory that the procession prefigured. Christ's meekness when honored and his zeal in cleansing the temple form a pattern for those in authority; his unchanged mind amid changed fortunes is set before the brothers. His argument includes polemic against Christ's Jewish opponents. The sermon appears under the Palm Sunday heading in the 1879 edition of Patrologia Latina. The collection is associated approximately with Guerric's abbacy in 1138–1157; the individual sermon is undated.

SERMON III.

Latin original

De duplici specie qua Christus proponitur spectandus.

English translation

On the twofold appearance under which Christ is set before us to be beheld.

¶cols. 133-134; cols. 135-136
Latin original

1. Desiderium animae nostrae, speciosum forma prae filiis hominum, hodierna dies alia et alia specie repraesentat filiis hominum: utraque quidem specie spectabilem, in utraque desiderabilem et amabilem, quia in utraque salvatorem hominum; licet in una sublimem, in altera humilem; in ista gloriosum, in illa aerumnosum; in ista venerabilem, in illa miserabilem: si tamen dici decet miserabilis, qui miseriam suscepit dignatione miserationis, ut de miseria sua misericordiam praestaret miseris; non ut qui sibi beatitudo est, misericordiam a miseris flagitaret. Ubi ergo miserandus videri voluit, ibi et amplius venerandus fuit. Sed sustinui, inquit, qui simul contristaretur, et non fuit; qui consolaretur, et non inveni (Psal. LXVIII, 21). Ita qui miseratione omnium miser ultro fieri voluit, apud nullum fere miserationem invenit. Sed ubi, inquies, spectatur hodie sublimis et gloriosus, ubi humilis et aerumnosus? Vide processionem; audi passionem. In his evidenter agnoscere poteris quod Isaias ait: Sicut obstupuerunt super eum multi, sic inglorius erit inter viros vultus ejus, et forma ejus inter filios hominum (Isai. LII, 14). Obstupuerunt multi de gloria quasi victoris triumphantis, cum Jerusalem ingrederetur; sed nihilominus paulo post vultus ejus inglorius et despectus, cum pateretur. Cum intrasset Jerosolymam, ait Matthaeus, commota est universa civitas, dicens: Quis est hic? (Matth. XXI, 10). Cum pateretur, confusio faciem ejus operuit, ut vere confiteatur: Exaltatus, humiliatus sum et confusus (Psal. LXXXVII, 16). Sane quod hic se confusum dicit, intelligendum est secundum hoc quod alibi dicit de facie corporis: Operuit confusio faciem meam (Psal. LXVIII, 8); quia non pepercerunt in faciem meam spuere, velare, percutere et illudere. Nam facies mentis quae semper et immobiliter habitabat cum vultu Dei, nec turbari poterat nec confundi. Dominus Deus, inquit, auxiliator meus; ideo non sum confusus; ideo posui faciem meam ut petram durissimam, et scio quoniam non confundar (Isai. L, 7). In te enim, Domine, speravi, non confundar in aeternum (Psal. XXX, 2). Confundantur potius illi, et non confundar ego; paveant, et non paveam ego (Jerem. XVII, 18).

English translation

1. The desire of our soul, beautiful in form beyond the sons of men, today's feast presents to the sons of men under one appearance and another: in each appearance indeed worthy of beholding, in each desirable and lovable, because in each he is the Savior of men; although in one sublime, in the other humble; in this one glorious, in that one full of hardship; in this one venerable, in that one pitiable—if indeed it is fitting to call pitiable him who took on wretchedness by the condescension of his compassion, so that out of his own misery he might show mercy to the wretched; not as one who, being blessedness itself, should beg for mercy from the wretched. Therefore where he wished to appear pitiable, there he was all the more to be venerated. But I waited, he says, for one who would grieve with me, and there was none; for one who would console me, and I found none (Psal. LXVIII, 21). Thus he who willingly chose to become wretched out of compassion for all found compassion from almost no one. But where, you will ask, is he beheld today as sublime and glorious, and where as humble and full of hardship? Look at the procession; listen to the Passion. In these you will be able clearly to recognize what Isaiah says: As many were astonished at him, so his countenance shall be without glory among men, and his appearance among the sons of men (Isai. LII, 14). Many were astonished at the glory, as of a triumphant victor, when he entered Jerusalem; but nonetheless, a little afterward his countenance was without glory and despised, when he suffered. When he had entered Jerusalem, says Matthew, the whole city was stirred, saying: Who is this? (Matth. XXI, 10). When he suffered, confusion covered his face, so that he might truly confess: I was exalted, I have been humbled and confounded (Psal. LXXXVII, 16). Indeed, when he here says he was confounded, it must be understood according to what he says elsewhere about the face of his body: Confusion has covered my face (Psal. LXVIII, 8); because they did not spare to spit upon my face, to cover it, to strike it, and to mock it. For the face of his mind, which dwelt always and unchangeably with the countenance of God, could neither be disturbed nor confounded. The Lord God, he says, is my helper; therefore I am not confounded; therefore I have set my face like the hardest rock, and I know that I shall not be confounded (Isai. L, 7). For in thee, O Lord, have I hoped; let me never be confounded (Psal. XXX, 2). Let them rather be confounded, and let me not be confounded; let them fear, and let me not fear (Jerem. XVII, 18).

2. Si igitur, ut dicere coeperam, hodierna processio simul et passio considerentur, ibi plane videtur Jesus sublimis et gloriosus, hic humilis et aerumnosus. In processione namque cogitatur in honore regis; in passione spectatur in poena latronis. Ibi circumdant eum gloria et honor; hic non est ei species neque decor. Ibi gaudium hominum et gloria plebis; hic opprobrium hominum et abjectio plebis. Ibi acclamatur ei: Hosanna filio David, benedictus qui venit rex Israel (Matth. XXI, 9); hic inclamatur, reus mortis (Id. XXVI, 66); subsannaturque quod se fecerit regem Israel. Ibi cum ramis palmarum ei occurritur; hic alapis palmarum in faciem caeditur, et arundine percutitur caput. Ibi praeconiis sublimatur; hic saturatur opprobriis. Ibi certatim ei via sternitur vestimentis alienis; hic exuitur propriis. Ibi tanquam rex justus et salvator suscipitur in Jerusalem; hic tanquam reus et seductor damnatus ejicitur de Jerusalem. Ibi sedet in asino stipatus obsequiis; hic pendet in ligno crucis caesus flagellis, confossus plagis, et desertus a suis. Ecce plus quam Job hic, cui tam repente, tam vehementer omnia semel in contrarium Deus vertit. Sufferentiam Job audistis, finem Domini vidistis (Jacobi V, 11), ait Jacobus apostolus. Ac si diceret: Sufferentia Job fuit usque ad reditum substantiae; sufferentia Domini usque ad exitum vitae. Job quidem patienter damna sustinuit, sed mox in terra sua duplicia suscepit; Christus ut erat repletus miseriis, et inebriatus amaritudinibus, e mundo migravit. Itaque plus quam Job hic, qui de summa, ut putabatur, repente semelque dejectus felicitate, in extrema atque gravissima finem sortitus est calamitate. Et haec, inquit, passus sum absque iniquitate manus meae, cum haberem mundas ad Deum preces (Job XVI, 18), etiam pro crucifixoribus, ut eis ignosceret.

English translation

2. If, then, as I began to say, today's procession and Passion are considered together, there Jesus is plainly seen as sublime and glorious, here as humble and full of hardship. For in the procession he is regarded with the honor of a king; in the Passion he is beheld with the punishment of a thief. There glory and honor surround him; here there is in him no comeliness nor beauty. There is the joy of men and the glory of the people; here the reproach of men and the outcast of the people. There he is acclaimed: Hosanna to the son of David, blessed is he who comes, the king of Israel (Matth. XXI, 9); here it is cried out against him that he is guilty of death (Id. XXVI, 66); and he is mocked for having made himself king of Israel. There he is met with branches of palms; here he is struck in the face with blows of the palm of the hand, and his head is beaten with a reed. There he is exalted with acclamations; here he is filled with reproaches. There the way is strewn before him eagerly with others' garments; here he is stripped of his own. There he is received into Jerusalem as a just king and savior; here he is cast out of Jerusalem as a condemned criminal and deceiver. There he sits upon an ass surrounded by homage; here he hangs on the wood of the cross, scourged with lashes, pierced through with wounds, and abandoned by his own. Behold, here is one greater than Job, for whom God so suddenly, so violently, turned all things at once into their opposite. You have heard of the patience of Job, you have seen the end of the Lord (Jacobi V, 11), says the apostle James. As if he said: The patience of Job lasted until the return of his possessions; the patience of the Lord lasted until the end of his life. Job indeed patiently bore his losses, but soon received double in his own land; Christ, filled as he was with miseries and made drunk with bitterness, departed from the world. Therefore here is one greater than Job, who, cast down suddenly and once for all, as it seemed, from the summit of happiness, met his end in the utmost and gravest calamity. And these things, he says, I have suffered without iniquity of my hand, since I had pure prayers to offer to God (Job XVI, 18), even on behalf of those who crucified me, that he might forgive them.

3. An non igitur recte videbitur velut turbatus tanta tamque subita rerum mutatione Filius Patrem interpellare illa voce lacrymabili: Deus, Deus meus, utquid dereliquisti me? (Matth. XXVII, 46). Certe manus tuae fecerunt me! in voluntate tua deduxisti me, et paulo ante cum gloria suscepisti me; et sic repente praecipitas me? Elevans allisisti me, quasi super ventum ponens me, et elidens valde. Exaltatus, humiliatus sum et confusus; et eo confusus amplius, quo tunc exaltatus sublimius, et nunc humiliatus profundius; quantoque tunc altius elevatus, tanto nunc gravius allisus. Justum quidem est, o Pater, ut qui se exaltat humilietur, et elatio indigni digna dejectione puniatur; sed nunquid similiter justum erat ut, quem tu exaltaveras, ita confusibiliter humiliaretur, et gloriam quam humilitas meruerat, tanta sequeretur contumelia? Nunquid mihi, Pater, iratus fuisti, quod vel ad horam hic me passus sum honorari; et illud etiam perexiguum quod susceperam de bonis hujus mundi, exsolvendum erat ante mortem in poenis et contumeliis, ne postea mihi posset objici: Fili, suscepisti bona in vita tua? (Luc. XVI, 25). At qui tuus erat honor, o Pater, honor erat Filii; quia qui non honorificat Filium, non honorificat Patrem qui misit illum. Ecce exprobrant inimici tui, Domine, exprobrant commutationem Christi tui; ecce gloriantur in contumelia mea et tormento, qui paulo ante cruciabantur gloria et honore meo. Nunquid non respondebo exprobrantibus mihi verbum, ut sciant aliquando qua dispensatione commutaveris Christum tuum, quo consilio sic exaltaveris mox humiliandum, vel humiliaveris exaltatum?

English translation

3. Will it not, then, rightly seem that the Son, as though troubled by so great and so sudden a change of circumstances, cries out to the Father with that tearful voice: "God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Matth. XXVII, 46). Surely your hands made me! By your will you led me, and but a little while ago you received me with glory; and do you now so suddenly cast me down? Lifting me up, you have dashed me down, setting me as it were upon the wind, and shattering me exceedingly. Exalted, I have been humbled and confounded; and the more confounded, the more sublimely I was then exalted, and the more deeply I am now humbled; and the higher I was then lifted up, the more grievously am I now dashed down. It is indeed just, O Father, that whoever exalts himself should be humbled, and that the pride of the unworthy should be punished with a fitting downfall; but was it likewise just that he whom you had exalted should be so shamefully humbled, and that such great contempt should follow the glory which humility had merited? Were you, Father, angry with me, because I allowed myself to be honored here even for an hour; and was even that very small portion which I had received of the goods of this world to be paid back before death in sufferings and insults, lest afterward it might be objected to me: "Son, you received good things in your life" (Luc. XVI, 25)? But the honor that was yours, O Father, was the honor of the Son; for he who does not honor the Son does not honor the Father who sent him. Behold, your enemies, Lord, reproach—they reproach the changed state of your Christ; behold, they glory in my contempt and torment, who but a little while ago were tormented by my glory and honor. Shall I not then answer those who reproach me with a word, that they may know at last by what dispensation you have changed your Christ, by what counsel you have thus exalted one soon to be humbled, or humbled one who was exalted?

Latin original

4. Respondebis, o Domine Jesu, respondebis exprobrantibus tibi verbum, et verbum asperum, quando scilicet respondebit eis in facie arrogantia eorum, et arguet eos malitia eorum, videntes in quem transfixerunt. Cum enim videbunt Filium hominis venientem in nubibus cum potestate magna et majestate (Matth. XXIV, 30), tunc scient quod modo nolunt credere, quia illius posterioris gloriae, prior hodiernae processionis gloria, mysterium fuerit et figura; sicut passionis contumelia, meritum et causa. Tunc intelligent quia Christus nunc honoratus, nunc ludibrio habitus, his qui pereunt positus est in offensionem et ruinam, sicut his qui salvi fiunt, in resurrectionem et doctrinam; quia quod sacramentum est redemptionis, simul est etiam documentum aedificationis. Sic enim postulabant merita superborum, ut eorum superbiae et honor Christi fieret scandalum, et mors praecipitium; atque invidiam juste pereuntium gloria triumphantis excitaret, morientis poena damnaret. Caeterum his qui salvi fiunt, id est nobis, omnino erat necessarium ut Christus, transiens viam hujus mundi, sequentibus se viam sterneret, sicut per adversa, sic etiam per prospera; atque prius exaltatus, post humiliatus, suo traderet exemplo qualiter modestia servaretur in honore, patientia teneretur in contumelia vel dolore. Ipse siquidem honorari potuit, inflari non potuit; voluit despici, sed nescivit aut pusillanimitate dejici, aut ira morderi. Sicut enim alias cum vellent eum rapere et facere regem, ipse refugiens, et in solitudinem ad orandum aufugiens (Joan. VI, 15), docuit exemplo quod dixit et verbo, ne scilicet velimus in sublime tolli; sic modo quadam alia dispensatione sese ad horam honorari patiens, et nihilominus solitam, quia inolitam, mansuetudinem in honore retinens, formam dabat his qui in potestate sunt constituti. Qui tamen ut discerent ea discretione per humilitatem esse mansueti, ut, cum res postularet, per zelum essent erecti, Dominus statim in templum regressus, flagello facto de funiculis vindicat injurias Patris, magisque eligit furorem sacerdotum in necem suam provocare, quam templi profanationem dissimulare (Matth. XXI, 12, 13).

English translation

4. You will answer, O Lord Jesus, you will answer those who reproach you with a word—and a harsh word—namely, when their own arrogance shall answer them to their face, and their own malice shall accuse them, as they see him whom they pierced. For when they shall see the Son of man coming in the clouds with great power and majesty (Matth. XXIV, 30), then they will know what they now refuse to believe: that the earlier glory of today's procession was the mystery and figure of that later glory, just as the contempt of the Passion was its merit and cause. Then they will understand that Christ, now honored, now held up to mockery, has been set, for those who perish, as a stumbling block and a ruin, just as for those who are being saved he has been set for resurrection and instruction; because that which is a sacrament of redemption is at the same time also a lesson for edification. For thus the deserts of the proud demanded: that for their pride even the honor of Christ should become a scandal, and his death a stumbling-block to ruin; and that the glory of the triumphant one should arouse the envy of those who are justly perishing, while the punishment of the dying one should condemn that envy. But for those who are being saved, that is, for us, it was altogether necessary that Christ, passing along the way of this world, should pave the way for those who follow him, as much through adversity as through prosperity; and that, first exalted and afterward humbled, he should hand down by his own example how modesty is to be kept in honor, and patience held fast in contempt or sorrow. For he indeed could be honored, but could not be puffed up; he chose to be despised, but did not know how to be cast down by faintheartedness, or stung by anger. For just as on another occasion, when they wished to seize him and make him king, he fled, withdrawing into solitude to pray (Joan. VI, 15), teaching by example what he had also said in word—namely, that we should not desire to be raised to the heights—so now, by a certain other dispensation, allowing himself to be honored for an hour, and nevertheless retaining in that honor his accustomed meekness (accustomed, because ingrained), he gave a pattern to those who are set in positions of power. Yet, that they might learn to be meek through humility with such discretion that, when the matter demanded it, they should be roused through zeal, the Lord, returning at once into the temple, with a whip made of cords avenges the injuries done to his Father, and chooses rather to provoke the fury of the priests to his own death than to overlook the profanation of the temple (Matth. XXI, 12, 13).

5. Ut igitur, fratres, inoffenso pede tam per prospera, quam per adversa sequamur ducem nostrum, in processione consideremus eum in honore positum, in passione contumeliis et doloribus subditum; nunquam tamen in tanta mutatione rerum mente mutatum, etsi vultum immutaverit coram Abimelech, id est regno [al. rege] Judaeorum, quam commutationem Christi caecitas exprobrat perfidorum. De immutabili mentis illius statu dicit Scriptura: Homo sanctus in sapientia permanet sicut sol; nam stultus ut luna mutatur (Eccli. XXVII, 12). De mutatione faciei illius dicit alia Scriptura: Sapientia hominis lucet in vultu ejus, et potentissimus faciem illius immutabit (Eccle. VIII, 1). Prorsus in vultu tuo, Domine Jesu, qualitercunque videatur immutatus, sive gloriosus sive inglorius appareat, lucet sapientia, de vultu tuo candor lucis aeternae radiat; utinam super nos lumen vultus tui, Domine, effulgeat! Vultus tuus sicut in tristibus, sic in laetis sibi modestus, serenus, ac totus arcano lumine cordis floridus; justis hilaris et jucundus, poenitentibus clemens et pius. Intendite, fratres, in vultum serenissimi Regis. In hilaritate vultus regis vita, dicit Scriptura, et clementia ejus quasi imber serotinus (Prov. XVI, 15). Aspexit in Protoplastum, et mox animatus spiravit in vitam (Gen. I, 27); respexit Petrum, et mox compunctus respiravit ad veniam. Mox enim ut Dominus respexit Petrum, Petrus de clementia piissimi vultus suscepit imbrem serotinum, lacrymas post peccatum (Luc. XXII, 61, 62). Lux vultus tui, o lumen aeternum, teste Job, non cadit in terram (Job. XXV, 3). Quae enim communicatio luci ad tenebras (II Cor. VI, 14)? Animae potius fidelium radios illius excipiant, aspiretque bene consciis laetitiam, sauciis medicinam. Plane vultus triumphantis Jesu, qualis spectandus est in processione, laetitia et jubilus; vultus morientis, qualis cogitandus est in passione, medela et salus. Qui timent te, videbunt me, inquit, et laetabuntur (Psal. CXVIII, 74); qui dolent se, videbunt me, et sanabuntur, sicut aspicientes in serpentem ligno suspensum post venena serpentium. Te igitur, gaudium et salus omnium, seu videant sedentem in asino, seu pendentem in ligno, vota benedicant omnium; ut, cum viderint regnantem in throno, laudent in saecula saeculorum, cui laus et honor per omnia saecula saeculorum. Amen.

English translation

5. Therefore, brothers, so that we may follow our leader with unstumbling foot both through prosperity and through adversity, let us consider him in the procession set in honor, and in the passion subjected to insults and sorrows; yet never changed in mind amid so great a change of circumstances, even though he changed his countenance before Abimelech, that is, before the kingdom [or, in another reading, the king] of the Jews—a change in Christ which the blindness of the faithless throws back at him as a reproach. Concerning the unchangeable state of his mind, Scripture says: The holy man abides in wisdom like the sun; but the fool is changed like the moon (Eccli. XXVII, 12). Concerning the change of his countenance, another Scripture says: The wisdom of a man shines in his face, and the most powerful will change his countenance (Eccle. VIII, 1). Indeed, in your countenance, Lord Jesus, however it may seem changed, whether it appears glorious or inglorious, wisdom shines; from your countenance the brightness of eternal light radiates. Would that the light of your countenance might shine upon us, Lord! Your countenance, whether in sorrow or in joy, is modest in itself, serene, and altogether flourishing with the hidden light of the heart; to the just, cheerful and pleasant; to the penitent, merciful and kind. Attend, brothers, to the countenance of the most serene King. In the cheerfulness of the king's countenance is life, says Scripture, and his clemency is like the late rain (Prov. XVI, 15). He looked upon the first-formed man, and at once the man, given life, breathed into life (Gen. I, 27); he looked back upon Peter, and at once, struck with compunction, Peter breathed again unto pardon. For as soon as the Lord looked back upon Peter, Peter received from the clemency of that most merciful countenance the late rain, tears after sin (Luc. XXII, 61, 62). The light of your countenance, O eternal light, as Job bears witness, does not fall to the ground (Job. XXV, 3). For what fellowship has light with darkness (II Cor. VI, 14)? Rather let the souls of the faithful receive its rays, and let it breathe joy upon those of good conscience, and healing upon the wounded. Clearly the countenance of Jesus triumphant, such as is to be beheld in the procession, is joy and jubilation; the countenance of the dying one, such as is to be contemplated in the passion, is remedy and salvation. Those who fear you, he says, will see me and will rejoice (Psal. CXVIII, 74); those who grieve over themselves will see me and will be healed, just as those who looked upon the serpent raised up on wood were healed after the poison of serpents. You, therefore, joy and salvation of all—whether they see you seated upon the ass, or hanging upon the wood—let the prayers of all bless you; so that when they shall see you reigning upon the throne, they may praise you forever and ever, to whom be praise and honor through all ages of ages. Amen.
