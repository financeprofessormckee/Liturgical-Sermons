Editorial introduction

Guerric reads the fiery tongues of the apostles at Pentecost. He wishes for a tongue to praise God, or at least one to heal the wounds of those who confess, and calls blessed those who draw grace from licking the sores of sinners. He asks for the unutterable groanings of the Spirit in place of the manual labor his office excuses, and urges the brothers to seek the gift of tears, through which the Spirit consoles those who mourn. The sermon appears under the Pentecost heading in the 1879 edition of Patrologia Latina. The collection is associated approximately with Guerric's abbacy in 1138–1157; the individual sermon is undated.

De linguarum mysterio et dono lacrymarum.

English translation

On the mystery of tongues and the gift of tears.

¶cols. 159-160; cols. 161-162
Latin original

1. Loquebantur variis linguis Apostoli magnalia Dei (Act. II, 11.). Nimirum ex abundantia cordis linguae loquebantur in eis. Exsultationes Dei in gutture eorum, quia charitas Dei diffusa erat in cordibus eorum. O Domine Deus meus, et ego utique similiter laudarem, si similiter potatus essem. Quia vero arida est anima mea, ideo est et torpida lingua. Sed sicut adipe et pinguedine repleatur anima mea, et labiis exsultationis laudabit os meum (Psal. LXII, 6). Eructabunt labia mea hymnum; sed cum docueris me justificationes tuas: id est cum dederis gustare quam suavis es, ut discam te diligere toto corde, tota anima, tota virtute. Bonus es tu, et in bonitate tua doce me justificationes tuas (Psal. CXVIII, 12). Bonitas siquidem tua, unctio tua est, qua doces eos de quibus praedictum est: Erunt omnes docibiles Dei (Joan. VI, 45). Beatus homo quem tu erudieris, Domine, et de lege tua docueris eum (Psal. XCIII, 12). Lex Domini immaculata convertens animas (Psal. XVIII, 8), charitas est: lex utique ignea, quae est in dextera ejus, quae cum digito Dei scribitur super latitudinem cordis, et ipsum cor amoris incendio, et eos ignito fervere facit eloquio. De excelso, inquit, misit ignem in ossibus meis, et erudivit me (Thren. I, 13). O quanta facilitate et celeritate, quanta copia et facultate, ignis ille quem Dominus Jesus misit in terram, non solum erudivit imperitos, sed etiam expedivit impeditos! Prorsus igneae linguae, quas ex se dispertivit ignis iste, quae sic ignescere fecerunt non solum mentes, sed et linguas Apostolorum, ut etiam nunc pius auditor ad sermones ignescat eorum. Prorsus ignea lingua Petri, ignea lingua Pauli, in quorum verbis nunc quoque vivit ignis perpetuus, qui et super corda nostra scintillat, si accedamus, si aurum1 vel animum ab eorum sermonibus non avertamus.

English translation

1. "The Apostles spoke in various tongues the great works of God" (Act. II, 11.). Surely it was out of the abundance of the heart that their tongues spoke in them. The exultations of God were in their throat, because the charity of God had been poured out in their hearts. O Lord my God, I too would indeed praise you in like manner, if I had been given to drink in like manner. But because my soul is parched, my tongue too is sluggish. But as my soul shall be filled with marrow and fatness, my mouth shall praise you with lips of exultation (Psal. LXII, 6). My lips shall pour forth a hymn; but only when you have taught me your justifications — that is, when you have given me to taste how sweet you are, so that I may learn to love you with my whole heart, my whole soul, my whole strength. You are good, and in your goodness teach me your justifications (Psal. CXVIII, 12). For your goodness is your anointing, by which you teach those of whom it was foretold: "They shall all be taught of God" (Joan. VI, 45). Blessed is the man whom you, O Lord, shall instruct, and whom you shall teach out of your law (Psal. XCIII, 12). The law of the Lord, unspotted, converting souls (Psal. XVIII, 8), is charity — indeed, a fiery law, which is in his right hand, which is written with the finger of God upon the breadth of the heart, and sets the heart itself ablaze with the fire of love, and makes them burn with fiery speech. "From on high," he says, "he sent fire into my bones, and taught me" (Thren. I, 13). O with what ease and swiftness, with what abundance and power, did that fire which the Lord Jesus sent upon the earth not only instruct the untaught, but also set free the hindered! Truly fiery were the tongues which this fire distributed from itself, which so set ablaze not only the minds but also the tongues of the Apostles, that even now the devout hearer catches fire at their sermons. Truly fiery is the tongue of Peter, fiery the tongue of Paul, in whose words even now a perpetual fire lives, which also sparks upon our hearts, if we draw near, if we do not turn our ear1 or our mind away from their sermons.

2. Si meruissem aliquam accipere de linguis hujusmodi, dicerem utique et ego, Dominus dedit mihi linguam mercedem meam, et in ipsa laudabo eum (Eccli. LI, 30); sicut de illis scriptum est, Loquebantur variis linguis Apostoli magnalia Dei. Dicerem et illud: Dominus dedit mihi linguam eruditam ut sciam sustentare eum qui lapsus est, verbo (Isai. L, 4). Apostoli et eorum similes linguis sibi datis magnalia Dei praedicant, tyrannos verberant, daemonia flagellant, terram compluunt, coelos aperiunt; quia linguae eorum claves coeli factae sunt, quippe quibus de ipsis coelis linguae missae sunt. Mihi autem utinam vel lingua canis data sit, qua possim primum quidem propria, deinde et aliena ulcera lingere, si qui forte sunt qui ad hoc ipsum sibi me dignentur admittere. Beati quidem, quibus amor et dilectio divinae laudis cor replet gaudio et os jubilo. Sed et illos utique beatos dixerim, qui, cum de vulneribus animarum virus ablingunt et purulentiam, trahunt in se, quo anima eorum saginetur, spiritum et gratiam. Esuriunt siquidem et sitiunt justitiam, et famem patiuntur ut canes: ideoque nihil fastidiunt quod in corpus suum possint trajicere; neminem peccatorem abominantur, quem ad justitiam possint convertere. Quod Deus mundavit, tu ne immundum dixeris (Act. XI, 9), dictum est Principi Apostolorum, et in ipso aliis: et ob hoc mactat et manducat omne genus reptilium et volatilium, et ait, Quae prius tangere nolebat anima mea, nunc prae angustia impatientis desiderii cibi mei sunt (Job. VI, 7). Quinimo mirum in modum quanto quisque peccator ante conversionem amarior, tanto postmodum conversio ejus dulcior; quantoque desperatior, tanto gratior nobis ejus salus: quia et salvantis gratiam plus miramur, qui ovem perditam reportans in humeris, gaudium facit Angelis super uno peccatore poenitentiam agente, quam super nonaginta novem justis (Luc. XV, 7).

English translation

2. If I had merited to receive any of such tongues, I too would certainly say: "The Lord has given me a tongue as my reward, and with it I will praise him" (Eccli. LI, 30); just as it is written of them, "The Apostles spoke in various tongues the great works of God." I would also say this: "The Lord has given me a learned tongue, that I may know how to sustain with a word him who has fallen" (Isai. L, 4). The Apostles, and those like them, with the tongues given them, preach the great works of God, scourge tyrants, flog demons, rain down upon the earth, open the heavens; for their tongues have become the keys of heaven, since it was indeed from heaven itself that tongues were sent to them. But as for me — would that at least the tongue of a dog were given to me, with which I might lick, first my own wounds, and then those of others, if perhaps there are any who would deign to admit me to this very task on their behalf. Blessed indeed are those whose heart the love and affection of divine praise fills with joy, and whose mouth with jubilation. But I would also surely call blessed those who, in licking away the venom and pus from the wounds of souls, draw into themselves the spirit and grace by which their own soul is fattened. For they hunger and thirst after justice, and suffer hunger like dogs; and therefore they disdain nothing that they can take into their own body; they abominate no sinner whom they might be able to convert to justice. "What God has cleansed, do not call unclean" (Act. XI, 9), was said to the Prince of the Apostles, and in him to others; and for this reason he slaughters and eats every kind of creeping thing and bird, and says: "The things my soul formerly refused to touch are now, through the anguish of impatient desire, my food" (Job. VI, 7). Indeed, in a wondrous way, the more bitter any sinner was before his conversion, the sweeter afterward is his conversion; and the more he was despaired of, the more welcome to us is his salvation — because we marvel all the more at the grace of him who saves, who, carrying the lost sheep back on his shoulders, causes more joy to the Angels over one sinner doing penance, than over ninety-nine just men (Luc. XV, 7).

3. Dicant igitur alii quibus datum est: Quam dulcia faucibus meis eloquia tua Domine (Psal. CXVIII, 103)! anima autem mea esuriens et famem patiens ut canis, etiam amarum sumet pro dulci, et abominabile pro desiderabili. Delectentur alii lambere mella Scripturarum; ego vero delectabor ulcera lingere peccatorum, mei scilicet mihique similium. Foedum quidem ulcus peccati, visuque horribile; sed tamen ipsum lingere, cujus sit saporis et gratiae, non sapit, non capit, nisi qui salutem periclitantium esurit, et famem patitur ut canis: de qualibus dictum est, Lingua canum tuorum ex inimicis (Psal. CXVIII, 24). Sed vae miseris qui tanto studio pereunt, tantoque desiderio mortem sibi consciscunt, ut vulnera sua tegant, curamque canum refugiant, et asperitatem linguae medicinalis arbitrentur morsum odii lethalis! Oderunt corripientem in porta, et perfecte loquentem abominati sunt (Amos. V, 10). Perfecte loquentem dico, non tam de perfectione disserentem, quam perfecta dilectione corripientem. Sed dicam, Perfecta dilectione, an perfecto odio? Imo utrumque, et perfecta dilectione, et perfecto odio: quia perfectum odium nihil aliud est, quam perfecta dilectio; et una est utriusque perfectio, quam Apostolus determinat, dicens, Odientes malum, adhaerentes autem bono (Rom. XII, 9).

English translation

3. Let others, then, to whom it has been given, say: "How sweet are your words to my palate, O Lord!" (Psal. CXVIII, 103). But my soul, hungering and suffering famine like a dog, will take even the bitter for sweet, and the abominable for the desirable. Let others delight to lick the honey of the Scriptures; I, however, will delight to lick the sores of sinners—namely, my own, and those of men like me. Foul indeed is the sore of sin, and horrible to see; yet what savor and grace there is in licking it, no one tastes, no one grasps, except him who hungers for the salvation of those in peril, and suffers famine like a dog: of such it is said, "The tongue of your dogs shall be red from the enemies" (Psal. CXVIII, 24). But woe to the wretched who perish with such zeal, and bring death upon themselves with such desire, that they cover their wounds and flee the care of dogs, judging the roughness of the healing tongue to be the bite of deadly hatred! "They hated him that reproved in the gate, and abhorred him that spoke perfectly" (Amos. V, 10). I say 'spoke perfectly'—not so much one discoursing about perfection, as one reproving with perfect love. But shall I say 'with perfect love,' or 'with perfect hatred'? Rather both: with perfect love and with perfect hatred; for perfect hatred is nothing other than perfect love, and the perfection of both is one, which the Apostle defines, saying, "Hating that which is evil, cleaving to that which is good" (Rom. XII, 9).

4. Sed revertar ad id unde digressus eram. Optabam mihi linguam qua laudarem Deum, vel certe linguam qua confitentium ulcera curarem: in altero quidem mihi quaerens divinae devotionis fructum; in altero, fraternae salutis lucrum: vobis quoque in altero dulcis, in altero fieri desiderans utilis. Nam et de saecularium arte et officio poetarum, ita sumptum est apud quemdam eorum: Aut prodesse volunt, aut delectare poetae. Omne tulit punctum qui miscuit utile dulci. (HORAT., Art. poet.) Optabam, inquam, mihi gratiam oris, qua Deo et vobis officiosus deservirem; ut aliquatenus compensarem verbo, quod minus prosum exemplo. Quanquam satis suspecta mihi sit haec consolatio, si dicam et non faciam; si suppetant suffragia linguae, cum desint merita vitae; imo magis fortasse timendum sit, ne peccanti dicat Deus, Quare tu enarras justitias meas, et assumis testamentum meum per os tuum? (Psal. XLIX, 16). Sed quid agam? Si locutus fuero, non quiescet dolor meus; et si tacuero, non recedet a me (Job. XVI, 7). Pavor est utrobique molestus, et coarctor e duobus; quia locutionem et officii locus exigit, et vita contradicit. Sed recordor verbi quod apud Sapientem inveni: Anima laborantis laborat sibi, quia compulit eum os suum (Prov. XVI, 26). Loquar igitur, non prout locus expetit, sed prout sensus suppetit, imo prout Dominus dederit, in cujus manu sumus et nos, et sermones nostri. Loquar, inquam, et me ipsum lingua obligabo propria, ut vel prae confusione aliquando laborare compellar: et si corpus a labore manuum excusaverit, certe anima laborantis laboret sibi, ut dicat cum David, Laboravi in gemitu meo (Psal. VI, 7). O si mihi dentur illi gemitus inenarrabiles, quibus Spiritus postulat pro sanctis, ut laborem in eis! Procul dubio labor hujusmodi gemituum satis digne pensaret mihi quotidianum laborem manuum.

English translation

4. But I will return to the point from which I digressed. I was wishing for myself a tongue with which to praise God, or at least a tongue with which to heal the sores of those who confess: seeking in the one case for myself the fruit of divine devotion, in the other the gain of my brothers' salvation; desiring to become, for you also, sweet in the one case, useful in the other. For concerning the art and office of the secular poets, this is taken from one of them: 'Poets wish either to profit or to please. He has carried every point who has mixed the useful with the sweet.' (Horace, Art of Poetry). I was wishing, I say, for the grace of speech, by which I might dutifully serve both God and you, so that I might in some measure make up by word for my being of less use by example. Though this consolation is quite suspect to me, if I should speak and not do; if the tongue's resources should be at hand while the merits of life are lacking—indeed it is rather to be feared that God might say to the sinner, "Why do you declare my justices, and take my covenant into your mouth?" (Psal. XLIX, 16). But what shall I do? "If I speak, my sorrow will not rest; and if I am silent, it will not depart from me" (Job. XVI, 7). The dread is troublesome on both sides, and I am constrained between the two; for the place of duty demands speech, and my life contradicts it. But I recall a saying I found in the Wise Man: "The soul of him who labors, labors for himself, because his own mouth has compelled him" (Prov. XVI, 26). I will speak, then, not as the occasion demands, but as understanding supplies—rather, as the Lord shall grant, in whose hand are both we ourselves and our words. I will speak, I say, and I will bind myself by my own tongue, so that I may at least be compelled by shame to labor at some point: and if the body be excused from the labor of the hands, let the soul of him who labors certainly labor for itself, that it may say with David, "I have labored in my groaning" (Psal. VI, 7). O, if only those unutterable groanings might be given to me, with which the Spirit intercedes for the saints, that I might labor in them! Without doubt, labor of such groanings would quite worthily make up for my daily labor of hands.

5. Sed et vos ipsi, fratres, si aemulari meliora charismata didicistis, optate gemitus hujusmodi a Spiritu vobis infundi. Nescio si sit in donis Spiritus aliud convenientius aut commodius his qui sunt infirmitate et miseria circumdati: nescio si Spiritui sancto qui in columba apparuit, alia vox familiarior gemitu, aut gratior sit. Hoc autem scio, quia in nullo opere tanta tamque parata nobis suppetat materia, sicut in gemitu et planctu nostri: nisi miseriam suam nostra dissimulet superbia, aut sensus obduretur stupore vel amentia. Hoc autem primum circa infirmos, quorum curam suscipit, operatur medicina sancti Spiritus, qui est illuminatio nostra et salus nostra, ut videlicet se ipsum phreneticus sentiat et sciat, et conversus ad cor dicat Domino cum propheta: Postquam convertisti me, egi poenitentiam; et postquam ostendisti mihi, percussi femur meum (Jerem. XXXI, 19). Nisi enim quis apponat scientiam, non apponet dolorem: nisi doluerit, non meretur consolationem. Beati enim qui lugent, quoniam ipsi consolabuntur (Matth. V, 5). Puto quia nec in Apostolis hodie locum invenisset Paracleti consolatio, nisi quia lugebant se desolatos, quia non poterant non lugere filii sponsi, cum sponsus ablatus esset ab eis. Inde erat quod eis dicebat: Nisi ego abiero, Paracletus non veniet ad vos (Joan. XVI, 7). Nisi desolati fueritis praesentia mea corporali, non vos consolabitur visitatio spiritualis. Date, inquit, siceram moerentibus, et vinum his qui amaro animo sunt, non scilicet illis qui ebrii sunt gaudio et luxu saeculi. Quae enim participatio justitiae cum iniquitate? nunquid bibere possunt calicem Domini, et calicem daemoniorum? Bibant, inquit, potius pauperes Apostoli, et obliviscantur egestatis suae (Prov. XXXI, 6, 7), ut dicant: Sicut egentes multos autem, locupletantes (II Cor. VI. 10). Bibant moerentes de absentia sponsi, et dolorum non recordentur amplius, sed dicant: Etsi cognovimus Christum secundum carnem, sed nunc jam non novimus (Id. V, 16). Nam et te ipsum, si pio affectu Psalmistae dicere potueris, Ego sum pauper et dolens (Psal. LXVIII, 30), sic ditabit, sic, laetificabit sobria ebrietas hujus praeclari calicis, ut, etsi pauper sis, paupertas animum non urat; et si dolebas quia peccasti, jam peccati reatus conscientiam non mordeat.

English translation

5. But you yourselves also, brothers, if you have learned to be zealous for the better gifts, desire that such groanings be poured into you by the Spirit. I do not know whether there is among the gifts of the Spirit anything more fitting or more suitable for those who are surrounded by weakness and misery; I do not know whether to the Holy Spirit, who appeared in the form of a dove, any voice is more familiar or more pleasing than a groan. But this I know: that in no work is there so great and so ready a store of material for us as in groaning and lamenting over ourselves—unless our pride disguises our own misery, or our perception is hardened by stupor or madness. Now this is the first thing that the medicine of the Holy Spirit, who is our illumination and our salvation, works upon the sick whose care it undertakes: that the one who is deranged should feel and know himself to be so, and, turning to his heart, say to the Lord with the prophet: 'After you converted me, I did penance; and after you showed me, I struck my thigh' (Jerem. XXXI, 19). For unless one adds knowledge, he will not add sorrow; unless he has grieved, he does not merit consolation. For blessed are they that mourn, for they shall be comforted (Matth. V, 5). I think that the consolation of the Paraclete would not have found a place among the Apostles today, had they not been mourning themselves as desolate—for the sons of the bridegroom could not but mourn when the bridegroom had been taken from them. Hence it was that he said to them: 'Unless I go away, the Paraclete will not come to you' (Joan. XVI, 7). Unless you have been left desolate of my bodily presence, a spiritual visitation will not console you. 'Give,' he says, 'strong drink to those that mourn, and wine to those who are of bitter mind'—not, that is, to those who are drunk with the joy and excess of this world. For what fellowship has justice with iniquity? Can they drink the cup of the Lord and the cup of devils? Let the poor Apostles rather drink,' he says, 'and forget their poverty' (Prov. XXXI, 6, 7), so that they may say: 'As needy, yet enriching many' (II Cor. VI. 10). Let those who mourn over the absence of the bridegroom drink, and let them remember their sorrows no more, but say: 'And if we have known Christ according to the flesh, yet now we know him so no longer' (Id. V, 16). For you yourself too, if with the pious feeling of the Psalmist you can say, 'I am poor and sorrowful' (Psal. LXVIII, 30), the sober inebriation of this glorious cup will so enrich you, will so gladden you, that although you are poor, poverty will not burn your soul; and if you were sorrowful because you had sinned, the guilt of sin will no longer gnaw at your conscience.

6. Vide autem, si non et ipse Spiritus sanctus in judicium venit in hunc mundum, ut qui dolent non doleant, et qui rident, aeterno et inconsolabili se luctui tradant. Melius est igitur ire ad domum luctus quam ad domum convivii (Eccli. VII, 3). Et revera sapiens, etsi aliquando consolationem meretur, ut dolorum non recordetur amplius, eorum scilicet de quibus consolationem accipit, semper tamen, ut locum paret consolationibus novis, novas exquirit de se ipso causas doloris; non statim blandiens sibi, quasi jam per omnia justus sit; sed eo subtilior, quo jam illuminari coepit; eo districtior, quo justificari coepit, fit accusator et judex sui. Ei ergo qui ejusmodi est, crebro, nisi fallor, Spiritus consolator advenit, quoniam et adventum jam ipse suum antevenit; adveniens scilicet ut consolationem tribuat, sed anteveniens, ut lugere doceat. Luctus siquidem pius atque religiosus, in doctrina Spiritus et ordine primus, et utilitate invenitur praecipuus; quippe cum sit summa sapientia sanctorum, custodia justorum, sobrietas modestorum, prima incipientium virtus, proficientium stimulus, perfectorum cumulus, pereuntium salus, periclitantium portus, promissionem denique habens consolationum quae nunc sunt, et gaudiorum quae futura sunt; ad quae nos perducat qui vivit et regnat per omnia saecula saeculorum. Amen.

English translation

6. But consider whether the Holy Spirit himself does not come into this world for judgment, so that those who grieve may grieve no more, and those who laugh may give themselves over to eternal and inconsolable mourning. It is better, therefore, to go to the house of mourning than to the house of feasting (Eccli. VII, 3). And indeed the wise man, even though he sometimes merits consolation so that he no longer remembers those sorrows for which he receives comfort, nevertheless always seeks out new causes of sorrow from himself, so as to make room for new consolations; not at once flattering himself as though he were already righteous in every respect, but becoming his own accuser and judge—the more discerning, the more he has begun to be enlightened; the more strict, the more he has begun to be justified. To one, then, who is of this kind, the comforting Spirit, unless I am mistaken, comes often, since he himself anticipates his own coming—coming, that is, to grant consolation, but anticipating it in order to teach mourning. For pious and devout mourning is found, in the teaching of the Spirit, to be first in order and chief in usefulness; since it is the highest wisdom of the saints, the guardianship of the just, the sobriety of the modest, the first virtue of beginners, the spur of those advancing, the crown of the perfect, the salvation of those perishing, the harbor of those in peril—having, finally, the promise both of the consolations that now are, and of the joys that are to come; to which may he bring us who lives and reigns through all ages of ages. Amen.
