Introduction & Scripture references

Editorial introduction

Pressed by the brothers, Guerric finishes the verse he began the day before: "Until the day breathe, and the shadows be inclined." He distinguishes shadows that chill and darken the soul from those that cool and enlighten it, and holds that even the clearest vision of this life is a bright shadow in a mirror. He invites the laboring brothers to rest in the shade of Christ, and warns against the heavy shade of worldly cares in which Leviathan sleeps. The sermon appears under the heading for the Apostles Peter and Paul in the 1879 edition of Patrologia Latina. The collection is associated approximately with Guerric's abbacy in 1138–1157; the individual sermon is undated.

SERMON III.

¶cols. 183-184
Latin original

De eo quod scriptum est: « Donec aspiret dies, et inclinentur umbrae. » (Cant. II, 17).

English translation

On what is written: "Until the day breathe, and the shadows be inclined." (Cant. II, 17).

¶cols. 183-184
Latin original

1. Erratis, fratres, in me; sed amore magis, ut arbitror, aut humilitate, quam temeritate. Putatis me Scripturarum scientiam habere, qui vix unquam attigi vel primum limen scientiae. Displicet enim vobis, ut mihi videtur, quod capitulum Scripturae, de quo heri sum locutus, non sum ad finem usque persecutus: quasi illa mihi sit facultas, ut valeam Scripturas explanare, aut etiam explanationes aliorum digne et congrue ad memoriam revocare. Sed neque hoc mihi solet propositum esse, ut Scripturam, unde sermonem ordior, exponam; sed ut de ipsa, et secundum ipsam sermonem debitum suo diei persolvam. Huc accedit quod magister noster (10)1 ille interpres Spiritus sancti, de toto illo carmine nuptiali loqui instituit, spemque nobis dedit ex eis quae jam edidit, quia si pervenerit ad locum de quo quaeritis, donec aspiret dies, et inclinentur umbrae, umbras ipsas ponat in lucem intelligentiae: quod dictum est vel erit in tenebris, nobis dicet in lumine. Vos tamen dicitis, et recte dicitis, quia vetera novis supervenientibus projicietis; quantoque magis incondita seu rancida fuerint vetera nostra, tanto nova illius sapidiora erunt et gratiora. Nam et ipse Jesus servat vivum bonum2 usque ad extremum, sciens utique quomodo nostro sit fastidio medendum.

English translation

1. You are mistaken about me, brothers; but out of love, I think, or out of humility, rather than out of rashness. You suppose me to have knowledge of the Scriptures—I who have scarcely ever touched even the first threshold of knowledge. For it displeases you, as it seems to me, that I did not pursue to its end the chapter of Scripture of which I spoke yesterday: as though I had the capacity to explain the Scriptures, or even to recall to memory, worthily and fittingly, the explanations of others. But it has never been my custom to set out to expound the Scripture from which I begin my sermon; rather, my aim is to pay to each day the sermon owed to it, drawn from and in accordance with that Scripture. To this is added the fact that our master (10)1, that interpreter of the Holy Spirit, has undertaken to speak on that whole nuptial song, and has given us hope, from what he has already set forth, that if he should come to the passage you are asking about—"until the day breathe, and the shadows be inclined"—he will set those very shadows into the light of understanding: what has been said, or will be said, in darkness, he will tell us in the light. Yet you say, and you say rightly, that you will cast aside the old things when the new ones come; and the more unrefined or stale our old things have been, the more savory and pleasing the new ones from him will be. For Jesus himself keeps the living good2 even to the last, knowing well how our distaste is to be remedied.

Latin original

2. Quia igitur cogitis me (nam impatientes dilationis vos video), nec spes incerta futuri praesenti satisfacit desiderio; ecce vobis morem gero, finemque versiculi, de quo heri loquebamur, suo, prout potero, reddam principio. Illud enim quaerere videmini, quid sit aspirante die spirituali umbras inclinari; cum magis consequens videretur, ut non crescere, sed minui tunc et deficere, dicerentur. Nam si sequamur proprietatem verbi, hoc est umbris crescere, quod inclinari: cum umbrarum ea natura sit, ut quanto inclinantur, tanto procumbant longiores; quantoque surgunt, tanto stent minores. Unde et poeta vesperum significans ait: Majoresque cadunt altis de montibus umbrae. (VIRG. Buc. eclog. 1.) Sed sciendum, quia cum haec proprietas umbrarum sit corporalium, longe alia est spiritualium, de quibus non dubitamus hic loqui Spiritum sanctum. Nam sicut corporalibus umbris inclinari est crescere, sic spiritualibus hoc est inclinari, quod deponi, minui, vel deficere. Umbrae autem spirituales sunt umbrosi spiritus et tetri, seu daemonum, seu hominum: umbrae sunt tenebrae errorum: umbrae sunt obscurae significationes veterum sacramentorum. Et umbrae quidem significationum jam inclinatae sunt ad occasum et finem: umbrae autem errorum de die in diem inclinantur ad diminutionem: umbrae vero tenebrosorum spirituum tandem inclinabuntur in infernum et mortem. Primum quidem factum est, cum dies aeternus aspiravit, apparens in carne: secundum quotidie fit, cum magis ac magis aspirat illustrans veritate: tertium novissime fiet, cum aspirabit rutilans in majestate.

English translation

2. Since, then, you compel me (for your impatience at delay is plain to me), and an uncertain hope of the future does not satisfy a present desire; behold, I will indulge you, and will render, as best I can, the end of the little verse we were discussing yesterday, together with its own beginning. For you seem to be asking what it means for the shadows to be inclined when the spiritual day breathes forth; whereas it would seem more fitting to say that then they are not increasing but diminishing and failing. For if we follow the proper sense of the word, for shadows "to be inclined" is the same as "to grow": since it is the nature of shadows that the more they are inclined, the longer they stretch out; and the more they rise, the shorter they stand. Whence the poet, signifying evening, says: "And longer shadows fall from the high mountains." But it must be understood that, while this is the property of bodily shadows, the case is far different with spiritual shadows, of which we do not doubt the Holy Spirit is speaking here. For just as for bodily shadows to be inclined is to grow, so for spiritual shadows to be inclined is to be laid down, diminished, or to fail. Now the spiritual shadows are the shadowy and gloomy spirits, whether of demons or of men: shadows are the darknesses of errors: shadows are the obscure significations of the old sacraments. And indeed the shadows of significations have already been inclined toward their setting and their end: but the shadows of errors are inclined, day by day, toward diminishment: while the shadows of the dark spirits will at last be inclined into hell and death. The first indeed came to pass when the eternal Day breathed forth, appearing in the flesh: the second happens daily, as he breathes forth more and more, enlightening with truth: the third will happen at the very last, when he will breathe forth glowing in majesty.

¶cols. 183-184; cols. 185-186
Latin original

3. Videre autem mihi videor et alias umbras intra nos, quae velut in quodam speculo de rebus aliis nascuntur in sensu nostro, et obumbrant nobis, vel obumbrant nos. Obumbrant nos quae refrigidant et obscurant nos: obumbrant nobis quae refrigerant et illuminant nos. Istae quidem noxiae et graves, illae delectabiles et salubres, istae namque de inferioribus sunt et mundanis; illae, de superioribus et divinis. Anima nempe humana in quodam medio condita et posita est, ut sub ipsa esset mundus, supra ipsam Deus. Supra ipsam, a quo et ad quem et propter quem facta est: sub ipsa, quod propter ipsam factum est. Nam sicut propter animam corpus, sic propter corpus domus ejus, id est, mundus. Cum igitur incurvatur ad corporalia vel mundana, ascendunt in eam umbrae de interioribus: cum erigitur ad divina, inclinantur super eam umbrae de superioribus. Illius enim rei quam cogitat, umbram sibi format. Quanquam de divinis satis dubia umbra est, quantumcunque cogitationis evacuatur acies: aut certe non umbra ipsius rei, sed aliud pro illa est, nisi cum aspirat dies ille. Nam tunc quoque non videtur, ut arbitror, nisi umbra, quae etsi luminosa, etsi gloriosa est, tamen umbra est; sicut cum in facie speculi tersa et lucida, splendidissimae rei splendens imago resultat. Caeterum videre faciem veritatis ipsius, vel veritatem faciei ejus, sicut nunquam erit hujus corporis: ita nec modo est hujus temporis, nec aliquando erit, donec corpus dissolvet mortalitas, aut absolvet immortalitas, et tempus absorbebit aeternitas, et spiritum qui nunc gravatur corpore et mutatur tempore, sublevabit ad se, et firmabit in se immortalis et immutabilis divinitas.

English translation

3. But it seems to me that I can see other shadows within us as well, which, as it were in a kind of mirror, arise in our senses from other things, and either cast shade for us, or overshadow us. Those overshadow us which chill and darken us: those cast shade for us which cool and illuminate us. The former indeed are harmful and heavy, the latter delightful and wholesome; for the former come from lower, worldly things, the latter from higher, divine things. For the human soul has been established and placed in a kind of middle position, so that the world should be beneath it, and God above it. Above it is he from whom, and to whom, and for whose sake it was made; beneath it is what was made for its sake. For just as the body exists for the sake of the soul, so the soul's house—that is, the world—exists for the sake of the body. When, therefore, the soul bends down toward bodily or worldly things, shadows from the lower things rise up into it; but when it is lifted up toward divine things, shadows from the higher things are inclined over it. For of whatever thing it thinks upon, it forms for itself a shadow. Although, concerning divine things, the shadow is quite uncertain, however much the keenness of thought is emptied out: or rather, it is not the shadow of the thing itself, but something else in its place, except when that day breathes forth. For then too, I think, nothing is seen except a shadow—which, even though luminous, even though glorious, is nevertheless a shadow; just as when, on the face of a mirror polished and bright, the shining image of a most splendid thing is reflected back. But to see the face of truth itself, or the truth of its face, will never belong to this body—just as it is not now the lot of this present time, nor ever will be, until mortality dissolves the body, or immortality releases it, and eternity swallows up time, and the immortal and unchangeable divinity lifts up to itself, and establishes firmly in itself, the spirit which is now weighed down by the body and changed by time.

Latin original

4. Initium hujus gratiae, et perfectionem illius gloriae Paulus satis proprie distinguebat, cum dicebat: Videmus nunc per speculum in aenigmate; tunc autem facie ad faciem (I Cor. XIII, 12). Ut autem et hoc ipsum sit, scilicet ut videamus per speculum et in aenigmate, opus est non solum ut facies speculi nostri ab omni phantasmate et umbra corporalium tersa sit: sed etiam ut ille sublimis, habitans lucem inaccessibilem, dignetur ad nos inclinari, et vel per umbram suae imaginis manifestari. Quantumcunque enim contendamus a visibilibus ad invisibilia, nihil nostra apprehendet humilitas, nisi condescendat illa majestas. Moyses ascendit in montem, et Dominus inclinavit coelos et descendit (Exod. XIX, 20). Et alius: Exsurge inquit in occursum meum, et vide (Psal. LVIII, 6). Ideo nec umbra videtur, nisi inclinetur; nec inclinari dicitur, nisi aspirante die: quia neque per speculum et in aenigmate possumus Deum videre, nisi inclinante se majestate, et aspirante favore gratiae. Sane hoc ipsum quod umbra vocatur comparatione manifestae veritatis, ineffabilis plerumque gloriae est et splendoris, in illis scilicet quorum est perlucidum speculum mentis. Cujus felicis experientiae Paulus sibi conscius, de se sibique similibus loquebatur: Nos vero omnes gloriam Domini speculantes, in eamdem imaginem transformamur a claritate in claritatem, tanquam a Domini Spiritu (II Cor. III, 18). Sic enim in promissione dictum est: Implebit splendoribus animam tuam (Isai. XVIII, 11.3). Ut autem claritates istas umbras esse noveris, audi David: Dies mei, inquit, sicut umbra declinaverunt. Transibat quidem et ipse David de claritate in claritatem, tanquam de die in diem, Sed heu! tam breves et obscuros dies, tam longas et laboriosas noctes habet hiems ista, ut et propheta laborans in gemitu suo, lavansque per singulas noctes lectum lacrymis, merito plangat: Dies mei sicut umbra declinaverunt! (Psal. CI, 12.) Prorsus melior est dies una in atriis tuis super millia (Psal. LXXXIII, 11) dierum meorum; quia dies mei sicut umbra quamvis lucida: dies illa una, lux vera et mera, sicut sine umbra, sic sine vespera; cum dies meos faciat plurimos et brevissimos assidua longarum noctium interpolatio.

English translation

4. Paul distinguished quite properly the beginning of this grace and the perfection of that glory, when he said: "We see now through a glass in a dark manner; but then face to face" (I Cor. XIII, 12). But in order for this very thing to come about—namely, that we see through a glass and in a dark manner—it is necessary not only that the face of our mirror be wiped clean of every phantasm and shadow of corporeal things, but also that he who is exalted on high, dwelling in inaccessible light, deign to incline himself toward us, and be made manifest to us at least through the shadow of his image. For however much we strive to pass from the visible to the invisible, our lowliness will apprehend nothing unless that majesty condescends. Moses went up into the mountain, and the Lord bowed down the heavens and came down (Exod. XIX, 20). And another says: "Arise to meet me, and behold" (Psal. LVIII, 6). Therefore the shadow is not seen unless it is inclined; nor is it said to incline except when the day breathes forth: because we cannot see God through a glass and in a dark manner unless majesty inclines itself and the favor of grace breathes upon us. Indeed, this very thing which is called a shadow, in comparison with manifest truth, is for the most part of ineffable glory and splendor—namely, in those whose mirror of the mind is perfectly clear. Conscious of this happy experience, Paul spoke of himself and those like him: "But we all, beholding the glory of the Lord, are transformed into the same image from glory to glory, as by the Spirit of the Lord" (II Cor. III, 18). For thus it was said in the promise: "He will fill your soul with splendors" (Isai. XVIII, 11.3). But that you may know that these very splendors are shadows, listen to David: "My days," he says, "have declined like a shadow." David too was indeed passing from glory to glory, as it were from day to day. But alas! this winter has days so short and dark, and nights so long and laborious, that the prophet himself, laboring in his groaning and washing his bed every night with tears, justly laments: "My days have declined like a shadow!" (Psal. CI, 12.). Truly "better is one day in your courts" (Psal. LXXXIII, 11) than a thousand of my days; because my days are like a shadow, however bright; but that one day is true and pure light—as it is without shadow, so it is without evening—whereas my days are made very many and very brief by the constant interruption of long nights.

Latin original

5. Caeterum, si quaeratur cur umbrae pluraliter dictae sint, cum res ipsa, cujus umbrae sunt, una sit: ideo nimirum, quia nunc minus, nunc amplius nobis Altissimi virtus obumbrat; nunc obscurius, nunc manifestius veritas sese nobis innotescens adumbrat: et sicut non aequaliter semper spirat, ita nec similiter semper nobis imagines format. Hoc et Apostolus innuit, qui transire se confitetur a claritate in claritatem (II Cor. III, 18), a minore scilicet in majorem: et propheta, qui non splendore sed splendoribus implendam promittit fidelem animam (Isai. LVIII, 11). Sic Jacobus Deum memorat patrem luminum (Jacobi I, 17), cum lumen, cujus pater est, et unum sit et unicum. Quanquam sic possit intelligi pater luminum, quomodo pater misericordiarum.

English translation

5. Furthermore, if it be asked why the shadows are spoken of in the plural, when the thing itself, of which they are shadows, is one: it is doubtless because now the power of the Most High overshadows us less, now more; now the truth, making itself known to us, casts its shadow more obscurely, now more manifestly: and just as he does not always breathe upon us equally, so neither does he always form images for us in like manner. The Apostle hints at this too, confessing that he passes "from glory to glory" (II Cor. III, 18), that is, from a lesser to a greater glory; and the prophet, who promises that the faithful soul will be filled not with a splendor but with splendors (Isai. LVIII, 11). Thus James calls God "the Father of lights" (Jacobi I, 17), though the light of which he is Father is one and unique. Although it can be understood that he is called "Father of lights" in the same way he is called "Father of mercies."

6. Jam vero vos fratres, qui laboratis portantes pondus diei et aestus, vel secundum Isaiam, in spiritu vestro duro meditamini per diem aestus (Isa. XXVII, 8), ad umbras istas placet invitare quas ille dies serenus aspirans rorem misericordiae cupit aestuantibus inclinare. Audite ipsum diem, ad umbrarum refrigeria clementer vos invitantem: Venite ad me, omnes qui laboratis et onerati estis, et ego reficiam vos (Matth. XI, 28). Servus enim sub sole laborans, ut ait Job, desiderat umbram (Job VII, 2), in qua scilicet a labore recreetur ad laborem, vel per horam dimidiam, qua silentium fit in coelo: quam cum obtinuerit indulgentia Prioris sui, gloriatur et dicit, Sub umbra illius, quem desiderabam, sedi. In qua nimirum umbra, quia non sola sessio est ad quietem, sed etiam refectio ad satietatem, congrue subdit: Et fructus ejus dulcis gutturi meo (Cantic. II, 3). Sic enim dictum est per prophetam: Convertentur sedentes in umbra ejus, vivent tritico et germinabunt sicut vitis (Osee XIV, 8). Servus seu mercenarius, quem saepius haec umbra recreaverit, longanimiter praestolabitur finem operis sui. Sic enim habes: Servus desiderat umbram, et mercenarius praestolatur finem operis sui (Job VII, 2). In umbra quidem non finis, sed pausatio. Finis enim erit operis remuneratio, cum evacuatis omnibus umbris, videbimus eum sicuti est: cui modo dicimus, In umbra tua vivemus inter gentes (Thren. IV, 20); inter angelos nempe, non in umbra, sed in luce manifesta.

English translation

6. Now, brothers, you who labor, bearing the burden of the day and the heat, or who, according to Isaiah, meditate in your hard spirit through the day of heat (Isa. XXVII, 8), it is fitting to invite you to these shadows which that serene day, breathing forth the dew of mercy, desires to incline toward those who burn with heat. Hear the Day himself, mercifully inviting you to the refreshments of the shadows: "Come to me, all you who labor and are burdened, and I will refresh you" (Matth. XI, 28). For the servant laboring under the sun, as Job says, desires the shadow (Job VII, 2), namely, that in it he may be refreshed from labor for labor, or at least for the half hour in which silence is made in heaven; and when he has obtained this by the indulgence of his Prior, he glories and says, "Under the shadow of him whom I desired, I sat." And because in this shadow there is not only sitting for rest, but also feasting to satisfaction, he fittingly adds: "And his fruit was sweet to my throat" (Cantic. II, 3). For so it is said through the prophet: "They that dwell under his shadow shall be turned, they shall live by the wheat and shall blossom as the vine" (Osee XIV, 8). The servant or hireling, whom this shadow has often refreshed, will patiently await the end of his work. For thus you have it: "The servant desires the shadow, and the hireling awaits the end of his work" (Job VII, 2). In the shadow, indeed, there is not the end, but a pause. For the end will be the reward of the work, when, all shadows having been emptied away, we shall see him as he is—to whom we now say, "Under your shadow we shall live among the nations" (Thren. IV, 20)—that is, among the angels, not in shadow, but in manifest light.

7. Hoc autem notandum, quod cum umbrae illae de superioribus inclinantur supra nos, umbrae istae de inferioribus inclinantur infra nos. Cum enim divinis propinquamus, mundana superamus: et quatenus vel ad umbram lucis accedimus, eatenus mortis umbram evadimus. Lux enim et vita in coelo, mors in inferno: umbra autem mortis in hoc terreno et tenebroso loco. Cum igitur spiritus altius nos elevat inter terram et coelum, non tangimur umbra terrenorum: et licet nondum plena luce fruamur coelorum, tamen umbris illuminamur montium aeternorum, vel certe montis illius umbrosi et condensi, de quo venit Sanctus sanctorum. Mox tamen pondere nostro in regionem umbrae mortis revolvimur, ubi et diutissime sedere cogimur, donec visitatione orientis ex alto consolemur. Cogimur, inquam, et utinam cogamur, et non delectemur! utinam quod patimur, necessitatis tantum sit, non etiam voluntatis aut voluptatis! Sed heu! tam facile, tam libenter projicimus nos, et obdormiscimus sub gravi umbra juniperi spinosae et infructuosae arboris, tam negligentes et securi torpescimus et immorimur curis et desideriis hujus mundi! Propterea Leviathan, qui sub umbra dormit, cubile sibi, nisi praecaveamus, ponit in nobis; quia videlicet amicas sibi umbras hujus saeculi invenit in sensibus et affectibus nostris. Istae siquidem umbrae, teste Job, protegunt umbra ejus (Job. XL, 20, 17); quia nimirum curae et desideria saecularia, quae sunt in nobis, umbram nobis abscondunt imminentis damnationis, ut eam non praevideamus, donec in ipsam impingamus. Aspira, o dies dierum, obumbra supra caput nostrum in die belli, inclina super nos umbras salutis et refrigerii, et inclinentur subtus nos umbrae torporis et taedii, imo caecitatis et mortis. Et licet contingant nos umbrae de cogitatione terrenorum, non nos contegant tenebrae, id est, umbrae crassiores de amore eorum; sed semper respiremus in luminosum amorem tuum, o Pater luminum, qui vivis et regnas per omnia saecula saeculorum. Amen.

English translation

7. Now this must be noted: that while those shadows from the higher things incline over us, these shadows from the lower things incline beneath us. For when we draw near to things divine, we rise above worldly things; and to the extent that we approach even the shadow of the light, to that extent we escape the shadow of death. For light and life are in heaven, death is in hell; but the shadow of death is in this earthly and darksome place. When, therefore, the spirit lifts us higher, between earth and heaven, we are not touched by the shadow of earthly things; and although we do not yet enjoy the full light of the heavens, nevertheless we are illumined by the shadows of the eternal mountains — or at least of that shadowy and dense mountain from which the Holy One of holy ones came. Yet soon, by our own weight, we are rolled back down into the region of the shadow of death, where we are forced to sit for a very long time, until we are consoled by the visitation of the Dayspring from on high. We are forced, I say — and would that we were only forced, and did not take delight in it! Would that what we suffer were a matter of necessity alone, and not also of will or of pleasure! But alas, how easily, how willingly we fling ourselves down and fall asleep under the heavy shadow of the juniper, a thorny and fruitless tree; so negligent and so unconcerned do we grow sluggish and lose ourselves in the cares and desires of this world! Therefore Leviathan, who sleeps beneath the shadow, makes his lair in us, unless we take care — because, indeed, he finds shadows of this age that are friendly to him in our senses and affections. For these shadows, as Job testifies, protect his shadow (Job. XL, 20, 17); because, truly, the worldly cares and desires that are in us hide from us the shadow of the damnation that hangs over us, so that we do not foresee it until we strike against it. Breathe upon us, O Day of days; overshadow our heads in the day of battle; incline over us the shadows of salvation and refreshment, and let the shadows of torpor and weariness — nay, of blindness and death — be inclined beneath us. And though the shadows that come from the thought of earthly things may touch us, let not the darkness — that is, the thicker shadows that come from the love of them — cover us over; but let us always breathe toward your luminous love, O Father of lights, who live and reign through all ages of ages. Amen.
